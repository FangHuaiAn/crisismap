The Straits of Malacca and Singapore (SOMS) are among the world’s most intensively monitored maritime corridors, yet low-level maritime crime persists. Despite sustained investment in patrols, surveillance systems and regional cooperation, incidents of piracy and armed robbery continue to cluster around specific choke points, anchorages and traffic separation schemes. This report focuses on criminality at sea rather than strategic competition between states, recognising that each operates according to distinct logics and policy tools.
Deterrence in the SOMS is spatially uneven and highly localised. Maritime security infrastructure is deliberately concentrated in high-risk, high-value corridors, producing overlapping zones where enforcement presence and criminal opportunity coexist. Incident clustering near security posts does not indicate deterrence failure, but reflects choke-point geography, infrastructure placement and offender adaptation.
Spatial analysis of data from 2007–August 2025 from the Regional Cooperation Agreement on Combating Piracy and Armed Robbery Against Ships in Asia (ReCAAP), shows that maritime crime is strongly clustered rather than randomly distributed. Clustering is most concentrated within approximately 50 nautical miles of the surrounding security infrastructures, reflecting the straits’ narrow, overlapping maritime boundaries, which effectively form a single connected risk area. Beyond this, especially over 200 nm into international waters, clustering diminishes significantly.
Deterrent effects also diminish rapidly with distance from security infrastructure. While risk declines measurably within a few nautical miles of security posts, deterrent effects do not extend across the wider straits. In narrow waterways such as the Phillip Channel, even small positional shifts between channel cores, adjacent traffic lanes and nearby anchorages can alter the level of exposure to risk, underscoring the microspatial nature of deterrence in choke-point environments.
Maritime crime in the SOMS is shaped by opportunity rather than by enforcement pressure alone. Offenders adjust their routes, timings and tactics in response to predictable patrol patterns, sustaining persistent hotspots despite surveillance. Managing maritime crime in the SOMS therefore requires risk management rather than elimination – by reducing blind spots, improving operational flexibility, and aligning local enforcement with regional coordination.
