# Improving Diffusion of Clinical Care Innovations in Public Health Emergencies: 7 Things Leaders of Professional Societies Can Do

Before Public Health Emergencies
Build a standing coalition of ED professional  
societies to identify, monitor, and coordinate  
responses to emerging public health threats. 
Medical, nursing, and other professional societies are important 
conveners of experts and disseminators of information.1 A stand-
ing coalition that includes one or more representatives of each 
society could meet infrequently but regularly to share information 
and scale up engagement as needed. Such coordination could  
prevent duplication of activities, reduce conflicting messaging,  
develop cohesive communications strategies, and otherwise 
streamline activities to support ED clinicians in future PHEs.
Encourage use of clinician-only virtual spaces  
for sharing information related to care innovations. 
Such virtual spaces as message boards and social media  
platforms are important venues for sharing care innovations  
and implementation strategies.2 Restricting access to clinicians 
can reduce unfounded information or politicized content found  
on public platforms.3 There should be ground rules enforced  
by active moderation to allow for sharing of differing clinical  
perspectives while maintaining collegial dialogue and minimizing 
off-topic discussions.
Create and offer communications training in  
preparation for PHEs.
ED clinicians are well positioned to counsel patients about health 
interventions in a way that engenders trust,4 but they might not 
have the knowledge or experience needed to communicate  
effectively on these topics in broader forums, including digital  
media platforms. Professional societies could offer communica-
tions training so clinicians are better prepared to explain complex 
and rapidly changing medical information with concise, relatable, 
and properly contextualized messaging when PHEs occur.

During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) public health emergency (PHE), emergency department (ED) clinicians on the front lines 
faced supply and staffing shortages, surges in patient volumes, and an evidence cycle that was outpaced by the spread of the pandemic. 
Despite these challenges, many ED clinicians quickly developed and disseminated information on new care innovations, and many EDs 
rapidly adopted and implemented them. The diffusion of these care innovations was uneven, however, and some EDs were faster and 
more effective than others in identifying, trialing, and, when necessary, discontinuing such innovations. Effectively and rapidly diffusing 
knowledge and innovation across diverse practice settings is a key priority for addressing future PHEs. To support ED clinicians in future 
PHEs, this document offers practical strategies that leaders of professional societies can take before and during a PHE to identify and 
spread effective innovations and discontinue ineffective or potentially harmful ones. These strategies were developed by RAND in 
partnership with emergency care experts.

During Public Health Emergencies
Convene live forums to facilitate information- 
sharing specific to emerging health threats.
Clinicians need high-quality information from experts on best 
practices for clinical care and appreciate the opportunity to en-
gage directly with experts, peers, and colleagues across disciplines 
(e.g., chat, Q&A sessions).6 Interactive events (e.g., Project ECHO7) 
can facilitate learning about care innovations, increase provider 
competency and self-efficacy in implementing such  
innovations, and create a sense of community, preventing  
moral injury and burnout. Such events could be large or small  
in scope and audience, depending on resources and capacity.
Provide best-practice guidance on ED clinical  
care in a “living guidance” document. 
During a PHE, frontline ED clinicians need support and guidance 
on what is known about best practices when evidence and infor-
mation are limited. As conditions and knowledge change rapidly, 
it is critical to ensure that information can be updated rapidly and 
transparently.8 Professional societies should provide best-practice 
guidelines via online tools that streamline editing, indicate when 
guidance was last updated, and allow users to view prior versions. 
Guidelines should be aligned among professional societies when-
ever possible to reduce confusion and time spent by clinicians 
reconciling differences.
Provide ED clinicians with resources to  
constructively engage patients at the bedside  
to support evidence-informed decisions. 
Clinicians are patients’ most-trusted information sources but 
might not have the knowledge or skills to effectively engage 
patients in making evidence-informed decisions in a chaotic and 
shifting information environment.9 Providing frontline ED  
clinicians with resources (e.g., handouts, training modules, 
scripts, talking points) can help them enhance trust and support  
decisionmaking with patients during ED encounters in PHEs.

■  How These Strategies Were Created
Over the course of a four-year study, RAND researchers synthesized lessons learned from the dissemination and implementation of ED care 
innovations during the COVID-19 pandemic to develop strategies for more-effective innovation diffusion in future PHEs.10 To develop these 
strategies, the team analyzed qualitative and quantitative data collected through multiple research activities. Some data were drawn from 13 focus 
groups (comprising a total of 17 physicians, seven advanced practice providers, 18 registered nurses, and seven respiratory therapists) held between 
November 2022 and June 2023 at five hospital-based EDs purposively sampled from different U.S. regions. Additional data were collected through 14 
interviews held December 2024 through September 2025 with key disseminators of ED care innovations intentionally selected from peer-reviewed 
literature, preprint platforms, professional society communications, and social media during the course of the COVID-19 pandemic. The team 
extracted findings from the qualitative data on barriers, facilitators, and strategies for the dissemination and implementation of ED care innovations. 
The team also reviewed quantitative findings from archived discourse on ED care innovations from April 2022 through May 2024 and conducted a 
nationwide survey from February through September 2024 of 808 physicians and 822 nurses who were involved in clinical care during the pandemic. 
During a two-day, in-person meeting in October 2025, both inductive and deductive approaches (e.g., pile-sorting and role-playing) were used to 
develop strategies for different stakeholder groups to improve the diffusion of ED care innovations, particularly during PHEs. An expert panel of 
ED professionals from a variety of clinical backgrounds provided feedback from November 2025 through February 2026 to inform the final list of 
strategies. 
This document presents strategies specifically aimed at leaders of professional societies. Although there are many ways that such leaders can help 
improve management and response in future PHEs, the strategies presented here focus on improving the diffusion of innovations for delivering 
clinical care in EDs; other aspects of PHE response (e.g., communications with the public aside from ED encounters) were considered out of scope.
