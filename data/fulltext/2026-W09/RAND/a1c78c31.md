Summary
Biological tools (BTs) are at the forefront of advancements in biological research and medicine, enabling 
researchers to probe, manipulate, and understand living systems in novel ways. Recently, the convergence of 
artificial intelligence (AI) and biology has given rise to AI-enabled biological research, which is driving some 
of the most-transformative developments in the field. AI-enabled biological research relies on both large 
language models and BTs that are more tailored for computational methods and used for a variety of tasks, 
such as predicting protein structures, analyzing and designing genomes, and modeling complex molecular 
binding scenarios. However, AI tools in AI-enabled biological research might also pose significant dual-use 
risks; the same technologies that drive innovation can be misused for harmful purposes, such as suggesting 
modifications to existing pathogens to increase virulence.
It is, therefore, important to consider the complexity that underlies the ideation, design, and construction 
of a virus-based construct so that experts can identify which biological capabilities are the most amenable 
to synthetic modification and which are the most dangerous and urgent to control to prevent nonstate-actor 
misuse. In this report, we discuss biological functions that we anticipate could be modified using AI tools 
and that are most relevant in the optimization or design of a viral pathogen. These functions are (1) altered 
host range or tropism, (2) increased genome replication, (3) immune or medical countermeasure evasion, 
(4) increased environmental stability, and (5) increased transmission dynamics.
We also designed a biological modification risk-scoring system that comprises risk factors and associated 
indicators to illustrate the impact of modifying these functions using AI tools. Additionally, we designed an 
actor capability scoring system to assess the actor capability levels that we anticipate are required to modify 
the five functions and how much uplift AI tools would provide. Together, both scores make up a risk-scoring 
tool that can be used to evaluate the severity of potential misuse in AI-enabled biological design. This risk-
scoring tool could be applied to several hypothetical use cases, such as in anticipating misuse with published 
research or in developing redlines.
The audience for this report is researchers looking to understand the threat vector for AI-generated 
pathogen creation by nonstate actors. The scope is limited to assessing biosecurity risks in AI-enabled virus 
biological design and investigating an actor’s ability to modify a biological agent; we are not focused on the 
weaponization of the modified agent.
We hypothesize that, as AI tools (such as BTs) and equipment advance and become more readily acces-
sible over time, the technical skills required to modify these functions will decrease. We envision this 
scoring tool to be a foundation for a more robust decisionmaking framework for research involving AI-
enabled biological design. Such a framework could help identify potential risks from using AI in biological 
research and ensure that such research occurs safely and securely without stifling responsible biotechnol-
ogy research and innovation.

CHAPTER 1
Introduction
The convergence of artificial intelligence (AI) and biotechnology has opened new frontiers in biological 
research and medicine, enabling breakthroughs in protein engineering, genome design, and predictive mod-
eling. At the center of this transformation are AI tools that are used for AI-enabled biological research, 
including large language models (LLMs) and more-narrow biological tools (BTs) that are capable of either 
predictive or generative tasks in molecular biology. AI-enabled biological research offers exciting new ave-
nues in research but might also pose dual-use risks; the same systems that can optimize therapeutics or 
enhance vaccine platforms might also be misused to design or modify harmful biological agents.
A 2025 report from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine found that AI tools 
used for biological research are already capable of designing toxins and might be capable of predicting traits 
linked to virulence, raising concerns about the potential to misuse these tools (National Academies of Sci-
ences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2025). Although the report found that these tools are not able to design 
new viruses outright, the rapid pace of innovation suggests that more-powerful and more-accessible capa-
bilities are likely to emerge in the near future. For example, a preprint paper posted on September 17, 2025, 
used the BTs Evo 1 and 2 to generate novel bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) that demonstrated 
higher fitness than their wild-type (WT) counterparts (King et al., 2025). Efforts to inform the responsible 
development of AI tools used for biological research—through model evaluations, red teaming, and policy 
forums—are important but insufficient. Critically, these efforts often lack precise, structured definitions of 
what biological functions are being enabled or accelerated by AI, especially in the context of viral design.
Outline of the Report
In this report, we seek to fill that gap. Our goal was to develop an objective risk-scoring tool to analyze key 
biological functions that, when modified using synthetic biology techniques facilitated by AI tools, pose the 
greatest potential threats. We specifically focus on viral engineering and define the following five key modifi-
able biological functions that could be enhanced in the laboratory:
•  altered host range or tropism
•  increased genome replication
•  immune or medical countermeasure (MCM) evasion
•  increased environmental stability
•  increased transmission dynamics.
We designed a scoring tool with metrics to assess the impacts of modifying these functions and the actor 
capability level required to achieve those modifications. As part of assessing actor technical capability require-
ments, we also examined whether advancements in AI could enable lower-skilled actors. This dual-axis tool—
which scores biological modification risk and the likelihood of success—could provide a foundation for a

Viral Engineering and AI-Enabled Biological Research
Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites. They have a deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or ribonucleic acid 
(RNA) genome surrounded by a protein coat (enveloped viruses also have a lipid membrane), and they rep-
licate only inside living host cells (Baron, 1996; Luria et al., 1978). In other words, viruses are essentially 
genetic material—DNA or RNA—enclosed in a protective shell made of protein. They cannot reproduce on 
their own, so they hijack a host cell’s machinery to replicate and produce new viral particles. Because of their 
simplicity, well-characterized life cycles, and clinical and agricultural relevance, viruses offer a powerful 
system for studying core biological processes, such as genome replication, protein–nucleic acid interactions, 
and host-pathogen dynamics (Flint et al., 2015). These properties, combined with advances in molecular biol-
ogy and microbiology, also make them ideal platforms for engineering and design.
New AI Tools in Synthetic Biology and Viral Engineering
Revolutionary tool advances that have happened every decade since the 1970s have propelled the field 
of synthetic biology forward to its modern state-of-the-art position (Blattner et al., 1997; Helinski, 2022; 
Kaunitz, 2015; Komor, Badran, and Liu, 2017; Smith and Welcox, 1970). The 2020s have been marked by 
rapid advances in AI and synthetic biology, both of which have already demonstrated immense benefits. 
Some BTs, such as AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021) and ProGen (Madani et al., 2023), can predict and gen-
erate novel biomolecular structures, while others promise to elucidate incredibly complex gene regulatory 
networks (Afonja et al., 2024). The combination of (1) capabilities for nucleic acid sequence amplification, 
editing, assembly, and cloning; (2) access to immense repositories of genomic data; (3) the increasing capa-
bilities of BTs for novel design; and (4) an unraveling of gene expression promises the arrival of a new era 
in synthetic biology.
Contemporary Understandings of the Capabilities and Risks of Narrow 
Biological Tools
Capability analyses of the intersection of AI and biology have examined how broad AI tools, such as frontier 
models and AI agents, interact with biological information (Dev et al., 2025; Rose and Nelson, 2023). These 
technologies are different from narrow AI-enabled BTs that are trained on hyper-specific biological datasets 
to perform specific tasks. There have been calls for more research evaluating narrow BTs and their capabili-

Biosecurity Considerations
Advances in biotechnology and gain-of-function pathogen research have led to increased biosecurity con-
cerns.1 Such progress is seen as dual-use—useful for both legitimate science and illegitimate, malicious 
purposes—and as contributing to the risk of dangerous pathogens being leaked from labs. The recent explo-
sion of publicly accessible open-weight frontier AI models (e.g., Large Language Model Meta AI [Llama]) and 
the development of advanced tools for biological research (e.g., AlphaFold) have strongly amplified these 
dual-use concerns.
In 2012 and 2024, the U.S. government published policy guidance for the oversight of dual-use research of 
concern (DURC) in the life sciences (Administration for Preparedness and Response, 2012; Executive Office 
of the President of the United States, 2024). Additionally, in October 2023, the executive order “Safe, Secure, 
and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence” highlighted AI as contributing to risk in 
the creation of biological weapons (Executive Order 14110, 2023). Although this executive order was revoked 
on January 20, 2025, America’s AI action plan, which was released in July 2025, also acknowledges that the 
“most powerful AI systems may pose novel national security risks in the near future in areas such as cyber-
attacks and the development of chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear, or explosives (CBRNE) weapons” 
(White House, 2025, p. 22). The plan goes on to call for evaluations of frontier AI systems regarding their 
CBRNE risks (White House, 2025, p. 22).
It is essential that the benefits and risks associated with biological research are appropriately balanced. 
Overly strict regulation can throttle scientific progress and increase burdens such that only the most well-
established organizations are able to remain standing; however, a lack of regulation can allow lax safety 
requirements and passage along high-risk avenues of research. Thus, we envision that this scoring tool 
could serve as a foundation for a more robust decisionmaking framework that helps identify risks from 
AI-enabled biological research while ensuring that such work occurs safely and securely without stifling 
responsible innovation.

CHAPTER 2
Assessing Biological Capabilities in the Context of 
High-Risk Research
The National Research Council’s 2004 report Biotechnology Research in an Age of Terrorism (hereafter 
referred to as the Fink Report)1 introduced the concept of DURC. The Fink Report identified seven cat-
egories of biological experiments that could pose significant risks if misused. These included experiments 
that aimed to reduce the effectiveness of vaccines, increase pathogen transmissibility, enhance virulence, or 
enable the weaponization of a biological agent (Committee on Research Standards and Practices to Prevent 
the Destructive Application of Biotechnology, 2004). As the field of biotechnology has expanded and devel-
oped, the Fink Report maintains its relevance. Advances in biotechnology, such as gene-editing tools and 
AI, have since amplified concerns about dual-use research. In response, the U.S. government has issued sev-
eral policy frameworks to oversee such research, including the 2024 policy United States Government Policy 
for Oversight of Dual Use Research of Concern and Pathogens with Enhanced Pandemic Potential (Executive 
Office of the President of the United States, 2024) and the 2025 executive order “Improving the Safety and

Alterations to Biological Functions Most Relevant for Pathogen Design
Altered Host Range or Tropism
Description
Host range and tropism are determined by a combination of molecular, cellular, and environmental factors 
that influence where and how a pathogen can replicate and cause disease. One organism might be able to 
infect, be transmitted from, or replicate within a variety of other organisms. This is often referred to as host 
range. A pathogen might have a preference or specificity for interacting with certain hosts, tissues, or cell 
types. This is known as host tropism. Molecular, cellular, and environmental factors can influence host range 
and host tropism, thus affecting the site and mechanism by which a pathogen can replicate and cause dis-
ease. For a viral infection specifically, a virus must bind to and enter susceptible cells, typically by interacting 
with host receptors or through membrane fusion. Host receptors refer to specific molecules on the surface of 
susceptible cells. If a cell does not express the receptors necessary for virus binding, then the cell is not sus-
ceptible to infection. Therefore, the presence of proteins on the surface of a cell to which a virus successfully 
binds determines host cell specificity. The viral proteins necessary for interacting with host receptors are 
typically surface proteins in the protein coat (for nonenveloped viruses) or proteins that protrude from the 
outer lipid envelope (e.g., spike proteins). Virus-host interactions occur during the attachment phase of the 
infection, followed by viral entry, during which the virus enters the intracellular space.
Altering host range or tropism changes a virus’s ability to infect certain cell types or hosts. For example, 
the vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) glycoprotein is the main viral protein involved in viral entry, and altera-
tions to the VSV glycoprotein can deselect certain cell types from VSV infection, while other alterations can 
help adjust the virus toward targeting cancer cells (Hastie et al., 2013). Similarly, changes to the coronavi-
rus spike protein are also known to lead to changes in host tropism (Hulswit, de Haan, and Bosch, 2016). 
Together, these changes could lead to the creation of engineered pathogens that target different cell types or 
different species, thus tailoring the pathogen toward a particular set of targets.
Key Constraints Today
Viral surface proteins are highly evolved for specific host receptors, suggesting that altering native proteins 
to change host tropism would require nontrivial, multisite modifications. Genes that influence tropism 
might be entangled with the biology of the virus, such that altering tropism could be difficult without alter-
ing or losing functions elsewhere. Rational design approaches might be limited by data availability on host 
receptor protein or endosomal trafficking requirements. However, to circumvent this limitation, proteins 
important for host range could be swapped from different viruses entirely. For example, the major host 
receptor for the VSV glycoprotein is the low-density lipoprotein receptor (Nikolic et al., 2018). The function 
of this receptor in the host is to regulate cholesterol homeostasis, and it is expressed on almost all tissues 
(Nikolic et al., 2018; Protein Atlas, undated). Because it exhibits wide host tropism, it is commonly used to 
pseudotype lentiviruses in gene therapy applications to alter or improve virus tropism (Gutierrez-Guerrero, 
Cosset, and Verhoeyen, 2020).
Increased Genome Replication
Description
Virus attachment and entry are followed by the biosynthesis phase, which includes DNA or RNA replica-
tion and protein production. After entry, animal viruses uncoat so that the genetic material is released in the 
intercellular space outside the capsid (for bacteriophages, DNA is injected directly into the cytoplasmic space). 
Viral genetic material must then replicate within the host cell, ensuring that a single infectious unit produces

multiple progeny genomes. For DNA animal viruses, this typically happens in the host cell’s nucleus, while 
for RNA viruses, this typically happens in the cytoplasm. Although some viruses encode their own polymer-
ases for replication, others rely on host polymerases. A notable example of the former are RNA viruses that 
use their own RNA-dependent RNA polymerases to replicate their RNA. Nucleic acid reproduction is at the 
heart of an infection’s ability to propagate—reproduction of many copies, with the right balance of fidelity and 
mutation, can enable the long-term persistence of an infectious lineage within human hosts.
Increased genome replication would result in the production of more viral genomes and, thus, lead to more 
virions produced per unit time from infected cells. In addition to the viral proteins that might be involved, 
viruses could use other strategies toward increasing genome replication. Some viruses, such as rabies and 
respiratory syncytial virus, induce stress granule formation that could help with replication (Lindquist et al., 
2010; Nikolic et al., 2016). Other viruses might disrupt the typically highly regulated nucleocytoplasmic 
transport to aid in viral replication (Yarbrough et al., 2014). An engineered pathogen could take advantage of 
these viral and host processes to increase its own replication efficiency, thus leading to increased viral trans-
mission and potentially altering its virulence profile (Gupta, 2023).
Key Constraints Today
Genome replication requires cellular machinery or viral polymerases and often involves the evasion of cellular 
innate immunity. Insofar as host machinery is involved in genome replication, some constraints are imposed 
on attempts to augment the process. Furthermore, the number of infectious virions created is rate-limited 
not only by the number of genome copies but also by other key components and processes in viral packaging 
(Gastaminza et al., 2008; Nurmemmedov et al., 2012). In addition, altering viral or host factors could result 
in a cascade of effects that might be unintended and cause detrimental impacts to viral replication.
Immune or Medical Countermeasure Evasion
Description
On entry into a host’s system, viral pathogens find and infect susceptible cells, replicate, and egress from 
the infected cells as part of the pathogen’s life cycle. However, from the moment of entry into the host, the 
host’s immune system presents a challenge. In humans, on first exposure to a new virus, the innate immune 
system is the first to detect and respond through pathogen-associated molecular patterns. Toll-like recep-
tors and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain–like receptors are located at the plasma membrane and 
sense viral components, whereas retinoic acid-inducible gene I–like receptors are found in the cytoplasm. On 
detection of an infection, these three pathways activate a cascading response that ultimately results in the 
production of interferons and cytokines, including interleukins in a cell-type–dependent manner (Takeuchi 
and Akira, 2009). The innate immune system draws antigen-presenting cells and lymphocytes to the infec-
tion, thereby bridging the innate and adaptive immune systems that are largely mediated by B cells and T cells. 
B cells produce antibodies that help prevent further infection, while T cells target infected cells (Mueller and 
Rouse, 2008). This leads to a period of immunity against the initial viral pathogen during which the immune 
system remembers past infections and can mount a faster response on a subsequent encounter. Vaccines rely 
on this principle, and other antiviral MCMs rely on disrupting various stages of the viral life cycle (Kausar 
et al., 2021). In bacteria, just under a half dozen distinct antimicrobial mechanisms have been documented, 
including CRISPR-Cas, chemical defense, abortive infection, and innate mechanisms that include restriction 
site cleavage and toxin-antitoxin systems (Bernheim and Sorek, 2020; Zhang et al., 2022).
Viruses that are engineered and selected to have increased immune and MCM evasion characteristics 
would escalate the risk of disease and worsen outcomes. Viruses are known to use a plethora of strate-
gies, including accumulating mutations during antigenic drift, recombining and switching gene segments 
during antigenic shift, minimizing and masking viral genetic material, blocking signaling and intracellular

(Kim, Webster, and Webby, 2018; Minkoff and tenOever, 2023; van de Sandt, Kreijtz, and Rimmelzwaan, 
2012; van de Weijer, Luteijn, and Wiertz, 2015; Yao et al., 2023). In a now infamous 2001 mousepox study,

Key Constraints Today
Naturally occurring viruses exhibit immune evasion strategies that are context dependent because they have 
emerged from complex evolutionary pressures that arise between pathogens and hosts. There have been lim-
ited efforts in the lab to design such traits, aside from studies done to better understand viruses or to develop 
MCMs. Engineering immune evasion would be challenging because there is host-specific variability, meaning 
that improving evasion in one host or pathogen type might not effectively translate across all hosts or patho-
gens; because it could simultaneously cause fitness costs and hinder transmission or replication or because 
immune evasion could be polygenic and require modifying multiple proteins.
Whether engineered immune evasion can coexist with similar transmission profiles and virulence char-
acteristics of WT viruses remains an open question. Tools (such as EVEscape) that predict viral escape could 
be misused toward the creation of new pathogens, but laboratory experiments are still needed to confirm 
such in silico results (Thadani et al., 2023).
Increased Environmental Stability
Description
Viruses need a host to replicate in; without a host, individual viral particles are susceptible to environmen-
tal factors. Temperature, ultraviolet radiation, humidity, and other environmental factors (such as surface 
material) affect whether a virus can be viable within a host. For example, severe acute respiratory syndrome 
coronavirus (SARS-CoV) -1 and -2 have been found to be stable and remain viable up to three days on steel 
and plastic surfaces (van Doremalen et al., 2020). However, temperature extremes have also been found to 
affect viability. Infectious SARS-CoV-2 particles have been isolated at 28 days at 20°C, although the half-life 
at that temperature is up to three days (Riddell et al., 2020). Studies have also shown time variability in the 
reduction of viable virus levels in wastewater (Bivins et al., 2020). However, the starting titers—the amount of 
infectious virus present in a sample—is an important determinant in how much viable virus remains after a 
certain amount of time and at a certain temperature. Multiple hypotheses have been proposed for the exact 
mechanisms by which environmental factors affect viruses. In aerosolized enveloped viruses, for example, 
these mechanisms include water activity, surface inactivation because of physical stress and tension, salt 
effects on membranes, and changes in pH (Yang and Marr, 2012).
Increased environmental stability can cause an infectious virus to persist on fomites and aerosols for 
longer periods and, thus, increase the number of infected organisms. SARS-CoV-2 is rapidly inactivated in 
sunlight, thereby leading to a differential risk between indoor and outdoor environments (Ratnesar-Shumate 
et al., 2020). Viruses that are resistant to ultraviolet radiation could also lessen the effectiveness of steriliza-
tion methods involving this type of radiation. This concern also applies to increased stability against other 
chemical cleaning agents.

Key Constraints Today
As noted previously, various environmental conditions can affect the half-life of viruses. The specific changes 
needed to make a virus more stable can greatly depend on the exact virus, including whether a virus is envel-
oped or nonenveloped. Some viruses require fluid transmission and do not survive well in aerosols. Others 
do not survive well outside of aerosols and can be affected by volume and droplet evaporation (French et al., 
2023). As with other viral characteristics, changes that render a virus more stable could have unforeseen 
effects and reduce virulence, for example.
Increased Transmission Dynamics
Description
Viral transmission is the act of a virus moving between hosts, either through intraspecies or interspecies 
transmission. This can be facilitated through the spread of fomites, aerosols, saliva, and/or other bodily 
fluids, and viral transmission is linked to factors affecting environmental stability (as noted in the previ-
ous section). For example, influenza is primarily spread via the respiratory pathways (Cowling et al., 2013; 
Richard et al., 2020), while human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is spread via bodily fluids, resulting in 
horizontal and vertical transmission (Amin et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2019). The amount of time before an 
infection leads to symptoms and serious disease can affect transmission. Ebola patients rapidly deteriorate 
and are highly infectious; however, because of the speed by which patients become ill, the Ebola virus does 
not always spread widely beyond its initial starting point. The viral shedding that happens before major 
symptoms appear can also facilitate increased transmission (Lewis et al., 2021). Transmission dynamics are 
also discussed from the perspective of viral-host receptor recognition and binding. Viruses undergoing anti-
genic shift and drift can lead to the expansion of the host range both within a species and between species. 
Increases in viral transmission dynamics result in the more-efficient spread between susceptible, and poten-
tially susceptible, organisms.
Key Constraints Today
In addition to pathogen biology, large-scale spread depends on host behaviors, social factors, and geogra-
phy. Forecasting the consequences of specific molecular changes on both epidemiological and time scales 
is challenging and can be expected to remain so for the foreseeable future. Directly engineering viruses for 
increased transmission changes that enable cross-species transmission (most concerningly from a natural 
reservoir into humans) requires overcoming species differences at multiple dimensions—including the cel-
lular, organismal, and societal levels. Reservoirs more similar to humans are more likely to present simpler 
challenges because host receptor binding sites are likelier to be more closely related at the molecular level. As 
with other viral characteristics discussed in this chapter, genetic changes leading to increased transmission 
could affect other characteristics. Viral engineering for this trait will need to account for such a challenge.

Although previous studies and reports (such as the Fink Report and the United States Government Policy for 
Oversight of Dual Use Research of Concern and Pathogens with Enhanced Pandemic Potential) have outlined 
high-risk research categories, none has sought to assess the levels of risk associated with different DURC 
experiment types, particularly in the context of pathogen design and emerging technologies in synthetic biol-
ogy (Committee on Research Standards and Practices to Prevent the Destructive Application of Biotechnol-
ogy, 2004; Executive Office of the President of the United States, 2024). Once we identified the five modifiable 
biological functions explained in Chapter 2, we aimed to develop a risk-scoring tool that could measure both 
risk factors (i.e., the potential negative impacts that could be caused by modified biological functions) and 
likelihoods of success (i.e., what is the minimum actor skill level required to execute the modification given 
sufficient knowledge, access to resources, and capacity) (Figure 3.1). We refer to the former as the biological 
modification risk-scoring system and the latter as the actor capability scoring system. This risk-scoring tool 
allows us to better understand (1) how consequential modifying one of the biological functions would be and 
(2) the actor capability levels that we anticipate would be required to modify the biological function in ques-
tion. The tool could also help assess how the capability levels for modifying each biological function change 
over time given the rapid advancement of emerging technologies.
What follows is a description of how the biological modification risk and actor capability scoring systems 
were developed. The ability to anticipate how achievable different experiment types will be given existing 
or future technologies, along with the potential consequences of such research, will be critically important 
for the biosecurity community to consider before such research is performed or before bad actors are able 
to perform said experiments. The actor capability scoring system is designed to assess varying degrees of 
expertise and resources that could affect an actor’s ability to modify one of the five key biological func-
tions outlined in Chapter 2 and whether AI could enable the success of a less capable actor. The scales were 
derived from the work of RAND researchers who analyzed biosecurity risks in complex laboratory spaces 
(Del Castello and Willis, 2025).
The risk-scoring tool can also be used to estimate the anticipated benefits of such research with the aim 
of ensuring that those benefits outweigh the risks. However, we did not develop a benefit score in this report 
because our objective was to evaluate the severity of misuse, and in such an evaluation, there are no benefits. 
A separate report that applies the severity of consequences scale to weigh the risks and benefits of DURC and 
pathogens with enhanced pandemic potential can be found in Williams et al. (2025).

risk assessment literature, such as the Collection of Biothreat Risk Assessments (COBRA) archive (COBRA,

Indicators and associated scoring metrics are included for each of the five risk factors. Each indicator is

and pandemic potential) that must be inferred. Each risk factor has two to three associated indicators with

Semiquantitative Biological Risk Factors: Indicators and Scoring Systems
Health Impacts in a Model System
This risk factor concerns a modified biological agent that is more virulent than an unmodified version, could 
cause worse disease outcomes (including lasting morbidity after recovery), or could lead to more-significant 
morbidity or mortality than an unmodified agent in humans. For experiments that alter host range and enable 
the agent to infect humans, this factor includes any resulting morbidity or mortality in the new model host 
after exposure to the modified agent. Determining the control group for comparison purposes can be com-
plex, particularly for reassortant mutants or strains generated by recombinant technologies, and baselines 
might vary. It is important to specify the comparison group when applying the scoring system for this factor.
In the following sections, we explain the indicators used to assess this risk factor and their corresponding 
scoring systems.
Increased Virulence in a Model System
This indicator captures the degree of harm or damage that a biological agent causes once it infects a host. 
The most-direct in vitro measurements in model cells that are meant to mimic human impacts might include 
such tests as cytopathic effect (CPE), cell death pathway assays (e.g., apoptosis), and median tissue culture 
infectious dose (TCID50). The most-direct in vivo metrics in a model system that is meant to mimic human 
impacts might include a histopathology of organ or tissue damage (e.g., lung hemorrhage, inflammation). 
Indirect measurements might include cytokine secretion assays, metabolic assays, tissue chip models, and 
transcriptomic profiling.

This indicator captures how sick a host becomes after exposure to a modified biological agent, including the 
resulting symptoms and functional impairments, regardless of whether the infection is fatal. Direct in vivo 
tests might include weight loss, activity-level monitoring, and clinical scoring of symptoms. More indirect in 
vitro tests might include cytokine secretion assays, metabolic assays, or tissue chip models.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that there are no observable signs of disease or illness compared with a control.
•  A 0.5 means that there is mild illness or moderate clinical signs of disease in some of the animals

control.
•  A 1 means that there are significantly more sick animals or disease presentation in a significant number

•  NA means that the indicator cannot or will not be measured, cannot be reasonably inferred, or is not

Increased Mortality in a Model System
This indicator captures the extent to which exposure to the modified biological agent leads to death in a 
model system relative to a control study. The most-direct measurements in a model system that are meant 
to mimic human impacts might include survival curves, median lethal dose, median mouse lethal dose, 
time-to-death postinoculation, or humane endpoint triggers. Indirect measurements might include the 
virulence indexes described previously.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that there is no mortality observed in a model system compared with a control.
•  A 0.5 means that there are low levels of mortality observed in a model system, even if not significantly 
elevated, compared with controls.
•  A 1 means that there are significant levels of mortality in a model system compared with controls; for 
research altering host range, this includes any mortality in a new host.
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.

most-direct in vitro metrics in model systems that are meant to mimic human impacts might include infec-
tion studies using human or humanized cell lines or in vivo transmission studies in model systems. Indirect 
in vitro studies might include protein–protein binding assays between surface host and agent proteins.

•  A 0 means that there is no change in host range.

Medical Countermeasure Availability and Immune Recognition
This risk factor concerns a modified biological agent that could lead to less available, ready-to-deploy MCMs 
or a modification that allows the agent to evade or overwhelm the immune response. Such a modified bio-
logical agent would limit the possibility of controlling it and facilitating public health interventions. Scores 
for this factor were adapted from the integrated technology readiness levels for MCM products from the 
Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority (MCM, undated).
In the following sections, we explain the indicators used to assess this risk factor and their corresponding 
scoring systems.
Decreased Number of Medical Countermeasures That Are Available or Ready
This indicator entails a reduction in the effectiveness or availability of ready-to-deploy MCMs (including 
vaccines, antivirals, monoclonal antibodies, and antibiotics) against the modified biological agent. The 
reduction could be measured by a plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) to assess the agent’s antibody-
neutralizing ability, an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and flow cytometry to evaluate 
antibody binding or the efficacy of treatment in animal models. More-indirect in vitro tests might include 
treating cell lines with or without a modified agent after exposure to different treatments and measuring 
functional endpoints.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that there are approved or preexisting MCMs that are available.
•  A 0.5 means that preexisting MCMs are less viable or function less effectively.
•  A 1 means that there are no available MCMs or no MCMs that are in the pipeline or development stages.
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.
Decreased Diagnostic Capability
This indicator captures a reduction in the ability of existing diagnostic tools (e.g., ELISA, PCR) to detect a 
modified biological agent accurately or reliably. Direct measurements might include evaluating false negative 
or positive rates in PCR assays, antigen tests, or serological tests when applied to the modified agent or evalu-
ating antibody binding or cross-reactivity using standard diagnostics. Indirect measurements might include 
genetic analyses, such as sequence divergence analyses to identify mutations in primer or probe-binding sites.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that researchers are able to detect the modified biological agent by existing diagnostic tests.
•  A 0.5 means that existing diagnostic tests of the modified agent produce more false positives or negatives 
compared with tests of the unmodified agent.
•  A 1 means that, beyond whole genome sequencing, existing diagnostic capabilities are unable to detect 
the modified agent.
•  NA means that the indicator cannot or will not be measured, cannot be reasonably inferred, or is not 
relevant.

Host Immune Evasion
This indicator entails the increased ability of a modified agent to avoid detection or neutralization by the host’s 
immune system, including both innate and adaptive responses. Direct measurements might include reduced 
neutralization by PRNT, decreased antibody binding in ELISA or flow cytometry, impaired T-cell activation 
by intracellular cytokine staining assays, or in vivo evidence of reinfection despite prior immunity. Indirect 
measurements might include sequence or structural changes in key antigenic sites or epitope mapping.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that the modified agent does not evade the host’s immune response.
•  A 0.5 means that the modified agent partially evades the host’s existing immunity.
•  A 1 means that the host has no preexisting immunity (i.e., the agent is something that the host’s immune 
system has never encountered), the modified agent evades the host’s existing immunity (this includes 
overwhelming innate immune mechanisms), or, if there is preexisting immunity, the modified agent 
exacerbates disease outcomes.
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.
Qualitative Societal Factors: Indicators and Scoring Systems
Although the evaluation indicators that we have described focus on semiquantitative risk factors, there are 
often secondary, societal implications that should be considered. The following indicators represent a need 
for integrating qualitative components, particularly those related to societal and environmental harm, that—
although likely more complex in their grading—are nonetheless part of the larger risk ecosystem.
Potential Societal or Environmental Impact
This risk factor concerns a modified biological agent that, if released into the environment, could cause 
significant harm to human populations, the environment, and/or the economy compared with an unal-
tered form of the infectious agent. This factor includes a large-scale epidemic in human populations, animal 
populations, or the food supply, and the agent’s release could have national security implications. This factor 
is more difficult to directly measure, but implications could be derived from, for example, transmission or 
virulence studies or One Health considerations.
In the following sections, we explain the indicators used to assess this risk factor and their corresponding 
scoring systems.
Ecological Impacts
The release of a modified agent could endanger or cause the extinction of any species that are not part of the 
cash economy (e.g., pets, wildlife) or could have greater negative impacts on environmental health indicators, 
such as soil quality or drinking water quality, than the release of the unmodified agent would. These impacts 
would have indirect effects on human well-being.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that there is no anticipated or measurable impact on nontarget species or ecosystems.
•  A 0.5 means that the modified agent could cause a localized or short-term ecological disruption (e.g., it 
could affect a single species or population).
•  A 1 means that the modified agent could cause broad, long-lasting, or irreversible ecological damage 
(e.g., disrupt the food web, lead to species extinction).
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.

Critical Agriculture and Food Systems
The release of a modified agent could more negatively affect systems that are important for human survival 
or well-being and economic stability, excluding direct human health outcomes but including systems related 
to food-producing animals, livestock, agricultural crops, farm-raised animals, and drinking water quality or 
infected water. This indicator captures risks to economic, national, and agricultural security caused by the 
disruption of critical biological or environmental systems, including significant impacts on laboratory ani-
mals but excluding human mortality and morbidity, which are captured in the “health impacts in a model 
system” risk factor.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that the modified agent has no anticipated or measurable effect on livestock, crops, or other 
economically significant organisms.
•  A 0.5 means that the modified agent could cause moderate disruption (e.g., reduced yield in crops or 
livestock illness that could affect production).
•  A 1 means that the modified agent could cause major disruptions to agricultural systems or animal 
production (e.g., livestock die-off, crop failure, food insecurity).
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.
Essential Human and Societal Systems
The release of a modified agent could negatively affect essential systems that support modern life and public 
welfare, such as health care, transportation, utilities, supply chains, and critical workforces compared with 
the release of an unmodified agent. This indicator focuses on indirect social and economic impacts beyond 
those captured in the “health impacts in a model system” risk factor.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that there is no anticipated or measurable effect on essential services, supply chain, or 
quality of life.
•  A 0.5 means that the modified agent could cause a partial or temporary disruption of infrastructure 
(e.g., hospital overcapacity, localized supply chain disruption).
•  A 1 means that the modified agent has the potential to cause a widespread, sustained disruption of 
infrastructure (e.g., health care collapse, halted transportation, national economic impact).
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.
Adversary Control and Pandemic Potential
This risk factor concerns a modified biological agent that would allow a bad actor more control over the 
modified agent or that poses catastrophic risks to society if released into the environment. This factor is 
more difficult to directly measure, but its implications could be derived from considering the ability of a bad 
actor to misuse the information or constructs generated, the ability of a bad actor to program the expression 
of the agent or target certain hosts, or the extent to which one or more of the other indicators were modified 
(e.g., there could be a notably significant increase in morbidity or mortality and the modification might lead 
to grave impacts on humankind).
In the following sections, we explain the indicators used to assess this risk factor and their corresponding 
scoring systems.
Actor Ability to Control the Modified Biological Agent
This indicator captures the extent to which an attacker could control, modulate, or direct the behavior, 
dissemination, or impact of a modified agent. The ability to control the agent includes adjustable features 
(its expression can be turned on or off) or the selective targeting of specific populations.

The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that the modified biological agent has no additional features compared with a naturally 
occurring agent that could enhance an attacker’s ability to control its dissemination or spread.
•  A 0.5 means that the modified agent has some added control features, but they are not highly specific 
or easily adjustable.
•  A 1 means that the modified agent has a high degree of controllability or specificity (e.g., genetic 
switches, population-targeting mechanisms, on and off triggers).
•  NA means that the indicator cannot be reasonably inferred or is not relevant.
Global Catastrophic Event Possibility
This indicator captures additional intangible or emergent risk factors or some other contribution that is not 
captured in the previous indicators. This indicator reflects uncertainties, philosophical considerations, or the 
potential for unforeseen consequences based on the implications of the research, and it highlights research 
elements that could elevate the possibility of global-scale catastrophic outcomes.
The scoring system for this indicator is as follows:
•  A 0 means that no additional concerns are warranted; there is no indication that the study introduces 
risks beyond existing baseline threats.
•  A 0.5 means that some expert uncertainty exists regarding the potential for catastrophic outcomes; the 
research raises cautionary red flags, but it remains unclear whether any outcomes of the research could 
truly lead to something catastrophic.
•  A 1 means that there is a strong justification on the basis of existing knowledge, to emphasize the 
heightened severity or unanticipated catastrophic risks associated with this research if it were to be 
misused; the study might reveal novel or previously unrecognized pathways that could be misapplied 
or lead to global catastrophe.
Scoring System Overview and Example
The scores of all 14 indicators are summed, and the higher the score, the more severe the impact. A rating of 
NA means that the information will not be measured and cannot be inferred, so it cannot be included in the 
score. Fourteen is the highest impact score, while 0 is the lowest. All indicators are defined relative to either a 
control, such as a nonaltered version of the virus (WT strain), or the absence of the biological agent. Table 3.1 
provides an example of how the scoring system might be used.
Actor Capability Scoring System
The actor capability scoring system is a semiquantitative scale that considers an actor’s knowledge, facility, 
and capacity. Each category is defined by the following three characteristics:
•  knowledge—tacit and referential knowledge and the ability to ideate and execute experiments to create 
novel bioproducts
•  facility—financial resources and material resources (in the form of labs and equipment)
•  capacity—staffing and personnel.
These characteristics are derived from the work of RAND researchers who analyzed risk in complex labora-
tory spaces (Del Castello and Willis, 2025).

Each category is graded on a three-point scale: if the actor is not capable of fulfilling a category, they receive 
a 0; if they have questionable capabilities, they receive a 1; and if they are capable, they receive a 2 (Table 3.2). 
Scoring should be done by experts with knowledge of both the technology in question (e.g., does AI provide 
any sort of assistance to the actor performing this task?) and the protocol or experiment that would be theo-
retically performed by the actor (e.g., does a representative actor have the knowledge, facility, and capacity to 
perform the protocol or experiment?). Expert opinions are pooled to calculate the average for each category. 
Possible scores range from 0 to 6. Although the numbers in each category can and do increase over time, that 
increase does not have to be in a linear fashion. There might be instances of decline in an area because of 
extenuating circumstances, which should be noted. Additionally, some categories might not see immediate 
increases over time and could develop more slowly. We provide an example of this scoring system in Table 3.3.
The actor capability score is then calculated as how many actors have achieved a 4 or more on the capabil-
ity scale. We chose a score of 4 because it can be achieved only if the actor has either two yeses or one yes and 
two maybes—which would indicate potential for success. In the example provided, the potential-of-success 
score would be 3, because three categories received a total score of 4 or greater. Additionally, the potential-of-
success score is calculated for the following three conditions:
•  pre-AI—a baseline score of how capable the actor would be without the use of AI
•  current AI—a score of how capable the actor is in summer 2025
•  future AI—a score of how capable the actor would be in one year (which, for this study, would be 
summer 2026).
The future condition was set under the expectation that AI would continue to rapidly develop. These condi-
tions allow for an analysis of how AI is affecting actor capabilities (Figure 3.2).

Applying the Risk-Scoring Tool to Example Use Cases
To demonstrate the risk-scoring tool described in Chapter 3, our team applied the tool to a series of published 
studies to assess the risks of using AI-enabled biological research to modify the five previously described 
modifiable biological functions, evaluate the impact of AI-enabled biological research on threat actor capa-
bilities, and estimate how these capabilities could change as AI advances. We engaged in a series of iterative 
team discussions that included individuals with expertise in biology and AI to determine scores for each ele-
ment in the risk-scoring tool.
We evaluated ten published studies, examining two studies for each of the five modifiable biological 
functions (altered host range or tropism, increased genome replication, immune or MCM evasion, increased 
environmental stability, and increased transmission dynamics). We focused on the impact of modifications 
to these functions and assessed actor capabilities in the context of present and future AI abilities. Although 
we meant for each selected paper to primarily represent one of the five modifiable functions discussed in 
Chapter 2, each paper was evaluated for impact across all criteria because impact, in general, is not limited by 
a single function, and modifying one function might lead to modification in another.

CHAPTER 5
Discussion and Future Directions
This report provides a foundation for how actor capability levels and modifications to viral functions can 
be scored to assess the risks of AI-enabled biological designs. This tool could be a starting point for a more 
robust framework that assesses the risks of using AI in biological research. Further, it could provide a valu-
able foundation for creating an objective mechanism to investigate and establish potential redlines in this 
type of research.
Our approach to assessing the risks of AI-enabled biological design involved applying our scoring tool to 
several published studies, but it could also be applied to other exploratory methods. Evaluations of existing 
AI capabilities rely on consistent, broad testing of all known capabilities. With the assistance of the scor-
ing system, more-modular and smaller evaluations could be conducted to test whether AI tools assist in 
performing specific wet bench tasks. Assessments could be regularly run with safe and unsafe AI models to 
compare results. It is worth noting that scoring might differ depending on how advanced the AI model being 
examined is, and individualized scores might be helpful for understanding specific developments by specific 
models, but the scores should not be considered generalizable.
An additional avenue for future work could be using an AI model to automate the scoring system. The model 
could learn patterns from a large dataset of expert-scored research studies and identify associations between 
experimental details and biological modification risk or actor capability scores. As more expert examples 
become available, the AI could improve its ability to generalize and consistently replicate expert assessments, 
potentially streamlining risk evaluations of new studies and reducing subjectivity in future applications.
Our scoring tool could also be applied to larger-scale evaluations to assess AI-enabled biological design 
for the purpose of informing potential redlines. One example of this is using a protein competition to assess 
AI capabilities that modify viral functions using a safe model organism as a proxy. We discuss how this could 
be accomplished in Appendix A and offer some key considerations regarding potential information hazards 
(infohazards) should this method be used.

Key Considerations
There are several key considerations that could be built on in future studies to enhance the robustness and 
applicability of the risk-scoring tool. For example, the key biological function modifications, the risk factors 
and their associated indicators, and the actor capability levels were assessed on a small number of studies by 
our team. Expanding the scope to include a broader, more representative range of research is necessary to 
ensure that the risk factors and indicators are applicable across diverse experimental and technological con-
texts, improving the external validity and reliability of the tool.
Additionally, there is limited information on AI uplift and no clear empirical benchmarks, so future 
research is necessary to better understand how actor capabilities will change given technological advance-
ments. Similarly, the risk-scoring tool does not provide a quantitative mechanism to evaluate how AI could 
facilitate modifying the biological functions discussed herein. We acknowledge that a limitation to this tool 
is that evaluators group the modifiable functions on a yes or no basis as opposed to measuring how much AI 
increases the risk of the modification type over time. A more quantitative method to evaluate the increasing 
risks posed by AI would be beneficial for evaluating AI capabilities over time. Finally, engaging a wider spec-
trum of subject-matter experts from diverse backgrounds would provide a more balanced risk evaluation. 
Although the tool has been developed after expert consensus, there are still inherent subjectivities that will 
lead to variable scores. Future work applying the tool to worked examples or exercises would help standardize 
interpretations and increase consistency.
The risk-scoring tool neither includes a mechanism to weight scores nor establishes score thresholds 
for assessing risk or threat actor capability levels. We acknowledge that some factors could have a greater 
influence on overall severity than others, even though weights were not applied in this report. Future work 
involving expert elicitation to determine appropriate weights for each indicator is warranted. There are also 
potential correlations among indicators because there are some inherent overlaps. This could lead to double 
counting and an over- or underestimation of the risk score. Explicitly noting these instances when applying 
the tool and, when feasible, statistically adjusting for them will be necessary to improve the robustness of the 
scores. Future work developing weights could quantify and take into account these correlations.
For the actor capability scoring system, instead of counting how many actor levels have achieved the nec-
essary threshold of capabilities, it might be possible to determine the lowest-capability actor that is able to 
pass the threshold. This could be of interest to individuals who are examining AI uplift in actors. We opted

A practical application of the tool might require the development of empirically derived or consensus-driven 
score thresholds, particularly if the tool is used to inform regulatory redlines or operational decisionmaking. 
Integrating thresholds, alongside refined weighting and expanded expert input, will be central to producing a 
fully robust tool that is actionable for policy and oversight. Continued iteration with real-world case studies, 
benchmarking against other risk assessment frameworks, and transparent documentation of all assumptions 
and deliberations that went into how each score was decided (as we provide in Appendix B) will ensure that the 
tool evolves to meet the needs of stakeholders across the science, security, and policy domains.
Concluding Remarks
The convergence of AI and biotechnology will continue to enable scientific breakthroughs, including through 
protein engineering, genome design, and predictive modeling. Although AI-enabled biological research 
offers exciting new avenues, it might also pose dual-use risks—for example, by enabling a malicious actor to 
design or enhance harmful biological agents. Despite this potential threat, there is a lack of assessments that 
could be used to draw redlines. In this report, we developed a scoring tool for understanding the risks posed 
by altering viral functions that was centered on two axes: the potential consequences of misuse and the actor 
capabilities required to modify each viral function.
Although our framing here is toward a biological capability assessment for functions of concern, trade-
offs exist in biological research in which experimental modifications can lead to unpredictable changes. 
Viral engineering that leads to an upregulation of transmission, for example, could also lead to a lowering 
of virulence. Thus, as of this writing, it is difficult to map these capabilities in isolation, and assessments 
should consider combining multiple capabilities in a biologically relevant manner. In addition, the use 
of certain proxies might not adequately reflect the life cycle and characteristics of more-dangerous viral 
pathogens. This could lead to inaccurate measurements and conclusions, thereby limiting the impact that 
assessments using the risk-scoring tool, such as the one discussed in Appendix A, can have.
Taken together, we need robust assessments to measure potentially dangerous capabilities that AI can 
provide toward viral engineering and pathogen design. Our scoring tool approach is a first step in examining 
the potential impacts of AI-enabled biological research and informing future redlines in this space.

A protein competition is a structured challenge in which participants develop and test computational 
models for predicting or designing protein sequences with desired functions, typically through a combi-
nation of in silico prediction and in vitro validation. These competitions serve as transparent benchmarks, 
democratize access to experimental resources, and generate datasets that inform best practices and ethi-

Critical Assessment of Function Annotation (Function Special Interest Group, undated), Align Bio’s Tour-

primarily on predicting protein function or designing individual proteins, such as enzymes or binders.

what will be necessary to evaluate synthetic biology capabilities in optimizing or designing entire patho-
gens. Some competitions have focused on more-complex endpoints than single protein-ligand interactions. 
For example, the International Genetically Engineered Machine (iGEM) competition is a model for what 
large-scale competitions can look like and a demonstration of interest in protein engineering from rising 
researchers in the field of synthetic biology. In its most recent competition in 2024, more than 400 iGEM 
teams participated, many of which relied on protein engineering (iGEM, undated).

viral entry, replication, and host specificity. We propose that the scoring tool designed herein could inform

Increased Replication Via O and P Protein Modulation
Background
Bacteriophage λ replicates its DNA in two distinct phases: an early bidirectional (θ) mode and a later 
unidirectional (σ or rolling circle) mode (Hayes, Horbay, and Hayes, 2012). During the early phase, the 
phage-encoded O and P proteins are essential for initiating replication by recruiting host machinery to the 
λ replication origin (oriλ) (Alfano and McMacken, 1989). The O protein binds oriλ and forms a nucleopro-
tein complex known as the O-some, coating the origin with O dimers to facilitate DNA unwinding. O then 
recruits the P protein to form the oriλ-O-P complex, which enables the loading of the host DnaB helicase. 
Host heat-shock proteins disassemble the complex, allowing DnaB to initiate bidirectional replication of 
the viral genome (Zylicz et al., 1984). In the later phase of infection, λ switches to rolling circle replication 
to generate concatemeric DNA for packaging, a process in which O and P are no longer required (Barańska 
et al., 2001; Erdile and Inman, 1984).
Competition Round Objective
The objective of this competition round would be to assess how AI can be used to enhance viral genome 
replication by designing novel variants of bacteriophage λ O and P proteins, which are essential for initi-
ating early bidirectional replication. Participants would use AI-based computational methods to engineer 
O and P proteins that retain or improve their cooperative interactions with host replication factors, par-
ticularly in assembling the O-some complex, facilitating DNA unwinding, and positioning the host DnaB 
helicase at the oriλ. The round could also be structured into two progressively complex sprints. In Phase 
2A, participants would have to restore the replication function to WT levels in λ strains carrying known 
attenuating mutations in O and P without reverting to the original WT sequences. In Phase 2B, participants 
would design O and P variants that exceed WT replication efficiency, pushing the boundaries of AI-driven 
protein optimization.

Immune or Medical Countermeasure Evasion
Background
Evasion of vaccine-induced immunity occurs when a virus acquires mutations that reduce recognition by 
antibodies generated through vaccination, an artificially acquired active mechanism of stimulating anti-
bodies. This can lead to decreased vaccine effectiveness, breakthrough infections, and challenges in out-
break containment. Although λ phage naturally infects bacteria and does not encounter adaptive immune 
responses elicited through artificial means, it can still be used as a safe model for studying immune escape 
from vaccine-induced antibodies. When mice are immunized with purified λ phage, they generate polyclonal 
antibodies against surface-exposed proteins. This model provides a controlled system for evaluating whether 
AI-designed modifications to phage surface proteins can reduce antibody binding and neutralization. Such 
experiments offer insight into how AI-enabled tools could be used to design pathogens capable of evading 
vaccine-based countermeasures.

Infohazard Limitations
When such information as data, protocols, or developed tools can be used to cause harm, it is important to 
establish how, when, and where that information is released to minimize the risk potential (Lewis et al., 2019). 
In the biosecurity community, these types of information are commonly referred to as infohazards.

Our team recognizes the infohazard potential of executing a protein competition, such as the one described 
in this appendix. Future researchers seeking to design or conduct a protein competition to assess AI capabili-
ties to modify key biological functions might also encounter infohazards. Because of this, we recommend 
the following measures to mitigate the proliferation of infohazards if such a competition were carried out:
•  Establish an infohazard panel. Advanced planning by the organizers is essential for managing poten-
tial infohazards. This could be handled by establishing a panel of experts that is responsible for iden-
tifying, assessing, and mitigating infohazards. This panel could assess infohazards incrementally—
such as by team, by assessing individual pieces of information, or holistically. This infohazard review 
could be analogous to a derivative classification review that is done for classified information in 
which individual pieces of information are labeled according to the varying degrees of national secu-
rity harm they might cause should they be released to the public. This panel could also make decisions 
about infohazard risks that arise when competition information is compiled, and the infohazard risks 
related to the compiled data would be analyzed.
•  Apply an infohazard framework or methodology. Applying a framework or methodology to assess, 
identify, and mitigate infohazards is key for the proposed competition. Such a process will better equip 
an infohazard panel to identify, assess, and mitigate infohazards. It can also steer decisionmaking by 
the panel when making recommendations about what information should be released publicly and the 
format of such information.
•  Release summarized results suitable for different audiences. If there are infohazards generated by the 
proposed competition, summarized results can be released rather than detailed results. The infohazard 
panel, using a predetermined framework and information review process as described previously, can 
determine the content and detail level of summary results that might be suitable for public consump-
tion. The panel can also make decisions about developing more-detailed summaries that are suitable for 
sharing with participants and competition sponsors.
Communicating the infohazard risk information, protocols, and decisionmaking processes with partici-
pants and sponsors in advance of the competition is key because many participants might expect to have the 
opportunity to publish their results in full. Therefore, it is important to communicate infohazard risks and 
information-sharing processes and rules to participants and sponsors before the competition begins.

This appendix illustrates how the scores for both the biological modification risks and the capability levels 
of the actors performing these modifications were evaluated and determined. Members of our project team 
served as the pool of experts and conducted two rounds of scoring. We recommend that, in future uses of this 
method, researchers outsource to experts where possible.
In Figures B.1 through B.6, we present the average of the combined scores from both rounds of scoring for 
studies on altered host range or tropism, increased genome replication, and increased transmission dynamics. 
Figures B.1, B.3, and B.5 illustrate the average biological modification risk scores, and Figures B.2, B.4, and B.6 
illustrate the average actor capability scores for select studies on these modifiable biological functions. For the 
results of the increased environmental stability and immune or MCM evasion modifications, please refer to 
Chapter 3. The subsequent sections will break down the highly detailed scoring process that occurred over two 
rounds of discussion, during which one study about each biological modification was selected and discussed. 
Scores presented in this appendix represent decisions agreed on through group discussions by subject-matter 
experts on our research team.

Altered Host Range or Tropism Study
To investigate whether bat-restricted SARS-CoV-1 strains could evolve the ability to infect human cells, Men-
achery et al. (2015) generated a chimeric virus that expressed SARS-CoV-1 bat spike protein in a mouse-
adapted SARS-CoV-1 backbone (the chimera is called SHC014-MA15 in the manuscript). The chimera virus 
was able to infect Vero (monkey) cells, primary human airway epithelial cells, and a mouse model. Chimera 
and mouse SARS-CoV-1 produced nearly identical viral titers in the mouse model, but the chimera led to less 
lethality and weight loss in mice compared with a WT virus. The chimera was less targeted by SARS-CoV-1 
monoclonal antibodies compared with the human SARS-CoV-1 strain (Urbani).

Increased Transmission Dynamics Study
In this study, Herfst et al. (2012) used site-directed mutagenesis on a highly pathogenic avian influenza strain 
(HPAI A/H5N1), which is not naturally transmissible via respiratory droplets, and serially passaged the virus 
in ferrets until it acquired airborne transmissibility. No significant differences in viral titers were observed 
in the respiratory tissues between the WT and mutant virus strains following intranasal infection. However, 
mutant virus titers were significantly higher in nasal swabs after transmission at specific points. Airborne 
transmission of the mutant virus did not result in mortality among recipient ferrets, although one of eight 
ferrets died after direct intranasal inoculation. The mutant virus remained sensitive to oseltamivir and was 
recognized by antibodies generated against existing H5 vaccines.

Increased Transmission Dynamics Study
In their study, Imai et al. (2012) introduced random mutations into the HA gene of a human-isolated H5N1 
strain, which retained avian-type receptor specificity. They screened for variants capable of binding to human-
type receptors by solid phase binding assays and immunohistochemistry with human airway tissues. Select HA 
mutants were then incorporated into reassortant viruses with internal genes from the 2009 H1N1 strain. Result-
ing mutant virus strains were evaluated in ferrets, and a virus was isolated from the nasal wash of high-shedding 
ferret (107.5 PFU/ml) for a transmission study. This mutant virus HA (N158D/N224K/Q226L/T318I)/CA04, 
replicated efficiently in nasal cavities of four (out of six) contact ferrets and was isolated from brain tissue. 
The infection caused lung lesions and weight loss but was not highly pathogenic and did not cause mortality. 
Notably, the variant demonstrated efficient respiratory droplet transmission between ferrets. This transmissible 
virus also retained sensitivity to oseltamivir and was neutralized by antisera from individuals vaccinated with 
a prototype H5N1 vaccine.
There are several enhanced biosecurity aspects of this work worth mentioning: (1) The researchers did not 
use a WT H5N1 virus but a reassortant with H5 HA and the remaining seven segments from the 2009 H1N1 
pandemic strain; (2) there was an absence of serial passaging in the ferrets; (3) the researchers replaced the 
multibasic HA cleavage sequence with a nonvirulent-type cleavage sequence, making it a BSL2 virus to test 
library mutants (however, the mammalian transmissible virus tested in the ferrets did have the cleavage site); 
and (4) like other viral reverse genetic systems, the study made use of HA mutants in plasmids separate from 
other viral genes.
