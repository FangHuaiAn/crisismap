Summary 
Issue 
In 2023, the U.S. Air Force (USAF)1 implemented several changes to the officer and enlisted 
performance evaluation systems, including a redesign of the performance reports and a change in the 
USAF’s approach to providing developmental feedback. The USAF asked RAND Project AIR 
FORCE to first establish a method to evaluate and monitor whether the performance evaluation 
changes are having the intended effects and then conduct the designed evaluation.  
Approach 
We used a mixed-method approach that focused on Regular component Airmen and included a 
survey with a stratified random sample of airmen; focus groups with raters, ratees, and higher-level 
reviewers (HLRs); interviews with board members (e.g., promotion boards); and an analysis of 
performance evaluation data.  
Key Findings 
Redesign of the Performance Briefs 
• The inclusion of a separate block for HLR comments is helpful to members of talent management 
boards. 
• The major performance area framework provides a helpful structure for documenting 
accomplishments, but not all areas are equally relevant across ranks and jobs. 
• With the removal of the Whole Airman Concept from the enlisted performance brief (EPB), 
there is uncertainty about whether these elements still need to be documented and, if they do, 
where the documentation should be located. 
• Some board members expressed concerns that there is less information to differentiate 
performance after the removal of both the additional-rater block from the officer performance 
brief and the EPB and the overall performance assessment rating on the EPB.  
Change from Bullet Points to Narrative Writing Style 
• Overall, airmen viewed narrative statements as providing clearer information on performance and 
potential than bulleted text does.

• However, raters and ratees expressed concerns that the use of complete sentences and the 
reduction of acronyms and other abbreviations do not always provide space to fully highlight 
accomplishments. 
Static Closeout Dates and Changes to Stratification Policies 
• The introduction of static closeout dates for officer stratifications is viewed as reducing 
gamesmanship, but HLRs and ratees noted some concerns about their timing. 
• There is confusion about and a lack of compliance with the new officer stratification rules and the 
percentage of officers who should receive a primary stratification (e.g., top 10 percent or top 50 
percent). 
• Airmen have generally positive views regarding the increase of senior noncommissioned officer 
stratifications, but data show that HLRs have similar compliance issues to those they have with 
officer stratifications.  
Airman Leadership Quality–Based Performance Feedback and Proficiency 
Ratings 
• Although airman leadership qualities (ALQs) are viewed as providing a good framework for 
feedback, many ratees reported not receiving feedback or proficiency ratings. 
• Although most raters indicated that they feel they can provide honest ALQ proficiency ratings, 
many noted that there is still a culture that avoids giving negative feedback. 
Implementation of myEval 2.0 
• Airmen generally had positive perceptions regarding the overall functionality of the new system, 
but some challenges remain, including difficulties with routing, tracking the status of evaluations, 
and general usability. 
Recommendations 
• Provide additional guidance about documenting accomplishments across major performance areas 
and where and when to include accomplishments related to the Whole Airman Concept. 
• Continue to monitor the impact of eliminating the additional-rater block on board reviews. 
• Explore ways to incorporate more quantitative information into the EPB that can help talent 
management boards differentiate among enlisted airmen.  
• Provide additional examples of expected sentence structure and how to write concisely and clearly 
when documenting accomplishments. 
• Consider expanding the approved list of acronyms and other abbreviations to include additional 
commonly understood acronyms and other abbreviations across the USAF.  
• Review the alignment of talent management board timing and static closeout dates.

vii 
• Develop clearer guidance on stratification policies and explore ways to incorporate policy 
requirements into the myEval system to address confusion and compliance.  
• Provide additional guidance on how deeply to stratify officers. 
• Incorporate initial and midterm feedback into the myEval system to provide more accountability; 
ensure that ratees have access to their proficiency ratings during the evaluation period after they 
sign their annual performance brief. 
• Continue to monitor whether raters are completing the ALQ proficiency ratings and the extent to 
which airmen are receiving constructive feedback. 
• Assess the feasibility of addressing common user difficulties to improve the overall functionality of 
myEval and to improve policy compliance.

Chapter 1 
Introduction 
According to Air Force Instruction (AFI) 36-2406, there are three overall goals of the officer and 
enlisted performance evaluation systems (Department of the Air Force [DAF], 2024b).2 First, the 
systems are intended to “effectively communicate performance standards and expectations and provide 
meaningful feedback on how those standards and expectations are being upheld.” Second, they are 
designed to “establish a reliable, long-term, cumulative record of performance and promotion potential 
based on that performance.” And third, they are designed to “provide sound information to assist in 
making talent management decisions” (DAF, 2024b, p. 9).  
There are two key components of the U.S. Air Force’s (USAF’s) evaluation systems: 
• The annual officer performance brief (OPB) and enlisted performance brief (EPB)—
previously known as the officer performance report (OPR) and enlisted performance report 
(EPR)—are designed to document past performance and future potential of airmen other 
than general officers.3 Performance briefs are intended to provide key information for 
promotion boards and other talent management decisions (e.g., selection for command, 
professional military education, and other assignments). 
• Periodic performance feedback assessments provide developmental performance feedback to 
airmen and are not part of the official record used in talent management decisions. According to 
AFI 36-2406, this assessment is designed as a “formal, two-way communication between a rater 
and ratee to discuss standards, responsibilities, expectations, and goals” (DAF, 2024b, p. 53). 
Performance feedback is intended to be provided during an initial meeting, during a midterm 
review, and at the end of a reporting period. 
Apart from minor alterations in the structure and governance of USAF evaluations, the system of 
documenting performance and providing feedback to airmen has not changed in decades.4 This 
predictable process helped airmen make career decisions with an understanding of how their 
performance was likely to be evaluated and how the promotion system would treat them in the future 
based on those evaluations. However, the USAF’s officer and enlisted performance evaluation systems 
have not been without criticism. Concerns have included a lack of clear statements on past 
performance and future potential (e.g., the overuse of acronyms and other abbreviations that limit

In 2023, the USAF implemented several changes to its performance evaluation systems to address

policies for officers and senior noncommissioned officers (SNCOs)7 
• the implementation of airman leadership quality (ALQ)–based performance feedback and 
associated proficiency ratings 
• the establishment of an electronically based evaluation system known as myEval 2.0.

Project Objective and Approach 
In fall 2023, the USAF asked RAND Project AIR FORCE to conduct an evaluation to ensure 
that the changes the USAF made to its evaluation systems were achieving their intended effects. This 
project proceeded through two phases. First, we developed an evaluation framework and associated 
metrics for measuring expected outcomes associated with the specific changes. Then, we executed the 
evaluation plan and collected data to measure key outcomes. The evaluation and data collection for 
this effort focused on Regular component Airmen. 
Phase 1: Development of an Evaluation Framework  
As a first step toward an evaluation framework, we developed a logic model, which describes what 
a program is expected to accomplish and how it is expected to do so (Savitz, Matthews, and Weilant, 
2017). This framework includes identifying the resources (which encompass the various inputs that an 
organization can devote to the program), activities (in this case, the changes being made to the 
performance evaluation systems), outputs (which are observable during or very soon after the 
completion of activities), and outcomes (such as specific changes to behaviors, knowledge, and skills of 
participants) that are expected as a result of the activities (see W. K. Kellogg Foundation, 2004). 
Outcomes can be further divided into short-, medium-, and long-term time frames.8  
To inform the logic model, we reviewed documentation on planned changes to the system, held 
discussions with key stakeholders that use performance evaluation reports for talent management 
 
8 Short-term outcomes generally encompass the first one to three years after a program begins, medium-term outcomes

Phase 2: Assessment of Key Outputs and Outcomes 
Following the development of the evaluation framework, the USAF asked RAND Project Air

Survey 
From May through June 2024, we conducted a survey with a stratified random sample of airmen 
who participated in a recent officer or enlisted evaluation cycle as a ratee, rater, HLR, nonsignatory 
reviewer (NSR)9 or in an administrative role. Out of 51,658 airmen invited for voluntary participation 
in the survey, a total of 10,216 airmen responded to questions for at least one role in the survey. If a 
respondent held multiple roles in recent evaluation cycles (e.g., ratee and rater), they were asked to 
answer questions for each of those roles. The final overall response rate (inclusive of partially 
completed surveys) was 20 percent.  
Throughout this report, when describing survey findings, we report weighted survey results that 
are intended to be representative of the target population.10 In addition, to understand how precise our 
results are, we calculated an overall margin of error (MOE) for each role (Table 1.2).11

Chapter 2 
Redesign of the Performance Briefs 
In this chapter, we discuss changes reflected in the redesign of the performance brief (previously 
referred to as the performance report). These changes were intended to enhance the structure, content, 
and format of the brief that is used to assess airmen’s performance. Specifically, we discuss the 
inclusion of the MPA framework, the elimination of the additional-rater block, the removal of the 
overall performance assessment rating and WAC block on the EPB, and the inclusion of a separate 
HLR assessment block. We then describe results regarding whether the redesign of the performance 
brief affected the amount of information provided, the overall clarity of the information provided, and 
the ability to differentiate outstanding and average performance.  
Inclusion of the Major Performance Area Framework 
In February 2021, the USAF introduced ten ALQs to highlight key performance characteristics 
that airmen need to be successful. These ALQs underlie the four MPAs: executing the mission, 
leading people, managing resources, and improving the unit.12 Together, the ALQs and associated 
MPAs are intended to “represent the performance characteristics [the USAF wants] to define, 
develop, incentivize, and measure in our Airmen” (DAF, 2023). One major reform to the performance 
brief is the use of the MPAs as the framework for documenting accomplishments; raters provide an 
overall narrative evaluation for each of the four MPAs.13 The goal of using the MPA framework is to 
better communicate and hold airmen accountable for desired behaviors and expected performance 
standards aligned with USAF values.

Elimination of the Additional-Rater Block  
Another change to the performance brief was a reduction in the number of raters who sign it—
from three to only two raters. At a traditional wing, the additional rater for an officer was likely the 
squadron commander or the group commander. For majors and below, the senior rater was the wing 
commander. And for lieutenant colonels and above, the senior rater was the first general officer in the 
chain of command—typically a numbered USAF commander. For the new officer and enlisted 
evaluations, just two signatures are part of the performance briefs—the rater and the HLR—because 
the additional rater was eliminated. The HLR for officers at or below wing level was designated as the 
wing commander,15 and the HLR and senior-rater policy for enlisted members did not change. The 
Air Force's goal of eliminating the additional-rater block was to ensure that the raters in the rating 
chain have direct knowledge of the ratee’s performance and to provide parity with the other services. 
As a result, this should provide clearer information on performance.

would have signed OPRs and EPRs as the additional rater under the previous evaluation system were 
asked about the extent to which they preferred the previous evaluation and whether they believed that 
their signature would have positively affected their airmen on the new OPB and EPB. Figure 2.2 
shows that almost one-half of NSRs preferred having the additional-rater block, and around 40 
percent felt that their signatures would have positively affected their airmen on a future board.

Removal of the Overall Performance Assessment Rating on the 
Enlisted Performance Brief 
Because of past concerns from talent management stakeholders regarding rater inflation, as part of 
the EPB redesign, the USAF removed the overall performance assessment rating for enlisted 
members. The overall performance assessment previously asked raters to provide a comprehensive 
assessment of whether a ratee met performance expectations; response options included “not rated,” 
“met some but not all expectations,” “met all expectations,” “exceeded some, but not all expectations,” 
and “exceeded most, if not all expectations.” According to talent management stakeholders, most 
airmen were rated at the top end of the scale. With the removal of the overall performance assessment 
rating, the new EPB is now more consistent with the OPB, which does not contain such a rating.

Chapter 3 
Change from Bullet Points to 
Narrative Statements 
Another key change that the USAF implemented is how performance evaluations are written. 
Prior to 2023, raters used bullet points to describe the past performance and future potential of a 
ratee. According to interviews with key talent management stakeholders, one of the main criticisms of 
these bulleted statements was that they were often full of acronyms, other abbreviations, and 
terminology specific to a functional community. This made OPRs and EPRs difficult to understand 
for anyone outside that functional community (e.g., during board reviews) and particularly opaque to 
anyone outside the USAF, such as members of the joint force community who rate airmen in joint 
positions or who might review airmen’s records when selecting them for joint positions. To address 
these concerns, the new OPB and EPB require raters to write in full narrative sentences with few or no 
acronyms or abbreviations (DAF, 2024b, p. 10). This change is meant to improve the clarity and 
accuracy of performance statements so ratees better understand their performance and potential. This 
should ultimately also help increase satisfaction with the evaluations and perceptions of their 
usefulness. Additionally, providing clearer information on performance and potential is intended to 
make OPBs and EPBs more useful to talent management boards and the joint force community.

Use of Acronyms and Other Abbreviations 
As noted in the previous sections, airmen are concerned about space limitations with the new 
narrative writing style, especially with the new policy that limits the number of allowable acronyms 
and other abbreviations. Specifically, the USAF limited use of acronyms and other abbreviations in 
the OPB and EPB to support the narrative-style writing with “plain language words to communicate 
performance and achieve greater clarity to the broadest audience” (Air Force’s Personnel Center 
[AFPC], undated). The original list included 103 approved acronyms and other abbreviations that 
had broad understanding across the service and five general categories of common acronyms and other 
abbreviations (e.g., ranks and tiers, office symbols, organizations at and above squadron level, weapons 
and platforms, and symbols and measurements). In 2023 and 2024, users were able to submit 
recommendations for new acronyms and other abbreviations to be added to the approved list. 
Evaluation Data Insights 
As noted in our focus group discussions and in write-in comments on the survey, airmen are 
concerned about the space limitations that accompany the restricted use of acronyms and other 
abbreviations. To assess the extent to which acronyms and other abbreviations were still being used 
within the performance evaluations, we reviewed two sets of data. First, we reviewed the list of user 
submissions for approval consideration. Most of the acronyms and other abbreviations that appeared 
in these 4,500 submissions fell into one of two categories: (1) functional acronyms and other 
abbreviations and (2) specific acronyms and other abbreviations that were already included in the five 
general categories of approved acronyms and other abbreviations.26 Second, we reviewed myEval data 
for completed evaluations as of October 31, 2024, to identify potential acronym and abbreviation use 
in the MPA and HLR blocks across completed evaluations. Overall, we found 12,540 acronyms and 
other abbreviations used in the evaluations that had not been previously approved. Many acronyms 
and other abbreviations were functional-specific. However, some are widely known (e.g., LIMFAC 
[limiting factor ], SCOD, TASKORD [task order]). The full list of acronyms and other abbreviations 
was provided to the Force Management Policy Directorate (USAF/A1P) in January 2025 for future 
consideration.

Included in the recent changes to the performance evaluation system were the introduction of 
SCODs for officers and changes to the stratification policy for both officers and SNCOs.27 In this 
chapter, we describe each of these changes, along with findings from our survey, the focus groups and 
interviews, and analysis of relevant evaluation data.  
Officer Static Closeout Dates  
The USAF introduced SCODs for officers in 2022 to increase the accuracy and fairness of 
stratifications by ensuring that all officers of a particular rank are compared at the same time (DAF, 
2024b). Stratifications are a measure of performance and potential in which officers are rated in 
comparison with their peers (e.g., “#1/12 Capts” would be a stratification indicating that an officer is 
the highest performer of 12 peer captains). Aligning stratifications with a SCOD is intended to end 
the prior system that allowed raters to compare different officers at different points in time. The 
USAF’s goal is to prevent the gamesmanship that occurred when SCODs did not exist and raters 
would often repeat high stratifications throughout the year (i.e., put different officers as their number 
one at different times of year). This change is expected to provide more-accurate information on 
performance and potential by helping officers better understand how they compare to their peers. 
Similarly, the change to SCODs should also provide future boards with more-accurate comparisons to 
help them better distinguish top performers.

HLRs indicated that the SCODs did not make it more difficult to accurately stratify officers, and a 
sizable majority of raters agree that the SCODs allowed enough time with their ratees to accurately 
assess their performance.

Changes to Officer Stratification Policy 
The USAF also implemented new stratification policies for officers with more rules and 
exceptions than in the past. The basic rules for officer stratifications involve the use of primary and 
secondary stratifications. Specifically, the officer must first be stratified within their grade group—
referred to as the primary stratification. HLRs determine the percentage of officers given primary 
stratifications. Once a primary stratification is issued, the rater or HLR can then choose to also 
provide a secondary stratification, in which the officer is compared with members of a peer group, 
such as flight commanders. The secondary stratification allows the rater or HLR to demonstrate how

37 
that officer excels outside their grade group. A secondary stratification can be given only if an officer 
has first received a primary stratification.  
In addition to the basic rules for primary and secondary stratifications, the new policies provide 
rules and exceptions for who can be included in the denominator for the primary stratification and 
exceptions for groups that cannot receive a primary stratification, such as promotion selects or ratees 
who have a rater who is the same grade.29 Other new rules address such issues as veiled stratifications 
that do not comply with rules, quoted stratifications, and stratifications based on awards or 
development team rankings.

Underlying the MPAs are ten ALQs designed to highlight key leadership qualities airmen need to 
be successful.39 Although the performance brief was redesigned around the MPA framework, the USAF 
also developed a new ALQ-based developmental feedback form designed to complement the previous 
ACA framework. The USAF’s goals in terms of basing developmental feedback on the ALQs are to 
better communicate expected behaviors and performance standards that align with USAF values and to 
provide feedback on strengths, weaknesses, and key areas of improvement. This new approach to 
developmental feedback asks raters to provide a proficiency-level rating for each ALQ on a five-point 
scale ranging from “needs improvement” (1) to “exceptionally skilled” (5).40 As of this writing, ALQ 
proficiency ratings have been incorporated into the end-of–performance period evaluation as part of the 
OPB and EPB in myEval 2.0. As part of completing the OPB and EPB in the myEval 2.0 system, raters 
are asked to provide proficiency ratings on each ALQ. However, these proficiency ratings are not 
included as part of the official performance brief used for talent management decisions, such as 
promotion boards. Separating ALQ-based feedback from the official record is intended to help raters 
feel that they can be candid without jeopardizing an airman’s future career.

Challenges Routing and Tracking Evaluation Status  
Many write-in survey comments focused on challenges with routing and tracking evaluations in 
myEval 2.0. Comments in this category focused primarily on concerns from supervisors and 
administrative personnel being able to see the evaluations assigned to them or their unit during the 
evaluation draft and/or submission process. Of particular concern was that once an evaluation is 
finalized and sent to the AFPC as a final record, it disappears from the myEval system and does not 
reappear until it is loaded in the Personnel Records Display Application. Other comments included 
commanders and senior enlisted leaders not being able to see all the evaluations assigned to their units, 
which was a feature of the USAF’s previous evaluation system, vPC.   
In our focus groups, raters, ratees, and HLRs voiced similar concerns about routing and tracking 
the status of evaluations. For example, one rater explained, “If I can see who is sitting on [the 
evaluation form] so I can ask them why . . . that would be awesome.” Ratees concurred and expressed a 
desire to track their evaluations’ status, saying, “I used to be able to see where my OPR was at. Now I 
don’t know who it’s with or how long it’s been with anybody.” One HLR added, “I am very frustrated 
I can’t establish coordination chains in myEval,” noting that there is no function to manage who 
reviews and signs performance briefs.  
Although our focus group respondents suggested that there were challenges with myEval 2.0, the 
system does have the benefit of allowing them to process performance evaluation forms electronically. 
According to an individual whom we interviewed from the joint force community, this is not the case 
in some other services. This respondent explained, “The system is good. I had no problems with the 
electric files access. . . . The Navy is hard copy, so you know we have tried the electronic fitness report, 
and it didn’t work, so we went back to hard copy.”  
Challenges Accessing and Tracking Feedback 
HLRs and ratees expressed a desire to track and view changes or edits that had been made to the 
OPB and EPB. For example, one HLR commented, “There is no Track Changes function [in myEval 
2.0],” highlighting the inability to see how an OPB or EPB changes throughout the review process. 
One ratee agreed, saying, “I would love to see tracking of the changes. If I send up a product and it 
comes back completely differently, I don’t know who made the changes or why.”  
User Interface and System Challenges 
 Other comments about using myEval 2.0 focused on more-technical aspects of the system, 
including the extent to which the interface was user friendly. We heard similar comments in our focus 
groups and in the survey write-in comments. These included difficulties navigating the myEval 2.0 
website, including issues with the display font size and having to open multiple drop-downs to see all 
the information in an evaluation. In addition, users voiced difficulties with account creation and 
permission access, an inability to remove documents once attached, a lack of prepopulated standard 
data (e.g., name, unit of assignment), the creation of multiple evaluation sites for the same airman 
during the same cycle, and the lack of an autosave function.

Chapter 7 
Summary of Key Findings and 
Recommendations 
In this chapter, we summarize key findings related to the outputs and outcomes associated with 
each of the primary changes to the enlisted and officer performance evaluation systems. We also 
provide recommendations for addressing any identified shortcomings or gaps in which the intended 
outputs or outcomes were not fully met.   
Redesign of the Performance Briefs 
To enhance the type and clarity of information provided and improve the ability to differentiate 
among outstanding, average, and lower performers, the USAF made several changes to the structure 
and content of the performance briefs that are used to assess airmen’s performance and potential. 
After analyzing these changes, we found the following: 
• Inclusion of the MPA framework: The MPA framework was generally viewed as a useful 
structure for communicating expected performance standards and documenting performance 
aligned with USAF values. However, boards often viewed this information as less crucial  to 
their decisions unless the associated narratives were needed to differentiate among comparable 
ratees. Despite its general usefulness, airmen noted several limitations with the framework, 
such as the concern that not all MPAs are relevant across all pay grades (particularly for junior 
airmen) and for all job duties.  
• Elimination of the additional-rater block: Close to one-half of NSRs who would have signed 
performance briefs as the additional rater preferred having the additional-rater block and 
thought their signature would have positively affected their airmen on a future board. Most 
board members reported feeling that the change did not matter because they focused primarily 
on the HLR block. However, some board members reported feeling that this change removed 
potential information that could help differentiate airmen.49 We note that although NSRs, who 
were previously additional raters, no longer sign airmen’s performance briefs, they are still able to 
provide input to both HLRs and raters, which could be included in final briefs in some form. 
 
49 During this study, the USAF also implemented a policy change regarding the authorized HLR for lieutenant and 
captain OPBs, which should provide more junior-officer ratees an opportunity for an HLR stratification. These 
changes might address some of the concerns that we noted regarding a loss of information with the reduction in raters.

66 
• Removal of the EPB overall performance assessment rating: Perceptions regarding the removal of 
the overall performance assessment rating were mixed, although slightly larger percentages of 
HLRs and raters preferred including the overall performance assessment rating. Some raters 
and board members expressed concern that, although the ratings were generally inflated, 
markdowns were helpful to differentiate lower performers. Board members expressed a desire 
for more quantitative information on the EPB, and data show that the current EPB construct 
might not provide adequate quantitative data to help promotion board members differentiate 
performance and potential for all specialties.  
• Removal of the WAC: A majority of ratees indicated that WAC accomplishments should not 
be included in the EPB; focus group participants indicated that its removal helps focus the 
EPB on documenting job accomplishments. However, there appeared to be confusion about 
whether aspects of the WAC are still appropriate for inclusion in other parts of the EPB and, 
if included, in which section they should be documented.  
• Inclusion of an HLR assessment block: The inclusion of a separate HLR block was seen as a 
positive change both by HLRs and board members; board members noted that it is one of the 
most important sections that they review. However, board members also noted variation in 
the quality of the information provided in terms of its usefulness in differentiating airmen. 
Recommendations 
Many of the changes made to the performance brief were viewed positively by airmen, but there 
are several areas in which further guidance could be useful. They are as follows: 
• Provide more guidance and examples for what types of accomplishments (depending on pay 
grade and job duties) should be documented on each of the MPAs and the HLR assessment 
block. As of this writing, the USAF provided a knowledge article in the my Force Support 
Squadron (myFSS) IT platform titled “ALQ Writing Guide.” This guide could be expanded to 
include a grade-by-grade list of examples for each MPA. Functional communities could also 
develop their own examples to better support documenting accomplishments for their members.  
• Consider providing greater flexibility regarding the MPAs that raters may emphasize when 
documenting accomplishments. For example, provide a total character limit across all MPAs 
rather than a limit for each MPA so raters can document more accomplishments in one area 
than another, depending on what might be most relevant to the pay grade and job duties.  
• Continue to monitor the impact of eliminating the additional-rater block. Given concerns 
about the impact that eliminating the additional-rater block could have on board reviews, we 
recommend continuing to collect data from board members about their ability to differentiate 
performance and potential without the inclusion of additional rater comments. The USAF 
should also include guidance in the ALQ writing guide indicating that NSRs can still play an 
important role in reviewing the evaluation and providing needed or missing career impact 
information for HLRs to consider.  
• Explore ways to incorporate more quantitative information into the EPB that can help 
differentiate enlisted airmen for talent management boards. It was beyond the scope of this

67 
study to fully assess potential options, but the USAF could consider further changes to 
stratification policy or look to the practices of other services that incorporate more 
quantitative information in their performance evaluations.50 Any option would need to be fully 
assessed prior to implementation to ensure that it would address existing concerns (e.g., not 
result in rater inflation like the performance report did) and not have unintended 
consequences.   
• Provide guidance on where and when to include accomplishments related to the WAC in 
EPBs to eliminate confusion in the field. The USAF should determine whether WAC 
accomplishments are still important to document in some capacity and communicate the 
desire to include or exclude this information. Guidance related to its inclusion could be 
incorporated into the existing ALQ writing guide.  
Bullet Points to Narrative Performance Statements 
Another key change that the USAF implemented was requiring raters to write their evaluations 
using clear narrative sentences with minimal acronyms and other abbreviations instead of the previous 
bullet-point style. This change was intended to improve clarity and provide more-accurate and more-
understandable information on performance and potential. In addition, having clearer information is 
intended to make the performance briefs more useful to boards by making it easier to distinguish top 
performers. After analyzing these changes, we found the following: 
• Clarity of narrative statements: Overall, airmen viewed narrative statements as providing clearer 
information. Board members, in particular, noted that the decreased use of acronyms and 
other abbreviations is helpful when reviewing records across career fields. 
• Concerns about space limitations: Airmen shared concerns that the requirement of using 
complete sentences (that include a subject, such as rank and name) and the reduction of 
acronyms and other abbreviations do not allow enough space to fully highlight 
accomplishments within the character limits. 
• Differences in writing ability and quality: Airmen stated that the new writing style can be 
challenging, and board members noted that the quality of writing and information provided 
varies. 
• Use of acronyms and other abbreviations: Evaluation data show that acronyms and other 
abbreviations beyond the authorized list are still being used, although to a much lesser extent; 
some airmen expressed a desire for the approved acronym and abbreviation list to be expanded 
to include additional common terms. 
• Communication and understanding: Survey findings were mixed as to whether the change 
helped with raters’ communication and ratees’ understanding of performance and potential. 
 
50 An example of a change in stratification policy might be changing SNCO stratification policy to allow all HLRs of 
enlisted personnel to provide a “top 25 percent” indicator for their promotion-eligible SNCOs while continuing the 
existing practice of allowing the senior rater to stratify the top 25 percent within their rating scope of responsibility.

68 
Recommendations 
Although the change from bullet points to narrative statements was generally viewed positively, 
our findings show that there are additional actions that have the potential to further improve clarity 
and understanding. They are as follows: 
• Provide additional examples on expected sentence structure and how to write concisely and 
clearly when documenting accomplishments (e.g., standardizing the use of initials when 
referring to the ratee instead of writing out full name and rank). This should be included as 
part of the ALQ writing guide and could be provided within hover text in myEval for an easily 
accessible resource. Additionally, the USAF could explore using automation to help review 
rater submissions to check for compliance and appropriate structure. We acknowledge 
concerns about being overly prescriptive in sentence structure and examples, which could 
result in evaluations looking too similar. However, there appears to be a desire and need for 
additional writing guidance to lessen the degree that writing quality influences board reviews. 
• Consider expanding the acronym and abbreviation list to include additional commonly 
understood acronyms and other abbreviations used across the USAF. (We note that the 
USAF already has allowed airmen to submit acronyms and other abbreviations for potential 
inclusion.) Our data analysis also identified commonly used unauthorized acronyms and other 
abbreviations, many of which were function-specific. However, some are widely known (e.g., 
LIMFAC, SCOD, TASKORD). 
Introduction of Officer Static Closeout Dates and Changes in 
Stratification Policies 
To increase accuracy, fairness, and the ability to distinguish top from average performers, the 
USAF implemented SCODs for officers and made changes to its stratification policies for both 
officers and SNCOs. After analyzing these changes, we found the following: 
• Implementation of SCODs: Airmen viewed the implementation of SCODs positively and 
believe that they facilitate fairer and more-accurate stratifications. However, HLRs and ratees 
shared concerns about how SCOD timing might affect talent management boards. Some 
ratees also expressed concerns about SCODs and PCS timing, including confusion about 
which unit would provide the evaluation. 
• Changes to officer stratification policy: Evaluation data show that not all HLRs are following 
new officer stratification rules in alignment with policy. Some of this appears to be because 
of confusion about the new rules. There was also a perception by some raters and HLRs that 
the new rules could disadvantage some officers. Additionally, there was uncertainty 
regarding the percentage of officers that should receive primary stratifications (e.g., top 10 
percent or top 50 percent). 
• Changes to SNCO stratification policy: Airmen had generally positive views regarding the 
increase of SNCO stratifications up to 25 percent, and HLRs indicated that it will have a 
positive impact and will make it easier to recognize top performers. However, data show that

69 
HLRs have similar accuracy issues for SNCO stratifications to those they have with officer 
stratifications. 
Recommendations 
The following policy and process improvements should be considered to increase the likelihood 
that SCOD and stratification changes will have their intended impact: 
• Develop clearer guidance on stratification policies (e.g., a decision tree) and explore ways to 
incorporate policy requirements into the myEval system to help address confusion and 
compliance. We provide recommendations at the end of Appendix A for potential technical 
changes related to entering stratifications.  
• Provide additional guidance on how deeply to stratify officers. We note that some board 
members recommended stratifying up to 50 percent for large pools of officers and up to 100 
percent for small pools of officers. According to our interviews, deeper stratifications provide 
more quantitative information to board members, allowing them to better differentiate 
performance and potential. 
• Review the alignment of talent management board timing with SCODs to ensure that board 
processes account for recent performance when large time gaps exist between SCODs and 
individual boards. We also recommend reviewing and communicating policy about SCOD 
timing and undergoing a PCS to help clarify procedures about airmen’s responsibility for 
completing performance briefs so these airmen do not feel they will be disadvantaged during 
the process. 
Implementation of Airman Leadership Quality–Based Feedback 
and Proficiency Ratings 
Along with changes to the performance briefs, the USAF refocused the developmental feedback 
provided to airmen on ten ALQs that underlie the MPAs. The goal of this change was to better 
communicate expected behaviors and performance standards that align with USAF values and to 
provide feedback on strengths, weaknesses, and key areas of improvement. After analyzing these 
changes, we found the following: 
• Feedback framework and consistency: Although the ALQs were viewed as providing a good 
framework for feedback, many ratees reported not receiving initial or midterm feedback. 
Furthermore, data show that proficiency ratings that coincide with end-of-year EPBs and 
OPBs are not always provided. 
• Proficiency ratings: Some ratees reported not being able to view their proficiency feedback 
ratings after they signed their performance briefs.  
• Ability to provide constructive and honest feedback: Although most raters who took the survey 
indicated that they felt they could provide honest ALQ proficiency ratings, more than 40 
percent were still neutral or disagreed. Focus group participants also noted that there is still a 
USAF culture that avoids giving negative feedback. Our analysis shows that proficiency rating

70 
variance decreases at higher ranks. Although this might not be surprising because only higher-
performing airmen are selected for promotion to higher grades, the USAF wants to ensure 
that constructive feedback is provided at all levels.  
Recommendations 
Although the ALQ-based feedback framework was viewed positively, our analysis shows that 
airmen are not always receiving constructive feedback. We recommend the following opportunities for 
improvement to help address this gap: 
• Incorporate initial and midterm feedback into the myEval 2.0 system to ensure that there is 
rater accountability for providing feedback. As of this writing, our understanding is that the 
USAF has plans to address this. 
• Ensure that ratees can see their proficiency ratings even after the performance brief is signed 
by the ratee. Additionally, include an optional narrative comment block to go with the 
feedback ratings that is not part of the final performance brief and is still visible only to the 
ratee and rater. This can help facilitate more-constructive feedback to ratees.  
• Continue to monitor whether raters are completing the ALQ proficiency ratings and the 
extent to which airmen perceive that they are receiving constructive feedback (e.g., survey 
items could be incorporated in existing force-wide surveys or ratees could answer a question 
on receiving feedback as part of their evaluation sign-off). The USAF should also strengthen 
communication about the purpose of the proficiency ratings, reinforce that they do not 
become part of an airman’s record, and emphasize the importance of providing constructive 
feedback to airmen. 
Implementation of myEval 2.0  
The myEval 2.0 IT system is intended to provide users with easy access and minimal disruptions 
to the evaluation forms, and the overall goal is to improve efficiency and satisfaction with the system. 
In analyzing perceptions of the system change, we found the following: 
• Functionality: Airmen generally had positive perceptions regarding the overall functionality of 
the system (e.g., ability to access it, minimal disruptions, and ease of use).  
• Efficiency: However, survey results were mixed regarding whether myEval 2.0 provided a 
more-efficient platform for raters and HLRs to prepare their evaluations than the previous 
system did. 
• Challenges: Despite generally positive perceptions of the new system, airmen also identified 
some existing usability challenges. These included difficulties viewing the status or location of 
evaluations, correcting standard information, seeing edits, and navigating the website, as well 
as word-spacing and routing issues.

71 
Recommendations 
Although perceptions of the myEval 2.0 system were generally positive, some technical challenges 
remain. This is not surprising given that the system was designed to meet immediate needs, with a 
goal for further improvement in the future. To help address challenges related to myEval 2.0, we 
recommend the following broad steps (and we provide a longer list of more-specific actionable 
recommendations in Appendix A): 
• Assess the feasibility of addressing key technical issues identified by airmen (e.g., the ability to 
track evaluations, an autosave function, the usability of the interface) to enhance myEval 2.0’s 
overall functionality. 
• Assess the feasibility of incorporating changes to the system to help provide additional policy 
compliance (e.g., change grade block for ratees to a drop-down menu of appropriate grades, 
break out stratification inputs into separate blocks that enforce existing policies). 
• Provide more-robust myEval 2.0 educational materials tiered to ratees, raters, unit leaders, 
and administrative personnel at all levels. 
Conclusion 
Overall, many of the changes that were made to the enlisted and officer performance evaluation 
systems are viewed positively by airmen. However, our analyses revealed some gaps in fully meeting 
intended outcomes. We recommend additional areas of guidance and assessment to further ensure 
that the changes are having their intended impact. In addition, given that the changes were recent at 
the time of this assessment, we recommend that the USAF continue to periodically evaluate the 
performance evaluation system changes in line with the logic model that we developed (see the annex). 
This will help to ensure that longer-term outcomes are achieved and can help facilitate continuous 
process improvement.

Appendix A 
myEval 2.0 Technical Issues and 
Recommendations 
Our survey asked respondents about the implementation of the myEval 2.0 IT system for 
evaluations, including any technical difficulties they had accessing or using the system. Respondents 
were able to comment on difficulties in the section on myEval and in general comments at the end of 
the survey. We detail airmen’s perspectives on myEval 2.0 in Chapter 6. This appendix summarizes 
comments that survey respondents provided and outlines detailed, actionable recommendations that 
the USAF could consider to improve user interaction with myEval during and after evaluation cycles. 
Respondents (N = 2,916) provided 3,178 comments that we organized into four categories: 
• Administrative technical issues: This category includes 1,153 comments on issues that likely 
have administrative solutions, such as better training materials or changes to personnel access 
and permissions within the myEval system. 
• Infrastructure technical issues: This category includes 234 comments on issues related to the 
reliability of myEval for users in the USAF and in the other services or other government 
agencies.  
• Software and programming issues: This category includes 1,312 comments related to issues 
regarding testing and programming of the software of the myEval system. Comments often 
cited such issues as the lack of an autosave feature or duplicative evaluations created for airmen 
during a single cycle. 
• Crosscutting issues: This category includes 479 comments, which spanned at least two of the 
prior categories, such as when identification information for individual airmen (e.g., name, 
rank, and unit) fails to prepopulate in the approved format. This could be because of 
infrastructure incompatibility issues wherein some IT systems might be unable to adequately 
communicate with myEval. 
Administrative Technical Issues 
Nearly one-half (556) of the comments that we categorized as administrative addressed the lack of 
ability for an individual user to track evaluations not assigned to them. One write-in comment read, “I 
would like to see all who have reviewed before me.” Others noted that they could not see changes made 
by other users and that “supporting documents would disappear when assigning [a performance brief] to 
a new user in routing.” Perhaps most explicitly, one commenter said that there was “no visibility of who 
previously reviewed or coordinated on evaluations.” As already noted in Chapter 6, airmen reported that 
they were unable to track their evaluations or see to whom within the rating chains their evaluations

73 
were assigned. These users also noted an inability to view an evaluation once it was signed unless they 
saved a separate PDF copy prior to submission. Furthermore, leaders in large organizations with 
multiple units (e.g., wings) expressed frustration with being able to access only the evaluations for those 
airmen assigned to the leader’s unit and not the subordinate units (e.g., groups or squadrons).  
Another common theme (225 comments) in this category came from airmen who reported not 
using myEval 2.0 to complete their evaluations. Many respondents indicated that they used locally 
created PDF documents that were like the myEval interface to create draft evaluations and share them 
with their supervisors before submitting the final product in myEval. Other respondents indicated that, 
in their units, airmen recorded their evaluation comments in several formats and submitted them to their 
CSS and that the airmen assigned to the CSS would input all comments into the myEval system. In 
some cases, a respondent would indicate that their entire unit was not using the system because of 
various difficulties.  
Additionally, there were 70 comments related to a lack of training or guidance on using the 
myEval 2.0 system. These varied from general comments about not receiving training or materials 
explaining the platform or overall process to specific concerns, such as understanding performance 
brief routing or the different requirements for filling out the online evaluation. Some respondents 
mentioned wanting a “how-to guide” or a frequently answered questions page that they could refer to 
for help. There were also 83 comments about how certain information that was required to complete 
evaluations was not immediately apparent. 
Recommendations 
Using insights from these write-in comments and findings from our focus groups (as discussed in 
Chapter 6), we recommend the following improvements that the USAF should consider to address 
these concerns: 
• Provide a feature for individual users to track evaluations. This tracking feature should (1) 
allow airmen to see where an evaluation is in the routing process and who it is assigned to in 
the rating chain, (2) allow users to save a copy of a draft evaluation for at least 30 days after the 
evaluation is submitted, (3) prompt an airman to save a copy of their draft evaluation for their 
personal records after they sign their finalized evaluation, and (4) allow airmen in designated 
leadership positions to view all evaluations assigned to their personnel accounting symbol code 
and any subordinate personnel accounting symbol codes. 
• Seek to further understand the number of airmen who use administrative work-arounds to 
avoid myEval and the reasons airmen prefer to use these work-arounds. This research could be 
conducted via focus groups during future evaluations, and it could help the USAF understand 
what further system enhancements will improve user satisfaction. 
• Update existing materials and provide more-robust myEval 2.0 educational materials tiered to 
ratees, raters, unit leaders, CSS, and military flight personnel. For example, this could include 
creating a frequently asked questions page for common user issues. As we note in a later 
recommendation, this could also include providing additional guidance within the myEval 
system itself (e.g., hover text).

74 
Infrastructure Technical Issues 
Of the 234 comments in this category, 67 focused on reliability issues with the myEval website. 
These respondents noted that the website was often slow to load or would only partially load, 
webpages would time out, or the entire website would freeze or crash. In a possible indication that the 
infrastructure underlying myEval might be insufficient to handle the demands placed on it, one 
respondent stated, “It shouldn’t take me nine clicks to log in to an Air Force site.” Another major area 
of concern (167 comments) had to do with myEval account access for users in other services or parts 
of government who supervised airmen.  
Recommendations 
Although there were fewer comments in this category than in the others, deficiencies in this area 
could compound frustrations with myEval. We recommend the following actions to address these 
concerns: 
• Conduct an evaluation of the systems that host the myEval website to determine whether they 
can sustain the demands placed on them by myEval users. 
• Ensure that airmen in non-USAF unit assignments understand how to assist their raters and 
HLRs when myEval technical support is required. 
Software and Programming Issues 
There were 691 comments about the myEval system not being user friendly or intuitive. We 
highlighted many of these issues in Chapter 6. For example, respondents indicated that too much 
information was “buried” in myEval drop-down menus, which made completing an evaluation more 
burdensome. Respondents also expressed a desire to view the evaluation in a style similar to its final 
format prior to signing. Many also said that the digital form they fill out should mirror the printed 
version of the form. 
The second most common area of comments in this category (233) had to do with difficulties 
tracking or editing comments by various individuals in a given rating chain. Individual users noted 
having to suggest changes in the administrative and routing comment sections. One rater commented 
that there was not “understanding that routing comments are temporary.” Others said that they were 
“not able to see comment[s] on my report” and that “the routing system is clunky. . . . myEval 2.0 
needs some sort of version control added to it. I should be able to see changes made to each version.” 
This might stem from respondents’ previous experience using vPC, which allowed users in the rating 
chain to see previous versions of an evaluation and track changes.  
In addition to these issues, respondents noted several other programming issues that could be 
improved. For example, 82 comments focused on issues with signing the evaluation in myEval. The 
majority discussed potential website malfunctions as a reason, but others noted that they could not 
remove their own signature after they had signed something in error. Several respondents also noted 
issues with email notifications (27 comments), including multiple autogenerated notification emails 
for the same evaluation, a lack of detail on what action was needed, and reminders.

75 
Recommendations 
Using insights from these write-in comments and findings from our focus groups as discussed in 
Chapter 6, we identified the following opportunities for the USAF to consider to help improve the 
myEval user experience: 
• Streamline the myEval website to allow rating information and comment boxes to display with 
fewer drop-down options. This could allow the personnel who are providing inputs to see all 
of the sections of an evaluation that they are working on. 
• Improve the digital display of the evaluation so it appears similar or identical to the final OPB 
or EPB. 
• Document and track changes made to an evaluation as it moves through the rating chain. 
• Improve the myEval auto notification system to prevent sending multiple autogenerated 
notification emails for the same evaluation and provide details that allow rating personnel the 
ability to identify (1) what evaluation is assigned to them (e.g., name, rank, and organization of 
a given evaluation) and (2) any comments left by the reviewer that indicate what actions need 
to be taken. 
• Investigate and resolve digital signature issues. 
Crosscutting Technical Issues 
The issues that we categorized here were those that fit into multiple prior categories. The most 
cited example from respondents (209 comments) was the apparent lack of an autosave function to 
prevent loss of data, especially in comment boxes. Respondents particularly noted the need for this 
function given myEval’s auto log-off feature that does not notify users of when they are in danger of 
losing access because of inactivity. This could lead to airmen being logged out of the system without 
their knowledge.  
Another common area of concern (187 comments) indicated that administrative information that 
should be prepopulated—such as name, rank, and unit of assignment—was often either wrong or 
formatted incorrectly. One respondent noted, “I’m tired of correcting information that is 
automatically filled in.” This might occur because systems (such as MilPDS) format information 
differently from myEval.  
Furthermore, respondents also said that the character limits in the MPA and HLR assessment 
blocks inhibits the drafting of performance statements (97 comments). We discuss this theme in more 
detail in Chapter 3. As of this writing, the comment boxes in myEval do not allow a user to type more 
characters than the designated limit. Multiple respondents suggested increasing the character limit 
and having myEval provide some alert when they pass that limit. Several comments indicated that 
such an alert, combined with an inability to submit a finalized OPB or EPB until the character limit 
was adhered to, would help airmen who edited their comments in myEval.

76 
Recommendations 
Consistent with the write-in comments that we reviewed and the discussion in our main report, 
we recommend the following for the USAF: 
• Introduce the following automatic features to improve the user experience: (1) an autosave 
function that saves inputs at rapid and regular intervals, (2) a warning that a user will be 
logged off after a given time of inactivity and a window displaying the inactivity timer, and (3) 
an auto-refresh function that allows a user to pick up where they left off in the event of an 
unexpected log-off. 
• Consider allowing users to input more characters into comment boxes but not submit 
finalized evaluations until they adhere to the character limit. A warning should be provided 
that informs users which comment boxes are over the limit and by how much. 
Other System Recommendations 
The following recommendations are based on talent management board and myEval data analysis 
findings. They are intended to facilitate better policy compliance and data integrity and help address 
confusion about new rules. Consistent with data that we analyzed and discussed in our main report, 
we recommend that the USAF consider making the following changes: 
• Change the grade block for ratees to a drop-down menu with the appropriate grades. The 
most common administrative error across OPBs and EPBs involved the ratee’s grade—either 
nonstandard typing or incorrect grade data based on the ratee’s status as of the SCOD. This 
change would reduce administrative errors and would allow the grade selection to be a trigger 
for other fields in myEval. 
• Break the officer stratification narrative block into subblocks using a process similar to SNCO 
stratifications. The rater or HLR should check an appropriate box in the system for 
“stratification yes or no.” A “yes” then requires the user to fill in a numerator block, followed 
by a denominator block, and both blocks should allow only numerical entries. Because only 
grade is allowed for the comparison group, then the system would autopopulate with the 
ratee’s grade. This system change would ensure that raters and HLRs cannot add 
unauthorized qualifiers. Once the primary stratification fields are complete, then the IT 
system would unlock the secondary stratification block as a shorter narrative block. IT triggers 
for joint and DAF units would also need to be built in. Improving the IT system options 
would ensure rigor in primary stratification compliance and ensure that ratees are receiving 
clear feedback. 
• Use the IT system to better allow raters and HLRs to follow policy by using data triggers 
when the following situations apply: ratee is a promotion select, rater and ratee are the same 
grade, and rater and HLR are the same person.

77 
• For SNCO stratifications, link the Management Eligibility List (MEL) data to the 
denominator data for HLRs who are senior raters and use this link to autocalculate their 
stratification limits. Also, consider building MELs for officer HLRs and providing the same 
system links to stop denominator inflation. 
• Provide better help information in the hover text within myEval. Some boxes show helpful 
examples on how to fill out administrative fields, such as signature blocks and organizational 
data. However, the narrative boxes (e.g., MPAs, HLR Assessment, Mandatory Comments) 
contain hyperlinks to AFI 36-2406 (DAF, 2024b) with no specific paragraph indication. 
Instead, we recommend providing a link to a help page maintained by AFPC for that subject. 
The help page should include the AFI paragraph reference along with examples. If the hover 
text led to useful policy information and examples specific to the block, then users might be 
more likely to complete the blocks in accordance with policy. This option would require one 
set of system updates to provide new hyperlinks, but then the information can be maintained 
outside the IT system when policy changes or user feedback needs to be incorporated.

n 2023, the U.S. Air Force (USAF) implemented several changes to the officer and 
enlisted performance evaluation systems, including a redesign of the performance 
reports and a change in the USAF’s approach to providing developmental feedback. 
The USAF asked RAND Project AIR FORCE researchers to first establish a method 
to evaluate and monitor whether the performance evaluation changes are having 
the intended effects and then to conduct the designed evaluation. The project team used 
a mixed-methods approach that included a survey with a stratified random sample of 
airmen; focus groups with raters, ratees, and higher-level reviewers (HLRs); interviews 
with board members (e.g., promotion boards); and an analysis of performance evaluation 
data. This report details their methods, findings, and recommendations.
