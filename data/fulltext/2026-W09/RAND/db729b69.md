About This Report 
In this report, we (1) present an estimation of the Space Training and Readiness Command 
(STARCOM) headquarters’ (HQ’s) current and projected staffing levels and workload, (2) introduce 
an optimization model that optimizes staffing to best support mission priorities and minimize 
personnel shortfalls, and (3) assess HQ STARCOM’s organizational alignment and structure. Using 
data reported by HQ STARCOM staff, our analysis reveals that HQ STARCOM is understaffed, 
requiring nearly double its personnel to manage its workload effectively. Simultaneously, it faces 
organizational challenges, such as unclear mission connections, structural tensions, mission-related 
risks from resource strain, and external factors affecting operations.

Summary 
Issue 
The Space Training and Readiness Command (STARCOM) is striving to improve its functional 
alignment and human resource allocation to enhance its ability to provide education, training, 
doctrine, and test capabilities for the U.S. Space Force (USSF). In line with this effort, STARCOM 
asked RAND Project AIR FORCE to help determine the manpower needs of its headquarters (HQ). 
Analysis using data provided by HQ STARCOM staff reveals that HQ STARCOM requires nearly 
double its current staff to manage its workload and, simultaneously, faces organizational challenges, 
such as unclear mission connections, structural tensions, mission-related risks from resource strain, 
and external factors affecting operations. 
Approach 
We assessed HQ STARCOM’s current and projected staffing levels by compiling staffing and 
workload estimates, evaluating mission alignment, and categorizing workload into functional areas. 
This process involved collecting quantitative information about the functions assigned and performed 
by personnel across HQ STARCOM’s directorates, divisions, and branches. Furthermore, we 
compared the functions assigned with the functions performed by HQ staff. We assessed gaps and 
challenges related to functional alignment using document analysis via large language models, 
qualitative interviewing, and benchmarking against comparable organizations. Our findings illustrate 
HQ STARCOM’s current and anticipated future workload and staffing requirements along three 
dimensions: different levels of the organization (i.e., aggregate data for branches, divisions, 
directorates, or HQ STARCOM as a whole), type of staff (i.e., officer, enlisted, civilian, or 
contractor), and organizational and staffing alignment with STARCOM’s missions. Additionally, we 
present organizational findings and recommendations aimed at enhancing functional alignment. 
Key Findings 
Manpower analysis shows that HQ STARCOM would need nearly double its existing personnel 
to fully complete its reported current workload. Additionally, HQ STARCOM faces several 
organizational challenges, including the following: 
• Staffing gaps exist in virtually every directorate. There is more reported workload than 
personnel to conduct the workload. Civilians and contractors are used heavily across most 
directorates.  
• Unity of effort is difficult because personnel are fractionated across missions, roles are 
ambiguous, and the connection between functions and missions is unclear.

vi 
• Structural tensions stem from the friction between STARCOM’s flat structure and the need 
for mission command and control, the misalignment of functions, and insufficient support 
from the U.S. Air Force and others. 
• Mission-related risks include strain on resources and personnel as a result of the mix of start-
up, steady-state, and emergent or ad hoc functions and nonoperational tasks diverting staff 
from operational missions.  
• External factors also affect operations, including other entities misunderstanding 
STARCOM’s functions, split organizational alignment of the Operational Test and Training 
Infrastructure Integrated Program Office, and uncertainty related to the establishment of the 
Space Futures Command.  
Recommendations 
We recommend that HQ STARCOM use the STARCOM Staffing Optimization Model 
(STAR-SOM) described in this report to aid staffing decisions and implement changes to improve 
organizational design. STAR-SOM optimizes staffing to best support mission priorities and minimize 
personnel shortfalls. STAR-SOM can inform staffing decisions based on mission priorities (e.g., 
guardian development, combat credibility, organizational management) and user-defined staffing 
constraints. Using STAR-SOM can support near-term actions and lay the groundwork for middle- 
and far-term improvement. 
Additionally, we make the following recommendations: 
• To improve unity of effort, we recommend (1) establishing guardian development and combat 
credibility entities as O-6 or O-7 authorities, (2) establishing a management entity as a Senior 
Executive Service authority, (3) organizing management HQ under a chief of staff role, (4) 
emphasizing mission and management integration across all organizational levels, and (5) 
conducting a formal process for assigning responsibilities.  
• To relieve structural tension, we recommend (1) aligning staff structure and processes with 
clearly defined roles and responsibilities, (2) prioritizing primary missions, and (3) addressing 
external support concerns. 
• To address mission-related risks, we recommend (1) developing and implementing an 
organizational change management plan, (2) conducting bottom-up strategic planning, (3) 
establishing a comprehensive organizational performance management program, and (4) 
establishing a support contracts consolidation plan.  
• To influence or address external factors, we recommend establishing STARCOM operational 
policies and procedures clarifying how HQ and deltas can navigate external factors, including 
how to elevate issues with external support entities.  
• For internal alignment of program managers and contracting officers within acquisition and 
contracting, we recommend keeping the program manager as the lead; having a contracting 
officer oversee a program manager is not advisable.

vii 
Our findings highlight a variety of challenges that HQ STARCOM faces. Together, the use of the 
optimization model and the proposed organizational changes can better position HQ STARCOM to 
pursue its mission of preparing USSF guardians for space combat operations.

Chapter 1 
Introduction  
The mission of the U.S. Space Force (USSF)—to “secure our Nation’s interests in, from, and to 
space”1—shapes all USSF activities, from doctrine and organization to training and policy. The Space 
Training and Readiness Command (STARCOM) plays a central role in this mission by preparing the 
force through training, education, doctrine development, exercises, wargames, and capability testing.2 
To assess and improve its functional alignment and human resource allocation, STARCOM 
sponsored a RAND Project AIR FORCE project to help determine the manpower needs of its 
headquarters (HQ) as it prepares guardians (i.e., military and civilian personnel in the USSF) for 
space combat operations. In this report, we capture the findings and recommendations from that 
project, including an estimation of HQ STARCOM’s current and projected staffing levels and 
workload, an optimization model for staffing decisions, and an assessment of HQ STARCOM’s 
alignment and organizational structure.  
Background 
HQ STARCOM has been concerned that it does not have the manpower needed to meet its 
evolving set of requirements. To examine this issue, HQ STARCOM sought to understand the 
manpower requirements and optimal workforce composition needed to address its mission and how it 
can organize to achieve improved function alignment. The overarching issues behind these questions 
follow.  
STARCOM faces a unique set of challenges as a newly established entity with a decentralized 
organizational structure. As of July 2025, STARCOM is one of three field commands in the USSF; 
the other two are Space Operations Command (SpOC) and Space Systems Command (SSC).3 SpOC 
and SSC evolved from the established organizations Air Force Space Command (AFSPC) and the 
Space and Missile Systems Center. In contrast, STARCOM was created from the ground up on June 
21, 2021, following the establishment of the USSF in December 2019.4 STARCOM uses resources 
from a portion of the former AFSPC and from other U.S. Air Force (USAF) organizations (i.e., 
USAF resources provided for standing up the USSF).5 Although the organizational designs of SpOC 
and SSC are more mature, STARCOM continues to develop its structure and capabilities. As a

Functional Alignment and Organizational Structure 
We also analyzed HQ STARCOM’s functional alignment and organizational structure using 
qualitative and quantitative methods to compare the functions that HQ staff are assigned with the 
functions they perform and to identify improvement opportunities. Figure 1.2 illustrates our 
approach, which used data from policy documents and senior leader guidance. We conducted a 
document analysis using large language models (LLMs) and inputs from SMEs to generate a baseline 
of assigned functions from existing policy documents (see Appendix E). Data gathered from 
interviews and documentation were used to analyze issues affecting HQ STARCOM functions and 
organizational performance more broadly (see Appendix F). This mixed-methods approach informed 
our findings and recommendations to ensure that workforce capabilities meet organizational needs.

For this analysis, we conducted 39 interviews with key personnel from HQ STARCOM and 
STARCOM deltas, external stakeholders, and RAND SMEs.16 Interview data were analyzed using 
an iterative process of reviewing interview notes and identifying key takeaways supported by multiple 
SMEs (see Appendix F on the interview methodology). We reviewed more than 600 documents, 
identified 15,000 responsibilities, and narrowed the field to 121 documents and 500+ responsibilities

Manpower Analysis Caveats 
Our quantitative staffing and workload estimates come with the following caveats: 
• These numbers are estimates. All the estimates for needed staffing levels are from self-
reported data. These estimates represent data that were reported by individuals throughout 
HQ STARCOM’s directorates, divisions, and branches and encompass the entirety of HQ 
STARCOM, apart from the Chief of Staff and Surgeon General Directorates, for which we 
did not receive data. 
• Some workload is difficult to estimate. It was difficult for some offices to estimate staffing 
needs. For example, functions performed in the Historian Directorate depend on requests 
from DOW and the public, which are difficult to predict. Similarly, future functions can be 
difficult to predict because they depend on a variety of unknown factors (e.g., whether and 
how much STARCOM as an organization continues to grow or what a steady-state 
STARCOM looks like). 
• OTTI staff are not included. These data do not include personnel from the OTTI 
Integrated Program Office (IPO), a partnership between SSC and STARCOM with staffing 
provided by both.

Finding 1: HQ STARCOM Faces Challenges with Unity of Effort 
Unity of effort is important for synchronization, coordination, and integration of actions among 
disparate organizations working toward common objectives.19 Our analysis finds that challenges with 
unity of effort hinder HQ STARCOM’s ability to execute its key missions effectively. In this section, 
we address how personnel are fractionated across missions and describe challenges related to 
unsynchronized functions, the ambiguous nature of roles and responsibilities, and limited 
coordination across field commands. 
Although STARCOM’s primary roles are to develop guardians and increase combat credibility, 
staff struggle with the integration needed to accomplish these objectives.20 The data we collected in the 
staffing and workload analysis show that the majority of functions performed by HQ STARCOM’s 
directorates are related to management tasks and a relatively low number of functions are related to 
the two principal mission threads (see Figure 3.1). Additionally, most directorates perform functions 
across three missions (the two principal missions—combat credibility and guardian development—
plus organizational management), indicating that the workload for these organizations is highly 
fractionated. An optimal mix of these types of functions will be unique to any given organization, so 
there is likely no perfect mix or ideal benchmark.21 Additionally, all three types of functions are critical 
to STARCOM. That said, STARCOM has arrived at this mix organically, not strategically, and 
interviews indicate that this mix is not allowing personnel enough time to engage in tasks central to 
the two operational missions.

Finding 2: HQ STARCOM Faces Structural Tension 
In this section, we describe challenges related to STARCOM’s flat decisionmaking structures, 
insufficient support from other DAF organizations, and perceptions of STARCOM as top-heavy.  
First, STARCOM faces significant structural tension stemming from its organizational design. 
Although the USSF was explicitly structured as an agile, flat, and lean organization, the requirements 
of mission command undermine this lean design. As one interviewee put it, “We’re trying to function 
under the goal of being smaller and cheaper.”33 STARCOM’s relatively flat organizational design 
intrinsically pushes decisionmaking responsibilities to the deltas. In some ways, this autonomy is 
working effectively; for example, deltas can coordinate efficiently with other offices because 
coordination can occur independently from HQ (apart from potential funding questions).34 In other 
ways, this autonomy has created confusion and frustration. One delta interviewee stated that, 
although deltas are empowered to interact with others, they do not necessarily have the authority to 
adjust operations (such as training) as needed.35 Additionally, HQ STARCOM leadership faces 
challenges in monitoring and overseeing delta decisionmaking and related activities. Structural tension 
contributes to this problem in that STARCOM lacks the structures or processes needed to monitor 
and coordinate distributed decisionmaking.  
Second, insufficient support from the USAF has also become a concern. From its inception, the 
USSF—and by extension HQ STARCOM—was intended to be supported by the USAF.36

Finding 3: HQ STARCOM Faces Mission-Related Risks 
In this section, we discuss how HQ STARCOM is simultaneously attempting to manage start-up 
functions, transition to steady-state operations, and address emergent and ad hoc requirements and 
the impact of pending relocations and other organizational changes. 
First, HQ STARCOM faces significant mission-related risks exacerbated by the complex and 
dynamic nature of simultaneously transitioning to steady-state operations while still requiring some 
start-up functions. Lingering start-up organizational decisions from a time when resources and 
expertise were scarce detract from overall mission performance and the ability to grow into a more 
mature organization. For example, information technology duties remain dispersed and jumbled 
across the directorates and deltas instead of centralized under one office for the command. 
Additionally, the cyber directorate is not functioning at full capacity. These start-up–related 
challenges strain resources and personnel because of competing priorities that lead each directorate to 
take risks to balance management HQ responsibilities and emergent tasks. As one interviewee put it, 
[When we are given a go-do], we look at our skill set, see who aligns with the tasks 
that need to be done, and pull them out of their primary tasks, which hurts. The 
point being, we are trying to find the best athlete to fit projects, but we need to shut 
down other things in order to get priorities done.47 
Second, HQ STARCOM is fielding emergent and ad hoc requests that tend to come in the form 
of external support requests from USSF leadership for new or foundational efforts.48 For example, 
HQ STARCOM staff have been pulled from their core functions to support the implementation of a 
new officer training course, the reconfiguration of Delta Operations Squadron (shifting the structure 
to reflect Space Staff), and the rollout of new education programs. One interviewee described a recent

Finding 4: External Factors Shape HQ STARCOM Operations 
In this section, we describe the external challenges that HQ STARCOM faces, including 
misunderstandings of its functions within the USSF, overlapping responsibilities with other field 
commands, questions about OTTI organizational structures, and uncertainties related to the 
establishment of the SFC. 
Although HQ STARCOM faces many challenges stemming from internal factors, STARCOM 
does not operate in a vacuum, and the actions (or inactions) of external organizations also affect 
STARCOM’s operations. Other USSF organizations do not always have a good understanding of 
STARCOM’s functions. One interviewee noted that STARCOM’s reputation is questioned by some 
individuals who do not consider STARCOM to be on the same level as the two other field 
commands. They indicate that this leads to difficulties acquiring adequate resources. For example, one 
SME argued that “until STARCOM is accepted as operational,” STARCOM would struggle to 
acquire the resources it needs.60 The SME attributes this to a misunderstanding of STARCOM’s 
main functions and to a shortcoming with STARCOM’s organizational structure:

problem. They view STARCOM as just Delta 1, which does initial training. They 
don’t consider the most important Deltas, 11 and 12, where we are providing 
assessment of USSF weapon systems credibility. . . . The other two field commands 
have three-star commands; we only have a two-star. We should be on par with the 
other FIELDCOMs. . . . [People] look across the service and realize that we do not

Chapter 4 
An Optimization Tool Can Help Align 
Staffing with HQ STARCOM Missions 
We developed the STARCOM Staffing Optimization Model (STAR-SOM) to assist HQ 
STARCOM with staffing decisions within its current organizational structure.83 The main goal of 
STAR-SOM is to enable HQ STARCOM to make staffing decisions for each of its branches based 
on mission priorities (i.e., guardian development, combat credibility, or organizational management) 
and user-defined staffing constraints. A secondary goal of the tool is to reduce the overall staffing gap 
of HQ STARCOM so that branches receive the staff they need to complete their overall 
responsibilities. In this chapter, we describe STAR-SOM’s functionality and provide example 
scenarios to illustrate the tool’s utility. See Appendix D for a comprehensive description of the model 
and its mathematical formulation. 
STAR-SOM Functionality 
STAR-SOM allows users to set HQ STARCOM’s mission priorities and specify changes in 
staffing from current levels. The model then adjusts staffing holistically to best meet these 
requirements. For example, the tool can make staffing decisions that prioritize operational missions 
(i.e., guardian development and combat credibility) while additionally prioritizing guardian 
development over combat credibility (or vice versa). The optimization tool can also be used to make 
staffing decisions in scenarios in which additional (or fewer) staff are available. The model optimizes 
and reports staffing levels at the branch level and can also report aggregate staffing levels at the 
division, directorate, and overall HQ STARCOM level. 
The user can also control the “aggressiveness” of the suggested staffing decisions, ensuring that 
changes fall within a specified range from the current staffing levels and making them more feasible to 
adopt in practice. When the user sets the model to be less aggressive, the model’s recommended 
staffing levels are more similar to current staffing levels (e.g., within 10 percent of current staffing 
levels for each branch). When the user sets the model to be more aggressive, the user allows the 
model’s recommended staffing levels to be less similar to current staffing levels (e.g., within 50 percent 
of current staffing levels for each branch). By adjusting this parameter, HQ STARCOM can explore

Objective Function 
The objective function in STAR-SOM has two components. The first component maximizes an 
overall mission alignment score, and the second component minimizes an overall gap score. The mission 
alignment score is calculated from the number of personnel that STAR-SOM allocates to each branch, 
the average mission alignment ratings across the functions of these branches, and the user-defined 
relative priorities of these missions.  
To maximize this alignment score, the model will assign more staff to the branches that have 
higher mission alignment ratings relative to the prioritized missions. The gap score is the overall 
difference between the number of personnel that STAR-SOM allocates and the overall personnel 
required by HQ STARCOM’s current (or future) workload. To minimize the gap score, the model 
ensures that staff are not left unassigned to branches, if feasible under the constraints of the model.  
Key Constraints 
We constrain the total number of personnel that STAR-SOM allocates across branches so it 
cannot exceed the user-defined size of the organization (i.e., the self-reported staffing and workload 
levels collected in our manpower analysis). For example, if the size is 277, then the total number of 
personnel allocated across branches cannot exceed 277. This value can be the true size of the 
organization or a hypothetical value for evaluating scenarios with reduced or additional staff.  
The second constraint is that the personnel allocated to each branch cannot differ from the 
branch’s current staffing level by more than the aggressiveness-of-change percentage. We incorporate 
this constraint because we recognize that the optimization model described previously could provide a 
staffing assignment that is vastly different from the status quo staffing, making it harder to adopt in 
practice.  
Finally, we constrain the optimized staffing levels to be no greater than a target level specified by 
the user. The target is either the current workload or the future workload, as defined from our data 
call.

Organizational Recommendations Can 
Improve HQ STARCOM’s

In Chapter 4, we described how the STAR-SOM optimization model can be applied to enhance 
manpower allocation within HQ STARCOM’s existing organizational structure. However, 
manpower optimization alone will not fix several of the organizational challenges detailed in Chapter 
3. In this chapter, we present our recommendations in groups that respond to the Chapter 3 
categories of findings, including recommendations that aim to (1) improve unity of effort, (2) relieve 
structural tension, (3) address mission-related risks, and (4) influence or account for external factors. 
We used a combination of data sources—including interviews, SME insights, literature, and extensive 
internal discussion—to develop and refine the recommendations presented in this chapter. 
Recommendations Are Organized by Category with Estimates of 
Timing, Impact, and Difficulty 
We organize recommendations by category and provide estimates of the likely timing (near, 
middle, or far term), impact (low, medium, or high), and level of difficulty (low, medium, or high). 
Tables 5.1–5.4 summarize recommendations in this chapter. We engaged in a series of discussions 
with three RAND SMEs with deep expertise in manpower, personnel, and the USSF to apply specific 
criteria and achieve consensus across recommendations.  
Timing refers to how long it will probably take to implement the change. Near-term 
recommendations focus on the initial steps needed to improve internal alignment and related processes 
in HQ STARCOM and should be completed within roughly six months of the beginning of their 
implementation. Middle-term recommendations focus on larger-scale organizational and process 
changes and are expected to be completed in approximately 18 months from the start of implementing 
the near-term recommendations. Far-term recommendations focus on setting the vision, are strategic in 
nature, and typically require earlier recommendations to be executed first. Far-term changes are 
expected to be largely completed within approximately 36 months of the start of the implementation 
of the near-term recommendations. 
Impact refers to the extent to which a change affects organizational performance related to mission 
and culture. Criteria for assessing impact on mission include the extent to which a recommendation  
1. focuses STARCOM on developing guardians and increasing combat credibility 
2. can improve integration

32 
3. groups similar functions and actions 
4. removes duplication or disparate efforts.  
Criteria related to impact on culture address the extent to which a recommendation 
1. can support shared beliefs 
2. improves integration and communication 
3. fosters employee engagement 
4. supports disciplined and improved processes. 
Using the application of those criteria, impact levels are low (i.e., affecting few people or processes with 
incremental benefits), medium (i.e., affecting multiple teams with noticeable improvements but not 
fundamental transformation), and high (i.e., affecting the entire organization to significantly enhance 
performance, enable new capabilities, or achieve a greater degree of organizational transformation). 
Difficulty refers to the level of challenge, resources, and complexity involved in implementing a 
change. Criteria used for evaluating the level of difficulty consider resources, potential policy barriers, 
and the variety of stakeholders affected, including whether the recommendation 
1. departs from what has been done or what is “normal”  
2. requires organizational change (with change fatigue in mind) 
3. might lead to misalignment with HQ USSF or other organizations 
4. requires other implementation steps prior to execution.  
Results from the application of the criteria were used to determine three difficulty levels: low (i.e., 
straightforward with minimal resources), medium (i.e., requires moderate resources and coordination), 
and high (i.e., complex and resource-intensive with substantial changes needed). 
Recommendations for Improving Unity of Effort 
Our recommendations for improving unity of effort in HQ STARCOM (summarized in Table 
5.1) center on enhancing integration and communication, clarifying roles, and strengthening 
functional alignment. Clarifying staff roles and aligning functions would position HQ STARCOM as

Conclusion 
In this report, we present our evaluation of HQ STARCOM’s current and projected staffing 
levels and its related organizational challenges. We describe the current state of HQ STARCOM as 
of May 2025, analyzed its staffing needs, and highlighted key organizational issues related to HQ 
STARCOM’s functional alignment. We also explain how the staffing optimization model can be used 
and offer recommendations for organizational changes in the near, middle, and far terms aimed at 
enhancing HQ STARCOM’s effectiveness. By implementing incremental changes over time and 
leveraging staffing data and the optimization model, HQ STARCOM will be better equipped to 
achieve an optimized steady state and advocate for the mix of manpower needed to support its 
missions.

assistance in developing and executing an acquisition or contracting strategy that maximizes value to

is also not advisable because of some potential pitfalls. A PK lead would require an expansion of their 
operational scope to a great extent. They would presumably need to track and control program 
performance, which would mean taking on PM-type tasks. That increased scope could become a 
major challenge for the PK, and it would also likely mean that PM roles would be similarly 
diminished.

management in a lead role. The KO would not report to the PM because the KO must report to 
another KO, and the KO would act as a close partner with the PM with sole authority to legally 
commit the government to spending. The KO would present the PM with options and support their 
decisions.

one that allows contracting personnel to supervise program management personnel, then we 
recommend that such a structure be used temporarily. In general, we recommend against formalizing a 
structure that permanently positions the PK over the PM, even if this is being done in the near term. 
The PM’s regulatory responsibility and the importance of forming a team with the PK as a peer are 
considered critical to a successful acquisition organizational structure.

Possible Alternative Pathways 
In addition to the possible RCO-like path, HQ STARCOM has other options for how to 
organize its push to improve services and systems acquisition. It could pursue an alternative model, 
such as expanding the OTTI PEO role to support STARCOM acquisition needs beyond OTTI. Or, 
it could continue consolidating and centralizing services acquisition while building liaison roles to 
increase advocacy for STARCOM priorities within existing acquisition organizations.  
Expanded OTTI PEO Role 
The first alternative model proposes a dual-agency structure by expanding the role of the OTTI 
PEO to provide greater acquisition support within STARCOM. In this model, SAF/SQ would have 
to approve the expansion of OTTI PEO’s portfolio to encompass more of STARCOM’s mission 
needs. This structure is designed to leverage the strengths of two key entities, facilitating collaboration 
and shared decisionmaking to manage complex and specialized defense acquisition projects.  
The dual-command structure enhances STARCOM’s ability to respond to rapidly changing 
requirements and technological advancements by distributing leadership responsibilities between two 
complementary organizations. STARCOM can focus on defining requirements and evaluating 
solutions while SSC would concentrate on development activities. This approach creates a dynamic, 
responsive acquisition environment that fosters both mission readiness and innovation while ensuring 
accountability and efficiency. 
Potential Benefits 
This model would be somewhat easier to implement than an RCO model in terms of level of 
effort and resources. Although SAF/SQ authorities would be needed, this model does not disrupt the 
acquisition chain of command and makes use of existing organizational structures and roles. To be 
successful, this structure must have clear communication protocols and decisionmaking processes; 
thus, STARCOM could consider implementing regular cross-functional meetings and digital 
collaboration platforms, ensuring all stakeholders remain aligned and informed. These platforms can 
also support the integration of performance metrics and accountability frameworks, which drive 
continuous improvement.  
Potential Challenges 
This model sets a precedent in which STARCOM would be using an existing PEO structure to 
better meet its needs, so it is unclear whether STARCOM would face pushback from SAF/SQ, other 
field commands, or other organizations.

We conducted a manpower study to estimate HQ STARCOM’s current and projected staffing 
levels and workload. Although workforce demand methods, such as time-series analysis, regression 
analysis, and process mapping are typically used in manpower analyses, STARCOM’s newness and 
environment of uncertainty—marked by frequent turnover, organizational changes, ad hoc and 
nonrepetitive tasks, and temporary start-up functions—limit the effectiveness of these models.116 
Traditional approaches are tailored to steady-state organizations and do not account for the dynamic 
and evolving nature of HQ STARCOM’s current structure and activities. 
In this appendix, we detail a methodological approach designed for quantifying workload in HQ 
STARCOM and similar organizations characterized by decision-based and critical thinking tasks and 
a variable structure. The approach involves three interconnected analyses: compiling staffing and 
workload estimates, rating mission alignment, and sorting workload into functional categories. This 
approach captures the uncertainty and variability in HQ STARCOM’s current organizational 
structure, staffing, and workload.  
In this appendix, we also describe the process we used to identify functions that should be 
performed by organizations other than STARCOM (as discussed in Chapter 3) and delineate caveats 
to our analysis of STARCOM’s staffing and workload.

Mission Alignment Ratings  
To help STARCOM have visibility on how its workload aligns with its mission sets, we mapped 
each of STARCOM’s organizational functions to its three primary missions (combat credibility, 
guardian development, and operational management). These relationships are nonexclusive (i.e., a 
function can be relevant to multiple missions).  
As part of our data collection process, we asked participants to rank each function on a scale from 
1 to 5 in terms of its alignment with each of the three missions. However, the data gathered through 
this ranking exercise proved problematic because many respondents assigned all or nearly all their 
functions a score of 5 across all three missions. Assigning the highest score possible to every function 
resulted in limited differentiation of and poor visibility into the perceived degree of alignment between 
specific functions and missions. This response is both understandable—considering HQ 
STARCOM’s organizational context—and an important lesson learned for analysts. Exaggerating the 
importance of their section or team can provide the group with a buffer against change-driven anxiety 
and signal that their contributions are valued. This reinforces the need for researchers to build trust

56 
with research participants by communicating how the collected data will be used and the importance 
of its accuracy or to consider alternate methods for collecting mission alignment ratings. 
This type of result might also point to insufficient prioritization of tasks from STARCOM 
leadership. If tasks were very clearly prioritized, it is possible that participants might have considered a 
greater variety of response options. 
This challenge is significant because mission alignment is a foundational concept underpinning 
our optimization models, and it serves as a primary metric for comparing alternative organizational 
scenarios with the status quo. Accurate and nuanced alignment data are therefore essential for the 
validity of our recommendations. In response to the limitations of the initial data, we conducted a 
supplementary assessment using an internal mini-Delphi method with three SMEs from within our 
project team. Each SME was tasked with independently allocating 10 points among the three missions 
for each reported function, a method designed to force explicit prioritization and generate more-
meaningful distinctions in mission alignment. These rankings were used for the purposes of this 
project to show the utility of the analysis; we provided these rankings to STARCOM, along with an 
analytical tool and optimization model to edit the ratings if needed.  
The mini-Delphi methodology for assigning mission alignment scores was conducted in a 
structured and sequential manner for each function under review. Initially, a score of 10 was allocated 
to a particular mission area if the function’s alignment with that mission was clear and exclusive, as 
determined by the operational definitions provided for combat credibility, guardian development, and 
HQ management. In such cases, scores of 0 were automatically assigned to the remaining two mission 
areas, reflecting the absence of overlap. When a function had no clear or exclusive alignment, a score of 
1 was used to denote very minor or indirect—but nonzero—relevance to a mission. Conversely, scores 
of 8 or 9 in one mission area resulted from assigning a minimal score of 1 in one or two nonprimary 
entities, preserving the total allocation of 10 points. When functional alignment affected multiple 
missions either directly or indirectly, the remaining points (i.e., those not assigned as 10, 9, 8, or 1) 
were distributed between 2 and 7 across the relevant areas, proportionate to the perceived degree of 
alignment.  
The process involved multiple iterations, with experts sometimes assigning an initial score of 1 to 
an entity and then dividing the remaining allocation among all missions—for example, resulting in a 
final distribution, such as 4, 3, and 3. This iterative approach allowed for careful calibration and 
consensus, thereby ensuring that each function’s mission alignment was captured in a nuanced and 
analytically robust manner. 
Following the independent ratings, the SMEs convened to discuss their justifications for the 
assigned scores and reached consensus on the final mission alignment ratings for each function. In 
future studies, additional Delphi panels (such as a panel composed of organizational insiders and a 
panel composed of outside experts) could be used to compare relative scores and analyze similarities 
and differences among groups. 
Categorizations of Functions  
In another key part of our analysis, we categorized HQ STARCOM’s functions into a taxonomy 
using their definitions. This classification was a critical foundation for the subsequent organizational

57 
analysis because the functions serve as the building blocks for constructing and evaluating alternative 
organizational designs and structures. To ensure rigor and consensus in this process, we again 
employed a structured mini-Delphi methodology, whereby team SMEs with military experience 
independently assessed the data before engaging in collaborative deliberations to resolve differences 
and reach agreements. 
The analysis proceeded through several steps and was conducted sequentially so that the output 
from each step was fully accepted by the SME group before being used in the next step of the analysis. 
First, because of the large volume of data collected from STARCOM, generative artificial intelligence 
(AI) was applied to the comprehensive list of tasks and functions.119 This initial step helped identify a 
preliminary set of functional categories by normalizing the data at the functional level and surfacing 
inconsistencies, such as the conflation of tasks and functions, which helped establish a common 
operational language among respondents.  
Next, SMEs reviewed these AI-generated categories using the mini-Delphi process, thereby 
separating or combining categories as necessary to more accurately mirror the realities of military 
operations. During this phase, SMEs also clarified the definitions of each functional category to 
streamline subsequent analyses. 
The third step involved a detailed review of the AI’s attempt to assign each specific function to its 
respective category. The SMEs initially agreed with 35 percent of the AI-generated categorizations. 
They then reassigned functions where necessary because of AI misinterpretation on 49 percent of the 
categorizations and leveraged the mini-Delphi process to ensure agreement across the SME group on 
the new category. SME modifications to the category scopes, eliminating duplicate entries, and 
standardizing the wording of similar functions was necessary in the remaining 16 percent of the AI’s 
categorizations. SMEs also reached post-reassignment agreement for these new categories. This 
iterative refinement, guided by SME expertise and consensus-building discussions, ensured that the 
resulting categorization accurately represented STARCOM’s operational landscape.

Model 
STAR-SOM calls for inputs from SMEs who are familiar with the functions of HQ STARCOM 
branches and the overall goals of the organization to determine the number of personnel that should 
be allocated to each branch in HQ STARCOM. In this appendix, we provide an overview of the

Data and Inputs 
The tool uses information from our data call (see Appendix C for details). This includes the 
mission alignment ratings that capture the extent to which each branch’s functions are aligned with the 
three missions (guardian development, combat credibility, and organizational management). Also, 
each function is designated as an active, disconnect, or initiative function and indicates the number of 
personnel required for the function. As noted in Chapter 2, these personnel values are not necessarily 
whole numbers; they could represent a fraction of a person’s time. 
The user of STAR-SOM defines a set of three weights reflecting the relative importance or priority of 
each mission to HQ STARCOM at the time that the tool is used. For example, if guardian 
development, combat credibility, and organizational management receive weights of 1, 1, and 1.1, 
respectively, then organizational management is a slightly higher priority. If HQ STARCOM is 
agnostic among the three missions, they can receive weights of 1, 1, and 1, respectively. These user-
defined weights are used in conjunction with the mission alignment ratings to guide staffing decisions 
(see the next section). 
Another user-defined input is an aggressiveness of change percentage, which indicates the maximum 
amount that the user will allow the tool’s outputs (i.e., allocations of personnel to a branch) to differ 
from its current staffing levels.121 An additional input is the size of the organization (i.e., number of 
personnel available to allocate). Finally, the user of STAR-SOM indicates whether they wish to 
allocate to meet the current workload (i.e., the sum of personnel values for active and disconnect 
functions) or the future workload (i.e., the sum of personnel values for active, disconnect, and initiative 
functions). 
The STAR-SOM user can change any of the inputs as needed. For example, if guardian 
development and combat credibility become equally high priorities while organizational management 
is no longer a priority, then the weights for those missions could be changed to 1, 1, and 0, 
respectively.

Comparative organizational analysis enables the identification of leading practices, benchmarks 
performance, and highlights structural or functional differences that can inform operational 
enhancements and strategic decisionmaking. In this appendix, we identify comparative organizations 
for HQ STARCOM across DOW and the space domain and indicate a set of organizational 
characteristics from organizational design literature that is most applicable to the design of USSF 
organizations. 
Comparative Organizations Across DOW 
Because the U.S. military and many NATO countries widely adopted the Napoleonic continental 
staff system as standard, we looked at similarities and differences in the way that the services execute 
that structure across three key attributes: hierarchy, span of control, and division of functions.123 In 
this way, we could identify challenges and opportunities associated with functional alignment instead 
of simply contrasting structural alignments. Hierarchy refers to the number of organizational layers 
that have supervisory responsibilities, while span of control refers to the number of individuals directly 
reporting to a single manager. Hierarchy is described using a continuum from tall to flat, and span of 
control is described in terms of broad versus narrow. The division of functions attribute looks at how 
different missions or activities are balanced in their separation and in their integration across 
suborganizations and is most often described with more detail on a divided-integrated spectrum.  
As the generative organization of the USSF, the USAF has the greatest underlying influence on 
the USSF’s organizational design.124 USAF organizations have comparatively flat structures, using 
wings, groups, and squadrons to focus operational command within mission types. The USAF 
leverages modern communication and other C2 systems to enable its managers with a relatively broad 
span of control. Although each manager then has responsibility over a more diverse talent pool, a 
strong unity of command ensures that the activities of that organization remain flexible in achieving 
the commander’s intent. The same effective C2 and communication systems also allow the USAF to 
integrate separate and highly specialized mission areas without needing to physically colocate them.

broadness at the tactical level to enable flexibility and responsiveness to changing conditions, while 
being narrower at higher echelons to ensure effective oversight of key functions. The Army also uses

Some Army branches have more-specialized units with limited missions, while combined arms teams

We reviewed each feeder model’s organizational elements. We then identified a common set of 
descriptive organizational features that signify, from low to high, how an organization’s design is 
implemented. The following eight organizational design features were identified as the most relevant 
for comparing STARCOM with other organizations:  
• centralization—the degree to which decisionmaking authority is concentrated at the upper 
levels of an organization 
• formalization—the extent to which rules, procedures, and communication are written and 
standardized within an organization 
• specialization—the degree to which tasks are divided into separate jobs, with each job being a 
part of a larger task or process 
• standardization—the extent to which similar activities are performed in a uniform manner 
across the organization  
• hierarchy—the structure of authority and levels of management within an organization

• professionalism—the level of education, training, and expertise required by employees to 
perform their roles effectively  
• innovation—the focus and capacity of the organization to develop new ideas, products, or 
processes  
• flexibility—the ability of the organization to adapt to changes in the environment or market 
conditions.  
Through team discussions, we determined that the following two additional features were also 
important to military HQ staffs, which we incorporated into the initial list: 
• prioritization—the organization’s balance of workforce and activities dedicated to its core 
functions compared with its ancillary functions  
• independence—the extent to which the organization relies on others in the conduct of its 
mission. 
For each of the ten features, we highlighted their similarities and differences based on observation 
and interviews with members of the organizations. STARCOM and other USSF organizations can 
use the answers to those questions to inform future organizational design decisions by mapping them
