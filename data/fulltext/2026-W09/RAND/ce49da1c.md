or much of the 20th century, workers could be classified into two general categories: those 
who worked as employees for a given employer and those who worked on their own account, 
also known as the self-employed. The continuation of the Industrial Revolution—combined 
with new initiatives embedded in the New Deal to bring the country out of the Great 
Depression—ushered in new federal (and later state) legislation and case law providing employees 
with various protections to ensure fairness in hiring and firing practices, hours of work and time 
off provision, wages and benefits policies, access to collective bargaining, and workplace health and 
safety, among other areas of protections. Today, there is a patchwork of protections in these areas, 
with variation across classes of workers (i.e., employees versus the self-employed); across occupa-
tions, industries, and employer sizes based on coverage (or exclusion) rules; and across geography 
based on state or local augmentation of federal policies.
In recent years, there is a perception that employment is shifting toward nonstandard forms, 
such as independent contractors, freelancers, and gig workers. Some of these workers who fall 
outside traditional employer-employee relationships are a long-standing phenomenon—self-
employment, for example—while other jobs have been made possible by such technologies as the 
internet and apps that mediate the connection between the demand and supply sides of the labor 
market, jobs often associated with the gig economy. Such nonstandard jobs are not always covered 
by the array of worker protections that evolved over time to apply to traditional employees. Despite 
the changing nature of the employer-employee relationship, workers in nonstandard employment 
may still confront issues that lead to workplace disputes with respect to such issues as hiring and 
firing, hours and time off, wages and benefits, harassment and other work environment conditions, 
workplace safety, whistleblower claims, and the formation of unions. A related aspect of nontradi-
tional employment is whether workers have access to forms of social insurance tied to employment, 
such as workers’ compensation, unemployment insurance, disability insurance, and Social Security 
benefits, as well as employer-provided, group-based individual insurance benefits to protect against 
health care expenses, disability (not already covered by social insurance), and death and to facilitate 
retirement savings.
In this changing world of work, there is a need for employers, workers, and policymakers at the 
national and state levels to have access to objective data and analysis regarding how the changing 
nature of employment in the United States is affecting workers’ access to needed protections, appro-

classify the array of worker protections using a tax-

KEY FINDINGS
	
■There is a perception that an increasing percentage of the workforce is in nonstandard employment rela-
tionships that do not provide protections afforded to those in more traditional employer-employee rela-
tionships. This perception is not necessarily supported by government labor force and jobs data.
	
■Key protections that have evolved over time cover three types of risk faced by workers: (1) unfair prac-
tices on the part of their employers, their fellow employees, or other companies doing business with their 
employers; (2) financial harm from work-related injuries and job loss; and (3) life risks associated with 
health care costs, short-term illness, temporary or permanent disability, premature death, and inadequate 
savings for retirement.
	
■These risks are covered primarily through laws, regulations, and case law for the first type of risk; social 
insurance tied to employment for the second type; and benefits that employers offer, often voluntarily, as 
part of compensation for the third type.
	
■Certain workers have traditionally been excluded from these benefits based on specific characteristics, 
notably the self-employed and independent contractors, as well as employees in small firms, in certain 
industries, or working for firms not offering nonwage benefits. Some of these exclusions were based on 
rationales that are no longer justifiable (e.g., administrative cost, type of industry).
	
■Given that laws, regulations, and case law have evolved—not only at the federal level but also at the state 
and local levels—there is a patchwork of worker protections across the country in terms of the nature of 
the protections and which workers are covered or left uncovered.
	
■The number of workers affected by these exclusions, such as the self-employed and independent contrac-
tors, has been increasing in absolute terms as the economy has grown. The trend in the ­percentage of 
the workforce that is composed of these excluded groups depends on the data source and how excluded 
workers are defined. It is likely that the share has increased—but not by the magnitude implied by some 
estimates. For example, labor force data from 2023 suggest that about 7.4 percent of workers are indepen-
dent contractors, a share that has barely increased since 1995, when it stood at 6.7 percent. Accurately 
capturing the full variety of alternative work arrangements in official government data remains elusive.
	
■Aside from the self-employed, the current approach for determining which workers are covered by these 
protections is based on how workers are classified: as employees or as independent contractors.
	
■An alternative approach is to focus on each type or category of protection and determine which workers 
should be covered, the benefits and costs of expanding protections, and which stakeholders—workers, 
employers, or the public sector—lose or gain from a given change. This approach is likely to lead to more 
universal coverage, regardless of worker classification.

The aim of this report is to provide a framework 
for understanding the implications of the changing 
nature of work for access to the major worker protec-
tions summarized in Table 1. We do so by focusing 
on the following questions:
•	 What is the nature of worker protections tied 
to the traditional employer-employee relation-
ship and defined in law, regulations, and case 
law? Which public social insurance benefits 
are tied to traditional employment, and what 
types of private insurance and other benefits 
are provided by employers to protect their 
employees from other risks? Which workers 
are not covered by these various protections?
•	 What rules—based on legislation, regulations, 
and case law—determine which workers are 
classified as employees and have access to

changing over time and across jurisdictions?

accessing these protections because they are 
in nonstandard employment relationships—
such as independent contracting, freelanc-

contract workers? 
•	 What are the gaps in knowledge about the 
trends in worker protections and the segments 
of the workforce affected by these changes, 
and what are the best strategies for filling 
these information gaps?

The Nature of Worker 
Protections: A Taxonomy
Recent discussions of worker protections often have 
different types of protections in mind, from the right 
to collective bargaining, to coverage by unemploy-
ment insurance, to access to deferred compensation 
through employer-sponsored retirement savings.5 
With the aim of establishing a more comprehensive 
conceptualization of worker protections and systemic 
grouping of protections, we developed a taxonomy 
based on what a given worker protection aims to 
protect workers from. Our taxonomy describes three 
types of protections:
•	 protections from unfair employment or 
employment-related practices, primarily codi-
fied through laws, regulations, and case law at 
the federal, state, and local levels (Type A)
•	 protections against work-related risks, primar-
ily injuries on the job and job loss, through 
employer (or worker) contribution-based 
insurance (Type B)
•	 protections against other life risks, in which 
employers may voluntarily provide relevant 
fringe benefits, such as when social (public 
sector) insurance is not available (e.g., life 
insurance) or as a supplement to social insur-
ance to enhance the generosity of the insur-
ance or reduce the cost of the insurance (e.g., 
because insurance premiums are paid with 
pretax dollars or because employers can access 
group-based rates for its employees that are 
lower than the rates an employee would face 
through the individual insurance market) 
(Type C).
In addition to the nature of a given protection and 
how it is enforced, a key aspect of any given protec-
tion is which segments of the workforce are covered 
and which segments are not. Here, definitions often 
matter, such as who is an employee. As we discuss 
further, coverage is usually associated with the nature 
of the job, such as the existence of an employer (e.g., 
the self-employed are typically not covered), other 
aspects of the employer-employee relationship (e.g., 
independent contractors are often not covered), and 
the features of the job (e.g., managerial or supervi-
sory workers and other specific occupations may not 
be covered; those with part-time hours may not be 
covered). Coverage may also be tied to the employer’s 
characteristics (e.g., smaller versus larger businesses 
and industry of employer).

Type A: Protections Against Unfair 
Employment Practices
Several factors—the continued advance of industri-
alization, efforts by the labor movement to expand 
worker’s rights, and the response to the economic 
distress caused by the Great Depression, among 
others—came together in the 1930s to motivate the 
passage of key federal legislation seeking to protect 
workers from unfair practices on the part of employ-
ers, such as precluding workers from organizing for 
collective bargaining, requiring long work days and 
weeks, and paying uncompetitive wages (Table 2). At 
the federal level, these worker protections focused on 
rights to organize through the 1935 NLRA and later 
revisions and on provisions for a standard 40-hour 
work week and minimum wages through the 1938 
FLSA. Other protections that were added through 
federal and state legislation, regulation, and case law 
provided protections against discrimination, forms 
of harassment, unsafe workplace environments, and 
retaliation against whistleblowers. Many of these 
protections have been enhanced at the state and even 
local levels as well.
As these protections were established, some 
workers were naturally excluded because the pro-
tection was not required, given the nature of the 
employer-employee relationship or the nature of the 
industry or occupation. For example, for the self-
employed, the employer and employee are one and 
the same; thus, the self-employed worker is respon-
sible for setting their own hours and wages and 
ensuring a workplace free from safety hazards (they 
cannot discriminate against themselves, and collec-
tive bargaining is irrelevant). For these reasons, the 
self-employed are generally excluded from the set of 
protections from unfair labor practices in Table 2. 
For those who are not self-employed, other features 
of the worker’s job or their employer may be used to 
determine whether either the worker or the employer 
is granted the given protection or subject to the 
enforcing agency’s rules, respectively. Relevant fea-
tures of the job that may determine coverage include 
occupation, weekly hours, annual weeks, or the com-
bined annual hours. Employer features may include 
firm size (by volume of sales, revenue, or number of 
employees), public or private sector, and industry.6 
For example, firm size is the only requirement 
for a worker to pursue a Title VIII discrimination 
case under the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity 
Commission (EEOC). For discrimination based on 
race, color, religion, sex, pregnancy status, national 
origin, disability, or genetic information, employees 
at firms with 15 or more employees are covered. For 
age discrimination, the firm must have 20 or more 
employees. All employers are covered by the Equal 
Pay Act with regard to gender discrimination.7 The 
same rules apply to state and local governments, 
except that coverage extends to agencies of all sizes 
with regard to age discrimination.8 All federal 
employees and agencies and all employment agencies 
are covered, regardless of size.9
Two protections that are particularly complex 
under the FLSA are “wages and benefits” and “hours 
and time off provisions.” First, employer size keeps 
certain employees out of federal FLSA coverage 
because such coverage applies to businesses with 
an annual gross volume of sales made or business 
done of over $500,000 (although workers in smaller 
enterprises may be covered if their work contributes 
to interstate commerce). In terms of specific jobs, 
domestic service workers are only covered by the 
provisions if they work nine or more hours per week 
for one or more employers and make at least $2,800 
per year from at least one employer. Other occupa-
tions may be excluded from overtime pay provisions 
or both minimum wage and overtime pay provisions, 
such as executive, administrative, professional, and 
outside sales workers. 
Labor standards and discrimination are domains 
that are both, at least at the federal level, primarily 
under the purview of one agency. Other domains of 
protection are distributed across one or more other 
agencies based on topical jurisdictions. In the context 
of wrongful termination because of whistleblowing 
or retaliation, a large patchwork of laws and agencies 
are responsible for different industries. Some of the 
industries or occupations that are subject to wrong-
ful termination laws are pharmaceutical companies

premium subsidy, workers can still benefit if the 
group rate insurance premiums offered through

the private insurance market. Likewise, retirement

fees and other advantages over the private market.

other organizations that offer group-based coverage,

of private retirement saving vehicles.

Experience in Other Countries
In the United States, it is clear that many worker 
protections are tied to one’s status not just as a 
worker but as an employee. In the European Union 
(EU), workers may likewise be classified as employ-
ees or self-employed. In addition, an intermediate 
classification has been defined, called dependent 
self-employment, which defines a worker as provid-
ing “services that are performed for a business under 
a contract that is different from an employment 
contract.”21 Characteristics might include working 
primarily for or mostly for a single company, without 
the ability to hire additional workers and without the 
ability to make strategic decisions. In the U.S. rubric, 
this intermediate option is perhaps closest to an 
independent contractor who has a relationship with 
an employer—but with differences compared with a 
traditional employer-employee mode or with tradi-
tional self-employment.
In terms of worker protections, some protec-
tions in the EU are universal and more generous 
than those in the United States, while others are tied 
to employment status, as in the United States. There 
is variation between European jurisdictions about 
the balance between universal and employment-tied 
protections. For example, in the Nordic countries, 
most worker protections are universal. In other coun-
tries, such as Austria, Belgium, and France, there are 
separate social security systems for employees and for 
self-employed workers.22 In Belgium, for example, the 
social security system for the self-employed covers 
the same risks as for the employee-based system, but 
benefits may be provided for a shorter period of cov-
erage or as a flat rate. The exceptions are insurance 
against unemployment and workplace accidents, for 
which the self-employed are not covered. In a third 
group of countries, such as Latvia and Lithuania, 
employees and the self-employed are covered by the 
relevant elements of the social welfare state, such as 
health care or pensions. Other elements may exist for 
employees but are either nonexistent or voluntary for 
self-employed workers.23 A final group of European 
jurisdictions has the most limited universal protec-
tions for both employees and self-employed work-
ers. In Ireland, for example, health care coverage is 
determined by income level. Maternity, survivor, and 
state pension benefits are low for both self-employed 
workers and employees. Self-employed workers may 
get basic “means-tested long-term care, invalidity and 
unemployment benefits,” but sickness benefits are 
not available.24

Who Is an Employee?
The question of who is and is not an employee is 
not a new one. Indeed, in a 1944 opinion, the U.S. 
Supreme Court discussed the already “long and 
tortuous history” by which the common-law test of 
an employee in the torts context of liability for neg-
ligence had been worked out.27 During the 80-year 
history since then, the definition of an employee has 
been the subject of legislation, regulation, and case 
law. For example, as recently as January 2024, DOL 
issued a final rule on the definition of an employee 
versus an independent contractor under FLSA, 
replacing the guidance provided in a 2021 rule that 
was more favorable toward classifying workers as 
independent contractors and returning to an earlier 
rule in place since the 1940s.28 A full treatment of 
this history is beyond the scope of this report. How-
ever, in this section, we provide a brief discourse on 
this tortuous history of the common-law approach to 
defining an employee, an approach which continues 
to be used today,29 subject to tweaks and interpreta-
tions by different courts and agencies. Additional 
discussion of this history is provided in the appendix.
Tests Developed in Federal Law
From the beginning, courts considered the employ-
er’s control over the work being done as the main way 
to determine whether the worker was an employee or 
an independent contractor and considered such fac-
tors as whether the employer gives specific instruc-
tions, opportunity to control (because, for example, 
the work is within view), the duration of the rela-
tionship, and the relative size and sophistication of 
the businesses of the employer and worker. During 
the development of the test of control, other factors 
were also relied on by different courts, which served 
to further muddy the waters.30 The flexibility with 
which the tests were applied allowed the courts to 
restrict and expand the definition of employee to fit 
their purposes.31 Many of these factors ultimately 
became part of the modern common-law test.32
One approach relies on 13 factors identified in 
Reid,33 which centered on who had control of the way 
the work product is achieved, the skill required, the 
source of the work tools, the location of the work, 
and a series of factors related to the nature of the 
relationship between the parties (see the appendix for 
a full list of factors). Variations on this test include 
the NLRB’s ten-factor formulation, which also places 
control of the work as the first factor.34 A subsequent 
NLRB case in 2019, applying this test, found that air-
port shuttle drivers were independent contractors,35 
and a later advice memorandum also concluded that 
UberX drivers were independent contractors.36
Another well-known test adopted by DOL since 
the 1940s is known as the economic realities test. It 
relies on six nonexhaustive factors:
1.	 the nature and degree of the potential employ-
er’s control
2.	 the permanency of the worker’s relationship 
with the potential employer
3.	 the amount of the worker’s investment in 
facilities

14
4.	 the amount of skill, initiative, judgment, or 
foresight required for the worker’s services
5.	 the worker’s opportunities for profit or loss
6.	 the extent of integration of the worker’s ser-
vices into the potential employer’s business.37 
Administrative guidance from DOL suggested that 
the test resulted in a broader definition of employees, 
giving employers less room to classify workers as 
independent contractors who would be exempt from 
labor protections and thereby reducing employers’ 
costs.38 But these definitions are subject to change by 
the courts and administrative agencies. For example, 
guidance for the FLSA—which sets protections for 
minimum wages and overtime pay for hours worked 
beyond 40 hours per week, among other provisions—
issued in 2021 by DOL under the first Trump 
administration moved away from the long-standing 
economic realities test toward a five-factor test that in 
application effectively favored classifying more work-
ers as independent contractors.39 The January 2024 
final rule for the FLSA, developed under the Biden 
administration, withdrew the 2021 guidance and 
returned to the six-factor economic realities test.
Yet another test is used by the Internal Revenue 
Service (IRS) based on three broad factors delineated 
in 2004 (with 20 specific factors behind the three 
broad factors):
Behavioral control: A worker is an employee 
when the business has the right to direct and 
control the work performed by the worker, 
even if that right is not exercised.
Financial control: Does the business have 
a right to direct or control the financial and 
business aspects of the worker’s job?
Relationship: The type of relationship depends 
upon how the worker and business perceive 
their interaction with one another.40
Again, despite the differences in the criteria that 
constitute these tests, the central concept is who has 
“control” over the work performed, although the eco-
nomic reality concept puts a spotlight on economic 
dependence and vulnerability, which is particu-
larly relevant for lower-skilled vulnerable workers. 
And regardless of which classification test is used, 
employers may try to game the system by classifying 
employees as independent contractors. According to 
a 2015 report, “numerous state-level studies show that 
between 10 and 20 percent of employers misclassify 
at least one worker as an independent contractor.”41 
In certain sectors, such as trucking and construction, 
the problem is exacerbated.42 Other times, workers 
may be misclassified accidentally and not through 
fraud. Misclassification has consequences in terms of 
an employee’s access to worker protections to which 
they are entitled and lost revenue from employer and 
employee contributions to social insurance programs.
Action at the State Level
The question of who counts as an employee needs to 
be resolved not only at the federal level but also by 
the individual states. Some domains, such as workers’ 
compensation, are handled almost exclusively at the 
state level where the program is administered. Other 
domains are subject to both federal and state legisla-
tion and case law. This plays out in a few ways: Some 
state agencies may go further than the federal law in 
terms of the rights granted to employees; they may be 
less restrictive in defining covered employees; and, 
finally, state case law may differ with respect to the 
tests employed to determine whether a worker is an 
employee or an independent contractor. To examine 
the role of state law, we focus on California’s laws and 
regulations. We selected California for a few reasons, 
most notably because it has some of the strongest 
worker protection laws in the country.43

Related Developments in California and Other 
States
Worker classification is the major focus of legisla-
tive reform and retrenchment because, as explained 
earlier, that initial determination makes all the dif-
ference with respect to most rights and protections. 
In some cases, as an alternative approach, rights or 
protections traditionally reserved for employees have 
been extended to independent contractors. In 2015, 
for example, the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Dis-
trict of California interpreted the Fair Employment 
and Housing Act (FEHA), California’s antidiscrimi-
nation and harassment act, to include claims brought 
by independent contractors. The court explained that 
“courts must broadly construe FEHA provisions to 
implement the legislative intent to provide protec-
tions to workers and end the practice of sexual (and 
other forms of) harassment in the workplace, and to 
hold those with the ability and authority to prevent 
and remedy the situation responsible for addressing 
the problem.” The court continued that “[t]here is no 
basis in this statutory phrase or the definition of the 
phrase to preclude recovery for an individual who 
provided services under a contract merely because 
he or she is also employed by a separate entity with 
respect to the work performed.”55 In light of the 
#MeToo movement, California passed a law in 2018 
extending FEHA protection to independent contrac-
tors for any harassment, as opposed to sexual harass-
ment only.56 A few other states have followed suit by 
amending their harassment or discrimination laws 
to incorporate protections for independent contrac-
tors.57 (See the appendix for other examples.)
Whereas California and some other states have 
been moving toward recognizing the precarity of 
individuals making their living as gig or tempo-
rary workers, other states have gone in the opposite 
direction, attempting to enshrine laws that protect 
businesses from having to treat their workers as 
employees or to offer independent contractors certain 
benefits. 
One such state is Texas, which passed a law in 
2017 stating that a driver for “transportation network 
compan[ies],” such as Lyft or Uber, is an independent 
contractor “for all purposes” and not an employee if 
the company does not prescribe the driver’s hours, 
impose restrictions on their ability to work for 
other companies, limit the territory in which they 
may work, or restrict their ability from working in 
another occupation, and the driver and the company 
agree in writing that the driver is an independent 
contractor.58 Texas is not the only state creating these 
new rules or exceptions for online platforms with 
input from lobbyists and companies. At least eight 
additional states have carved out so-called transpor-
tation network companies or marketplace contractors 
from various protections and benefits, such as mini-
mum wage and health and safety regulations.59
The European Experience
In Europe, different jurisdictions also use well-
defined tests to determine whether a worker has 
been properly classified. A main element in all EU 
jurisdictions for determining whether a worker is an 
employee or not is very similar to the element of con-
trol relied on in the United States. The terminology 
used is subordination in Italy, personal dependence 
in Denmark and Germany, and legal dependence in 
Belgium and France. In some countries, such as Italy, 
this element is essential to proving an employment

17
relationship, while in others, such as Germany, no 
single factor is key.60 In addition to economic and 
organization subordination, other important ele-
ments of the employment relationship are whether 
the employer provides tools and equipment, whether 
the work is performed solely or primarily for the 
benefit of someone else, and whether the employer 
has the power to issue personal and functional 
instructions.61 
Rather than applying a test, several countries 
have adopted a “primacy of facts” approach, whereby 
courts may reject how the employment relationship 
is labeled and analyze the reality of the situation in 
practice.62 Alternatively, some European jurisdic-
tions have focused on whether there is a presumption 
of an employer-employee relationship. In Belgium, 
for example, there is a rebuttable presumption of an 
employment contract, subject to certain caveats, in 
certain industries considered riskier for worker mis-
classification, including construction, caretaking, 
and agriculture. In other countries, such as Greece, 
Italy, and Hungary, there is a presumption of an 
employment relationship in all disputes between a 
worker and their employer, and the burden is on the 
employer to prove otherwise.63

Employment Relationships and

definition of self-employment, about 10 percent of 
individual workforce members (or 17 million workers 
out of about 160 million workers in total) are identi-

the workforce would therefore be classified as wage 
and salary workers. At the same time, other esti-

falls outside the traditional employer-employee rela-

36 percent of the U.S. workforce, or about 58 million

form of gig employment in 2022, an increase from 
2021.66 Data reported by Statista are even more 
striking, with an estimated 72 million independent

26 million (36 percent of the total) are recorded as 
working as full-time independent workers.67 Accord-

just over 90 million independent workers by 2028.

ideally account for exclusions associated with self-
employment status, as well as firm size, industry, 
occupation, and any other worker or job character-
istic that affects access to worker protections. We 
would also ideally account for workers in terms of 
their main source of earned income versus secondary 
or more intermittent gig employment that is used to 
supplement a primary job.
Given these challenges with the data, our goal in 
this section is to understand the number of workers 
affected by various exclusions from worker protec-
tions and whether those groups are expanding or 
shrinking in absolute size and as a share of the over-
all workforce. In doing so, we draw on household-
based data from the monthly Current Population 
Survey as reported by BLS, which classifies individu-
als in the private and public sectors according to their 
primary job and, in some cases, accounts for people 
who hold more than one job at a given time. We also 
rely on statistics compiled from private sector estab-
lishment data based on the jobs reported by firms 
(or employers), typically captured in the monthly, 
quarterly, or annual Current Employment Statistics 
reported by BLS. Finally, we consider specialized data 
collection efforts, such as the Contingent Worker 
Supplement to the Current Population Survey house-
hold survey collected intermittently by BLS (spe-
cifically for 1995, 1997, 1999, 2001, 2005, 2017, and 
2023).68 IRS tax records are another source for pars-
ing out the types of earned income reported by tax-
payers. In our assessment of the available statistics, 
the current perspective is based on figures, where 
available, up to 2024. We set 1990 as the ideal starting 
point for examining trends over time (although some 
data series do not go that far back), thereby capturing 
as much as a 30-year time horizon.
Evidence of Changing Employment 
Relationships in the United States
Earlier, we noted that certain types of workers 
were more likely to be excluded from at least some 
worker protections in our three-way classification 
(see Table 5). These excluded groups include the 
self-employed and independent contractors, some 
agricultural and domestic workers, and some part-
time workers, all of whom are tracked through 
surveys of individuals. Information on employment 
by business size are tracked through data on jobs 
provided by employers. These categories of work-
ers or jobs are readily tracked in employment and 
jobs statistics, both their absolute numbers and as a 
share of the workforce or jobs, although there may be 
double counting when disaggregating the workforce 
by firm size, industry, or occupation. Other catego-
ries of workers, such as gig workers, are less readily 
identified in the official statistics, but we assemble 
the information that is available to gauge their likely 
numbers and workforce share.
Workers in Self-Employment (Household 
Data)
Focusing on the unincorporated self-employed69—
those least likely to have access to the employment 
protections listed in Table 1—the long-term trend is 
a decline in the share of the workforce that is self-
employed, from 8.6 percent in 1990 to 6.1 percent 
in 2024 (see Figure 1, blue dashed line, right axis).70 
The absolute number of workers in this category 
(blue solid line, left axis) also fell slightly during this 
same period, from 10.2 million to 9.8 million. At 
the same time, from 2000 onward, when the series 
on the incorporated self-employed is available, the 
estimates show an increase in both the absolute 
number of workers thus classified and their share of 
the workforce (3.3 percent to 4.3 percent). Given that 
these workers are classified as wage and salary work-
ers in official labor force data, they are potentially 
more likely than their unincorporated counterparts 
to have access to some worker protections. Neverthe-
less, the combined number of self-employed, regard-
less of their incorporation status, has not increased 
as a share of the workforce in the past two decades

Jobs in Small Firms (Establishment Data)
Data reported by firms to BLS indicate a modest 
downward shift since the early 1990s in the share 
of private sector jobs in firms with fewer than 50 
employees, from about 32 percent in 1993 to 27 per-

decline is evident for the smallest firm-size group of 
1 to 4 employees, and for the three other size groups

each of these firm-size groups has increased in abso-

firms with fewer than 20 employees (not shown in 
the figure), the share of the jobs has also fallen (to 
17 percent in 2024) at the same time that the absolute 
number has risen to about 22 million jobs.

self-employed are the largest group without access 
to the worker protections featured in Table 1, com-

in Table 5. Conceptually, identifying those who are 
self-employed should capture individuals who work

Further Refinement of Worker 
Classification Rules and Protection 
Coverage
One approach is to continue the debate over how 
to classify workers as employees versus indepen-
dent contractors. The furor over California’s post-
Dynamex efforts to enshrine the ABC test through 
AB5 and the countermeasures through Proposition 
22 has enlivened the debate about whether there 
is, or could be, a suitable middle ground between a 
traditional employee and an independent contrac-
tor (or the self-employed). Along these lines, prior 
to AB5, Assembly Bill 71, which was introduced in 
December 2018, was supported by the California 
Chamber of Commerce, as well as Uber, Lyft, and 
other major players in the gig economy.82 Assembly 
Bill 71 aimed to overturn Dynamex and require the 
use of the traditional common-law multifactor test of 
control from Borello.83 Whether the common-law test 
or multifactor test is the law of the land in California 
and elsewhere is an important issue, because if work-
ers are classified as employees, they gain a fuller set 
of protections. However, in the California context, 
and in other states, “Uber and Lyft have made clear 
in their conversations with labor leaders that full 
employment status is a nonstarter.”84 Yet, there has 
been recognition that a middle ground may be pos-
sible in which independent contractors would qualify 
for additional protections, such as Type A protections 
in our taxonomy (see Table 1).
As a California district court judge put it in 2013, 
“At first glance, Lyft drivers don’t seem much like 
employees. . . . But Lyft drivers don’t seem much like 
independent contractors either.”85 This statement, 
made about Lyft but applicable to gig economy work-
ers in general, helps explain why many people sup-
port the idea of a new designation for gig workers, 
with some, but not necessarily all, of the protections 
and benefits of a traditional employee. And such an 
approach could encompass other workers in non-
traditional employment situations outside the gig 
economy, as proposed by Seth Harris, a former DOL

Another option within existing labor laws would 
be to extend which workers are covered by specific 
labor provisions. Indeed, such coverage variation 
exists now for Type A protections (see Table 2). For 
example, the protections against discrimination and 
forms of harassment enforced by the EEOC are gen-
erally available to all traditional employees, with the 
exception of those working for small employers. On 
the other hand, independent contractors are gener-
ally excluded from potentially relevant protections 
related to discrimination, collective bargaining, 
workplace health and safety, and whistleblowing. But 
these restrictions are loosening in some jurisdictions, 
as noted earlier and in the appendix. For example, 
California courts have extended coverage under the 
state’s antidiscrimination and harassment act to 
include independent contractors, as discussed earlier. 
Furthermore, new laws may be passed to offer 
key protections. For example, the Freelancers Union 
helped get the Freelance Isn’t Free law passed in 
New York City in 2017. The law, the first of its kind, 
requires that engagements for more than $800 be 
memorialized in a written contract with the follow-
ing information: the name and mailing address of the 
hiring party and the freelance worker, an itemized 
list of all services to be provided by the freelancer, 
the value of the services to be provided, the method 
of payment, and the date the freelancer is to be paid. 
If no date is specified, the worker is entitled to pay-
ment within 30 days of completion. A worker who 
is not paid in a timely manner can file a civil action 
or an administrative complaint with the Office of 
Labor Standards, and the New York City Corpora-
tion Counsel may file actions against companies 
with a pattern of nonpayment.89 In addition, the 
law helps protect freelancers from acts of retaliation 
and harassment, such as “penalizing the freelancer, 
threats, or attempts to blacklist the freelancer in 
an effort to stop the freelancer for exercising their 
rights. . . . Hiring parties also cannot deny freelancers 
future work if they file a complaint under the Free-
lance Isn’t Free Act or threaten to sue the freelancer 
for exercising their rights.”90 Double damages are 
available when the hiring party attempts to retaliate 
against the freelancer for exercising their rights.91 A 
similar law was adopted in 2024 at the state level and 
has served as a model in other states and cities.92
The idea of granting collective bargaining rights 
to workers in the “gray zone” has been raised in 
Europe as well. A 2019 OECD report noted that col-
lective bargaining is traditionally a right granted only 
to employees but that, because some self-employed 
workers share relevant characteristics with employ-
ees, and some have little bargaining power over 
their employers, such rights should be extended to 
them.93 The report offers two suggestions: to simply 
provide collective bargaining rights to the depen-
dent self-employed or to offer those rights to people 
working in specific sectors.94 Austria, for example, 
has extended collective bargaining rights to perma-
nent freelance journalists and to people perform-
ing manual work at home (as opposed to the more 
common remote workers).95
Ultimately, these expansions to worker protec-
tions could occur as laws and regulations are rewrit-
ten, as case law expands the interpretation of existing 
laws, or as new laws are implemented. One recent 
example is New Jersey’s 2023 Temporary Worker’s 
Bill of Rights, which extends the protections available 
to employees of temporary help companies. Among 
its provisions, depending on certain conditions, tem-
porary workers are entitled to the same average pay 
and benefits as their regular employee counterparts 
at the client company.96 A similar approach has been 
adopted in Illinois through the 2024 Illinois Day 
and Temporary Labor Services Act. Another recent 
example is the 2024 Federal Trade Commission rule 
banning the use of noncompete clauses in most exist-
ing and future employment provisions, a rule that 
covers both employees and independent contrac-
tors.97 Noncompetes have been shown to limit worker 
mobility, suppress wages, and stifle competition and 
innovation. Making noncompetes unenforceable is 
viewed as protecting workers’ rights to job mobility.98

Portable Benefits and Other Models 
Another approach is to detach access to key protec-
tions from a particular job or employer through a 
portable benefits system or, more generally, a por-
table protections system.99 This approach would most 
likely be applied to Type B or Type C protections 
in our taxonomy (see Table 1). The Aspen Institute 
identifies three key elements of a system of portable 
benefits.100 First, such a system must, obviously, be 
portable, meaning that workers “own their own bene-
fits,” which would not be tied to the specific employer 
they are working for or the app they are using.101 
The second element is that it should be prorated. The 
different platforms should contribute to the work-
ers’ benefits at an amount commensurate with the 
amount that the contractor works for that company. 
Finally, the system should be universal, so that all 
workers have access to all the benefits they need. The 
authors explain that a 
Portable Benefits system could apply to any 
type of worker, though it is designed with work-
ers who do not have access to affordable bene-
fits, namely independent contractors and part-
time workers. The system should likely provide 
at least a core of health insurance, retirement, 
and insurance for injured workers, but could 
be expanded to cover optional types of insur-
ance (like vision, dental, life, etc.), paid time off, 
education and training, and potentially even 
novel products like income-smoothing tools or 
wage insurance. It could also form the basis of 
an effective and resourced worker voice orga-
nization in an era where traditional collective 
bargaining is increasingly inaccessible to most 
private sector workers.102
Much of the appeal of the portable benefits 
model is that it could be close to comprehensive, 
at least in terms of the benefits provided and the 
types of workers covered. Rather than being offered 
through employers, a public or private sector entity 
would serve as the intermediary. Guidance is avail-
able for policymakers to implement this strategy, 
notably at the state level.103 The portable benefits 
model, as its name implies, would be better suited 
to providing workers with protections against risks 
that are addressed through social insurance or 
group-rate private insurance products or products 
related to retirement investments. Harris points out 
that “[w]orkers’ right to organize and bargain col-
lectively, the requirements of overtime premium pay 
and the minimum wage, and workplace and labor 
market anti-discrimination protections are inex-
tricably bound up with a particular work relation-
ship and could not be included in portable benefits 
systems.”104 At the same time, some examples show 
how the intermediary has also taken up other types 
of protections.

“can result in disciplinary action ranging from repri-

also file a claim inquiry, alleging that an employer on 
a covered production violated the CBA.107 In addi-
tion, SAG-AFTRA members may submit harassment 
and discrimination complaints and have several 
other streams to handle potential harassment and 
discrimination issues.108 The #MeToo movement

Which Benefits and Protections 
Matter?
When considering the policy options discussed in 
this section, an important question is which protec-
tions should be included in a universal system. There 
might be some protections that workers are willing 
to give up but others that they are not, and employ-
ers likely have their own set of preferences. There 
has been some empirical work in this area, but more 
scholarly work would be very valuable.
The MetLife Employee Benefits Trends Study 
consists of interviews with 2,675 full-time employ-
ees. One of the topics of the 2019 survey was which 
benefits are “need to have,” which are “nice to have,” 
and which are “not needed.” The top benefits named 
as “need to have” were health insurance; prescription

drug coverage; dental insurance; a defined contribu-

insurance or discount programs; and life insurance. 
All these benefits were ones that at least half the 
respondents said were necessary.119 Similar responses 
are found in the Employee Benefits Research Institute 
2018 survey. When asked about the most important 
benefits in making a job decision, the top response 
was health insurance, followed by a retirement sav-

these are all Type C benefits according to our taxon-

or private employers. The nature of these surveys did 
not allow respondents to consider Type A or Type B 
protections in the relative rankings. For this reason, 
it is not possible to assess the importance of the full 
array of worker protections covered in our taxonomy. 
In 2018, the Society for Human Resource Man-
agement and SAP conducted a survey of external 
workers (contract or temporary workers), internal 
workers (traditional employees), and managers of 
external workers. In response to the question, “Which 
one of the following benefits, if provided in addi-

you/external workers to work for a certain company?” 
The top answer from workers, human resources

benefits, followed by paid vacation and retirement 
contributions. Interestingly, more HR employees and 
managers answered health care benefits and paid 
vacations than did external workers, whereas more 
external workers valued retirement contributions. For 
example, while approximately 17 percent of external 
workers said that paid vacation would motivate them 
most, 35 percent of HR employees said it would be the 
top motivator. External workers also gave priority to 
paid leave to care for sick family, tuition reimburse-

HR and managers. In general, there was a somewhat 
more even spread among the responses from external 
workers than the other two groups, suggesting that 
although HR and management are aware of the prior-

some of the diversity of potential motivations.121

Conclusion 
The types of worker protections and which work-
ers are covered has been evolving over time, even 
more so in the past several decades in response to the 
changing nature of work. The trends driving these 
changes—demographics, globalization, and techno-
logical change—are likely to continue, if not acceler-
ate, in the decades to come.126 Thus, there is a grow-
ing imperative to understand the nature of worker 
protections in the labor market, which workers are 
or are not covered, and the policy changes needed 
to support equitable access to critical protections. In 
this final section, we highlight our key conclusions 
based on current knowledge, the gaps in our under-
standing and how to fill them, and the policy options 
to address the shortfalls in the current system. 
Summary of Key Findings
The array of worker protections in the United 
States—classified by our taxonomy into three tiers—
is a patchwork of protections that vary across geog-
raphy, the nature of the employer-employee relation-
ship, and other features of the employer or the nature 
of the job the worker performs. Our taxonomy serves 
to classify worker protections into three categories 
based on the nature of the risks that the protections 
are designed to ameliorate: (1) unfair practices on the 
part of workers’ employers, their fellow employees, or 
other companies doing business with their employ-
ers; (2) financial harm from work-related injuries and 
job loss; and (3) life risks associated with health care 
costs, short-term illness, temporary or permanent 
disability, premature death, and inadequate savings 
for retirement. Such a classification can help policy-
makers and the public think clearly about the nature 
of the current protections, how they are imple-
mented, and ways in which the protections can be 
broadened through legal, regulatory, or institutional 
changes.
One reason for the interest in worker protec-
tions is the perception that a growing segment of the 
workforce is in nonstandard work arrangements—
independent contractors, freelancers, gig workers, 
and the self-employed more generally—and does 
not have access to many of these worker protec-
tions. Indeed, self-employed workers have long been 
excluded from coverage by key labor laws, work-
related social insurance (e.g., workers’ compensa-
tion and unemployment insurance), and employer-
provided group-based insurance for health costs, 
disability and death, and retirement savings, among 
other benefits. Some of these exclusions are justi-
fied by the nature of self-employment work. Other 
exclusions—such as for workers in small firms or 
workers in certain industries, such as agricultural or 
domestic work—may have had a rationale when the 
protections were first implemented but can no longer 
be justified in the current labor market.
Unfortunately, labor market data and govern-
ment statistics have not fully evolved to accurately 
capture the changing nature of employer-employee 
relationships and the trends in nonstandard work 
arrangements. Viewed from an imperfect lens but 
drawing on multiple data sources, data systems sug-
gest that independent contractors comprise about 
10 percent of U.S. workers, a share that has remained 
relatively stable since at least 1995, when data were 
first collected (although the absolute number of 
workers affected may have increased). Adding in the 
self-employed, another labor market group that has 
shown little change (if not a decline) in recent years, 
and those in nonstandard work, the figure comes to at 
most 20 percent of the workforce. Other data sources 
suggest that as many as one-third or more of work-
ers have at least dabbled in the gig economy, a figure 
that is not supported by more careful data analyses.

32
The lack of agreed-upon definitions of the new types 
of employment and reliable data to measure their 
numbers and workforce share results in great uncer-
tainty about which workers fall outside traditional 
employer-employee relationships and therefore are 
uncovered by many key worker protections.
A key feature of the present labor market and 
access to worker protections is the distinction 
between an employee and an independent contractor. 
Although both have a relationship with an employer 
(as opposed to the self-employed), there is a long 
and tortuous regulatory and case law history associ-
ated with delineating the multiple criteria that may 
be used to distinguish one type of worker from the 
other. That distinction is critical for determining 
whether key worker protections are applicable or 
not. Thus, considerable resources have been devoted 
to drawing the dividing line between employee and 
independent contractor. At stake are the costs facing 
employers when workers are classified as employ-
ees and entitled to various protections and benefits. 
Employers save labor costs when workers are classi-
fied as independent contractors, but those workers 
bear more of the costs associated with unfair employ-
ment practices, workplace injury or job loss, and 
other life risks. The changing nature of work away 
from traditional employer-employee relationships 
ensures that worker classification and misclassifica-
tion will remain an ongoing high-stakes battleground 
for labor market policy. Further work to understand 
which stakeholders—workers, employers, and the 
public sector—gain or lose from current or proposed 
policies will require further economic analysis.
The COVID-19 pandemic placed a spotlight on 
the consequences for workers and the labor market 
when key worker protections are absent. In the case 
of the pandemic, the lack of coverage for worker ill-
ness, job loss, or death for independent contractors 
and other uncovered workers led to unprecedented 
temporary extensions of coverage to otherwise ineli-
gible workers. Rather than responding after the risks 
are realized, there is merit in extending key benefits 
more universally, regardless of worker classification.
Knowledge Gaps and How to Fill Them
Efforts to address gaps in access to worker protec-
tions require filling in gaps in current knowledge in 
support of evidence-based policy reforms. Data gaps 
are perhaps most salient. First, there is a need to care-
fully map the variation in access to worker protec-
tions at least at the federal and state levels, if not at 
the local level (given policies established in several 
large cities). Each state varies in the nature of the laws 
and regulations that govern the array of worker pro-
tections and how they are implemented. Such varia-
tions include details about which groups of workers 
have access to the protections based on the nature 
of the employer-employee relationships, the charac-
teristics of the employer (e.g., firm size and indus-
try), and the characteristics of the worker and their 
job (e.g., their hours worked, their control over the 
work process and work product). A mapping of the 
variation in worker protections against the size and 
composition of the labor force in a given jurisdiction 
would support estimation of the number and makeup 
of workers uncovered by any given protection at the 
state and national levels. Such information could be 
used to track trends in access to worker protections 
as the labor market continues to evolve.

classified as independent contractors, but those 
workers bear more of the costs associated with 
unfair employment practices, workplace injury or

Second, there is a need to modify existing data 
collection efforts or to institute new ones to reflect 
the changes in the number and types of jobs that 
workers hold and the types of employer-employee 
relationships that apply to each job. Access to and 
take-up of different types of protections and benefits 
could also be addressed. The National Academies of 
Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine recognized the 
need for updated information in a 2020 report that 
contained specific recommendations for BLS regard-
ing its data collection programs, such as the Con-
tingent Worker Supplement discussed earlier.127 The 
steps for BLS include revised survey instruments in 
the Contingent Worker Supplement to better capture 
multiple job holdings and the nature of the employ-
ment relationship for each job. The ability to use tax 
return data in conjunction with household survey 
data could be advanced as well.
New data collection at the national level or spe-
cialized data collection at a state or local level could 
shed light on the consequences of gaps in worker 
protections by documenting the incidence of workers 
who experience a workplace injury or job loss with-
out access to workers’ compensation or unemploy-
ment insurance, who are not protected against retali-
ation following a whistleblowing incident, or who 
have no legal remedy when a wage or hours violation 
occurs. Qualitative or quantitative data collection 
could document the consequences of the status quo 
for worker employment outcomes and other aspects 
of well-being. 
Third, there is also a need to understand how 
future technology advancements—especially arti-
ficial intelligence (AI) and artificial general intelli-
gence (AGI)—will change the nature of work, where 
work is performed, and the implications for worker 
classification. For example, as more jobs are auto-
mated because of AI, some managerial jobs that were 
exempt from some labor law provisions (such as over-
time pay) may shift to nonexempt status, increasing 
employers’ costs. Changes in how work is performed, 
such the nature of performance supervision or con-
trol over work tasks, may lead employers to shift from 
using direct employees to independent contractors. 
AI may even provide a mechanism for automatically 
determining the classification for a given position 
based on information provided about the job as it 
relates to traditional classification tests.128 A more 
complete understanding of these issues and how they 
are changing will improve our ability to understand 
implications for workers’ current access to employ-
ment protections and how modifications, such as 
portable benefits, will affect the workforce.
Policy Considerations 
Our analysis suggests several options for moving 
forward. A first step would be identifying the most 
consequential worker protections and the share of the 
workforce without access to those key protections. 
The estimated magnitude of the exposed risk may 
be based on the likelihood of the risk occurring (e.g., 
job loss with no unemployment insurance cover-
age) and the expected economic loss that the worker 
would face (e.g., the expected earnings loss based on 
earnings and the expected length of an unemploy-
ment spell). Priority could be given to addressing 
those risks that have the largest cost in terms of the 
expected loss per uncovered worker and the sum of 
that cost across the uncovered workforce members. 
Costs to employers and the public sector of the cur-
rent system and any future policy changes should 
also be delineated. Spillover consequences could 
also be captured, such as the aggregate effects on the 
economy when unemployment spells are not covered. 
Other factors to consider may relate to equity consid-
erations or the effects on economic disparities. 
Once the key protections are identified, the tax-
onomy we present can be used to assess the policy 
options (see Table 9). Type A protections may be 
addressed by expanding the coverage of a given pro-
tection at the federal or state levels through changes 
in laws or regulations. Type B protections, which 
are based on public social insurance schemes, may 
require expanding existing insurance programs or 
expanding access through private entities, such as 
unions, guilds, or other organizations. In each case, 
there will be a need to determine how the additional 
costs of expanded coverage will be paid for, such as 
through some combination of contributions from 
employers, employees, or other stakeholders (e.g., 
government subsidies, private philanthropy). Finally, 
extensions of Type C protections, given that many are 
voluntarily offered by employers, may require gov-

34
ernment incentives to induce employers to expand 
access or to support portable benefits that are decou-
pled from a specific employer, as discussed in earlier 
examples. Again, which stakeholders will bear the 
costs of the expanded coverage will need to be deter-
mined when designing the policy. 
Finally, other principles can guide policy reforms 
in this area. For example, the principle of universal 
access may be given priority. Unless there is a justifi-
cation for excluding a class of workers, the aim would 
ideally be universal coverage of any given protection. 
For example, some Type A protections (e.g., wage and 
hours protections under the FLSA) are not relevant 
for the self-employed, so their exclusion can be justi-
fied. But each existing exclusion or new exclusion 
under consideration should be examined to deter-
mine whether it is merited. Given the cost of expand-
ing coverage to any given protection, it may be justi-
fied to initially target the most vulnerable of excluded 
workers, such as domestic workers, agricultural 
workers, or those with low wages in other sectors. As 
more resources become available, the protections can 
be extended to less vulnerable groups.
Another principle would be to seek to decouple 
worker protections from the employment relation-
ship, wherever it is feasible. Giving priority to porta-
ble benefits, in particular, allows for greater flexibil-
ity for workers to work for multiple employers at the 
same time or to seamlessly move from one employer 
to another. This feature is particularly relevant for 
Type C protections against various types of life risks, 
mitigated through health insurance, life insurance, 
disability insurance, and retirement savings. Such 
portability has already gained traction in sectors in 
which independent contractors, freelancers, or other 
nonstandard work arrangements are the norm. Por-
tability may be implemented through public mecha-
nisms, such as the ACA marketplace for health insur-
ance, or private organizations, such as unions, guilds, 
or worker associations.
Appendix. Legal Background in 
the United States
This appendix provides additional background on 
the legal history of the definition of an employee in 
the United States at the federal and state levels (with 
a focus on California), building from the brief back-
ground provided in the body of the report. 
Tests Developed in Federal Law
Common-Law Test of Employee Status
The common-law test of employee status requires 
courts to examine the full circumstances of the 
case and rely on many factors, although control is 
still the primary factor. The U.S. Supreme Court, 
in Nationwide Mut. Ins. Co. v. Darden, ruled that 
“definitions of ‘employee,’ ‘employer,’ and ‘employ-
ment’ are to be determined under the common law 
of agency, rather than individual state law, whenever 
statutes failed to specifically define ‘employee.’”129 
In Salamon, the Second Circuit explained that, 
although Darden was applied in the ERISA context, 
courts have used the common-law test in Title VII 
and other employment discrimination cases.130 The 
common-law test, the court explained, depended on 
an analysis of the 13 Reid factors:
1. the hiring party’s right to control the 
manner and means by which the product is 
accomplished;
2. the skill required;  
3. the source of the instrumentalities and tools;
4. the location of the work;
5. the duration of the relationship between the 
parties;
6. whether the hiring party has the right to 
assign additional projects to the hired party;
7. the extent of the hired party’s discretion over 
when and how long to work;  
8. the method of payment;
9. the hired party’s role in hiring and paying 
assistants;  
10. whether the work is part of the regular 
business of the hiring party;
11. whether the hiring party is in business;
 12. the provision of employee benefits; and
13. the tax treatment of the hired party.131

35
These factors are not exhaustive, and other 
factors may be relevant depending on the context. 
Special consideration, however, must be given to 
the question of control.132 Different agencies or 
courts sometimes have different formulations of the 
common-law test. The NLRB, for example, has tradi-
tionally relied on a ten-factor test, with control over 
the detail of the work as the first factor.133
In 2009, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals, in 
a case concerning the NLRB, shifted the emphasis 
in the common-law test, saying that “courts should 
examine whether ‘the position presents the opportu-
nities and risks inherent in entrepreneurialism.’”134 
One effect of this new definition was that it seemed 
to give employers an increased ability to exclude 
workers who would normally be considered employ-
ees.135 In 2014, the NLRB clarified that entrepre-
neurial opportunity should not be the main element 
considered but should just be examined along with 
the ten common-law factors.136
In 2019, the NLRB reversed, in a finding that 
airport shuttle drivers were independent contrac-
tors.137 The board explained that “control and entre-
preneurial opportunity are two sides of the same 
coin: the more of one, the less of the other. Indeed, 
entrepreneurial opportunity often flowers where 
the employer takes a ‘hands off’ approach.’”138 The 
Super Shuttle DFW case has played a significant role 
in NLRB cases involving gig economy workers. For 
example, in a 2019 advice memorandum from an 
associate general counsel of the NLRB, UberX driv-
ers were found to be independent contractors after 
a consideration of the common-law factors viewed 
“‘through the prism of entrepreneurial opportunity’ 
set forth in Super Shuttle DFW.”139
Other Tests of Employee Status
Another important test that some agencies rely on to 
determine whether a worker is an employee is known 
as the economic realities test, first used by the U.S. 
Supreme Court in a 1947 FLSA case and expressly 
adopted several decades later. The DOL’s guidance 
about the economic realities test lists six nonexhaus-
tive factors, none of which are dispositive (see the 
earlier discussion for details). 
In 2015, the DOL issued administrative guid-
ance, the thrust of which was that the “employee” 
classification should be “liberally construed to 
provide broad coverage for workers” because of the 
worry that employers were misclassifying employees 
as independent contractors to “cut costs and avoid 
compliance with labor laws.”140 In 2017, however, the 
DOL withdrew this guidance and cautioned that the 
legal responsibilities of employers stayed the same.141 
Courts are not required to follow DOL administra-
tive guidance, as the U.S. Supreme Court explained 
in 2000. Rather, “interpretations such as those in 
opinion letters—like interpretations contained in 
policy statements, agency manuals, and enforcement 
guidelines, all of which lack the force of law—do not 
warrant Chevron-style deference. They are ‘entitled 
to respect,’ but only to the extent that they are 
persuasive.”142 
As discussed earlier, the IRS uses another test 
to determine whether someone is an employee or an 
independent contractor. In the past, the IRS used a 
20-factor test, but, in 2004, it streamlined these factors 
into three broad categories labeled behavioral control, 
financial control, and relationship (described earlier). 
Although different agencies and courts use dif-
ferent tests to determine whether an individual is an 
employee or an independent contractor, it is clear 
that these tests are quite similar. In particular, the 
common-law test, economic realities test, and IRS 
three-factor test all rely on “control” as the first, and 
most important, factor.
The Consequences of Worker 
Misclassification
Misclassification is an important issue because 
employees generally receive more protections and are 
entitled to more benefits than independent contrac-
tors. The costs of misclassification for workers are 
potentially huge. On the other hand, employers who 
properly classify their workers are expected to face 
higher costs. As a New Jersey task force report on this 
issue explained,
For workers categorized as employees, an 
employer must provide workers’ compensation 
insurance, withhold federal income tax, make 
contributions to federal programs like Social 
Security and Medicare, and make contribu-
tions to state-run programs like unemployment

36
and disability. Employees are protected by 
social safety net programs like Social Security, 
unemployment, temporary disability, earned 
sick leave, and family leave insurance. For 
independent contractors, no taxes are withheld; 
no benefits are paid; neither unemployment 
nor disability contributions are made; and 
the employer is often not required to obtain 
workers’ compensation insurance. Employers 
misclassifying their employees as independent 
contractors neglect to pay as much as 30 per-
cent of payroll and related taxes otherwise paid 
for employees. Employers who use independent 
contractors do not pay unemployment, which 
results in law-abiding employers having to pay 
more to make up for the shortfall in the Unem-
ployment Trust Fund.143
In addition to the costs imposed on workers 
and compliant employers, the federal government 
loses substantial revenue because of misclassifica-
tion. Unfortunately, it has been decades since the last 
comprehensive study on the extent of revenue loss. In 
1984, the “IRS estimated that nationally about 15 per-
cent of employers misclassified a total of 3.4 million 
employees as independent contractors, resulting in 
an estimated revenue loss of $1.6 billion (in 1984 
dollars).”144 The number of misclassified workers has 
only increased. A 2000 DOL study found that 10 to 
30 percent of audited employers misclassified work-
ers, and a 2009 U.S. Government Accountability 
Office report noted that the number of employees 
found to be misclassified in state audits jumped by 
42 percent between 2000 and 2007.145 The drain of 
this misclassification on state and federal coffers is 
indisputably substantial. 
Classification History in California
Employee vs. Independent Contractor Tests in 
California
In California, the long-standing test for determin-
ing whether a worker is an employee is from S. G. 
Borello & Sons, Inc. v. Dept. of Industrial Relations, a 
workers’ compensation case. Borello is a multifactor 
test, in which the first factor is “whether the person 
to whom services is rendered has the right to control 
the manner and means of accomplishing the result 
desired.”146 The additional factors that may be con-
sidered are the following:
1. right to discharge at will, without cause; 
2. whether the one performing services is 
engaged in a distinct occupation or business; 
3. the kind of occupation, with reference to 
whether, in the locality, the work is usually 
done under the direction of the principal or by 
a specialist without supervision; 
4. the skill required in the particular 
occupation; 
5. whether the principal or the worker supplies 
the instrumentalities, tools, and the place of 
work for the person doing the work; 
6. the length of time for which the services are 
to be performed; 
7. the method of payment, whether by the time 
or by the job; 
8. whether or not the work is a part of the 
regular business of the principal; and 
9. whether or not the parties believe 
they are creating the relationship of 
employer-employee.147
Although set forth in a workers’ compensation 
case, the Borello factors constituted the common-law 
test in California and had been used in other con-
texts.148 This California test is very similar to the fed-
eral law tests detailed previously, in that it gives pri-
macy to the issue of control and emphasizes that many 
factors may be considered and none are dispositive.
In 2018, however, the Supreme Court of Califor-
nia issued a ruling replacing the multifactor test with 
the ABC test, which carries a presumption that the 
worker is an employee and puts the burden of prov-
ing otherwise on the hiring party. The ABC test had 
been adopted by several other states, often only in 
specific situations: Massachusetts,149 Connecticut,150 
Delaware,151 Illinois,152 Indiana,153 Nebraska,154 
Nevada,155 New Hampshire,156 New Jersey,157 
Vermont,158 Washington,159 and West Virginia.160 
According to the general counsel of the National 
Employment Law Project, however, Uber and Lyft 
have effectively negated enforcement of these laws in 
several of those states, through private arbitration.161

37
Under the ABC test, the employer must prove all 
of the following:
A. the worker is free from the control and 
direction of the hirer in connection with the 
performance of the work, both under the con-
tract . . . and in fact 
B. the worker performs work that is outside the 
usual course of the hiring entity’s business
C. the worker is customarily engaged in an 
independently established trade, occupation, 
or business of the same nature as the work 
performed for the hiring entity.162
The adoption of the ABC test by California was a big 
shift, making it more difficult for companies to clas-
sify workers as independent contractors. 
A subsequent case, Garcia v. Border Transpor-
tation Group, LLC, clarified that the ABC test set 
forth in Dynamex applies only to Industrial Welfare 
Commission wage order claims, and the Borello 
common-law test applies to other claims. As the 
court explained, 
[Dynamex] did not reject Borello, which 
articulated a multifactor test for determining 
employment status under the Worker’s Com-
pensation Act. Nor did it address the appellate 
court’s ruling that “insofar as the causes of 
action in the complaint . . . are not governed 
by the wage order” and predicated solely on 
the Labor Code, “the Borello standard is the 
applicable standard for determining whether a 
worker is properly considered an employee or 
an independent contractor.”163
In 2019, a panel for the U.S. Court of Appeals for 
the Ninth Circuit held that Dynamex applied retroac-
tively, as is the general rule. The panel explained that 
an exception to this is when a settled rule, on which 
the parties have relied, is changed, but that “Dynamex 
did not fabricate the ABC test anew, but instead care-
fully explained how the test remains ‘faithful . . . to the 
fundamental purpose of [California’s] wage orders.’”164 
The next important development in this area was 
the passage of AB5 later in 2019, codifying the ABC 
test and applying it to all provisions of the Labor 
Code and the Unemployment Insurance Act, unless 
a different definition of employee is ­provided.165 
Importantly, this provided an enforcement 
mechanism—the ability to sue noncompliant com-
panies166—which potentially gave the ABC test more 
teeth in California than in the other states where it 
had been implemented into law.
The bill (sponsored by the California Labor 
Federation) led companies, such as Uber and Lyft, to 
unsuccessfully attempt compromise to forestall its 
passage. Several companies entered into discussions 
with labor unions to come up with a legislative pro-
posal that would have given workers some employee 
benefits while allowing companies to continue to 
classify them as independent contractors, but those 
discussions ultimately went nowhere.167 Organiza-
tions, such as the California Chamber of Commerce, 
argued in support of these “on-demand” companies 
that such regulations would stifle innovation and 
ultimately harm consumers and workers.168
Many of the gig economy companies made 
clear that they would continue fighting against the 
bill—by pledging tens of millions of dollars in sup-
port of a planned ballot initiative that would exempt 
them from the law—but would offer drivers various 
concessions.169 Several companies and organiza-
tions filed lawsuits, including Uber, Postmates,170 
the American Society of Journalists and Authors, 
the National Press Photographers Association,171 the 
California Trucking Association, and the Western 
States Trucking Association.172 
Uber also said that it would litigate misclassi-
fication claims under arbitration.173 In a statement 
released on the same day that the passage of AB5 was 
announced, Uber’s chief legal officer, Tony West, reit-
erated that Uber’s yearlong attempt to compromise 
with drivers would have offered a minimum earnings 
standard, portable benefits, and “real sectoral bar-
gaining.” Uber’s proposal, the statement continues, 
“avoids the potential harm of forcing drivers to be 
employees, whether or not they want to—and the vast 
majority tell us they don’t want to be.”174 Importantly, 
Uber notes that AB5 does not automatically reclassify 
drivers as employees; it simply enshrines the ABC 
test. Finally, Uber committed to continue fighting 
for its compromise solution through the ballot ini-
tiative.175 In a news conference, West said that Uber 
would not treat its drivers as employees, because 
“drivers were not a core part of Uber’s business and

38
could maintain their independent status when the 
measure goes into effect.”176
The magnitude of the potential effects of AB5 
was the subject of some debate. Uber and Lyft 
warned that the bill would reduce drivers’ flex-
ibility because they would have to schedule drivers 
in advance, although experts counter that the bill 
does not require advance setting of shifts. One effect 
is that the ride-hailing companies may limit the 
number of drivers who can work in less busy areas or 
during less busy times. A law professor pointed out, 
however, that it would not make sense for Uber and 
Lyft to drastically limit driver’s flexibility and that it 
would still be in the companies’ interest to offer driv-
ers’ incentives to ensure that they had enough drivers 
to meet demand. Overall, though, industry officials 
estimate that the bill would increase costs for gig 
economy companies by 20 to 30 percent.177
Another important area to examine is state 
administrative agencies. A 2018 law review article 
claims that “most, but not all, states that have spoken 
on the issue concluded that the online platform com-
panies’ workers are ‘employees.’”178 With respect to 
California, the article notes that the California Labor 
Commission and California Unemployment Insur-
ance Appeals Board both found that Uber drivers are 
employees with regard to worker protection laws and 
unemployment insurance, respectively.179
Further Developments at the State Level
California is not the only example with changes in 
laws and regulations, and court rulings are chang-
ing the landscape of worker classification and the 
protections available to independent contractors. For 
example, Maryland passed a law in 2019 that expands 
the definition of employee to include independent 
contractors and allows independent contractors and 
employees of companies with as few as one employee 
to bring harassment claims.180 In August 2019, New 
York passed a law that, among other things, extended 
protection to independent contractors and domestic 
workers. Independent contractors had previously 
been protected from sexual harassment in the work-
place, but the law now protects these workers from 
unlawful discrimination “when the employer . . . 
knew or should have known that such non-employee 
was subjected to an unlawful discriminatory prac-
tice in the employer’s workplace, and the employer 
failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective 
action.”181 Vermont also passed a law enhancing pro-
tections for independent workers.182 
In November 2019, the New Jersey Department 
of Labor and Workforce Development determined 
that Uber owed $650 million in unemployment and 
disability insurance taxes.183 New Jersey, which uses 
the ABC test for wage and unemployment claims, 
has been pursuing Uber for several years. In 2015, 
the department obtained a court order against the 
company for $54 million for unemployment and tem-
porary disability insurance payments. That amount 
was not paid and is incorporated into the $650 mil-
lion judgment.184 As expected, Uber’s spokesperson 
responded to the news that it plans on challeng-
ing the “preliminary but incorrect determination” 
because Uber’s drivers are independent contractors, 
not employees.185
In contrast, Texas is an example of a state that 
has been eroding rights and protections for gig 
workers. In 2019, the Texas Workforce Commission 
adopted a rule that replaces its traditional 20-factor 
employee classification test focused on control with a 
new test specific to “marketplace contractors” for the 
purpose of unemployment insurance. Under the test, 
if the following nine conditions are met, the worker is 
not an employee, and the marketplace platform does 
not need to pay insurance:
1. All or substantially all of the money paid to 
the marketplace contractor is based on a per-
job or per-transaction basis;
2. The marketplace platform does not unilater-
ally prescribe specific hours during which the 
contractor must be available to accept service 
requests from the public (including third-party 
individuals and entities) submitted through 
the marketplace platform’s digital network;
3. The marketplace platform does not prohibit 
the marketplace contractor from using a digi-
tal network offered by any other marketplace 
platform;
4. The marketplace platform does not restrict 
the contractor from engaging in any other 
occupation or business;

39
5. The marketplace contractor is free from con-
trol by the marketplace platform as to where 
and when the contractor works and when the 
contractor accesses the marketplace platform’s 
digital network;
6. The marketplace contractor bears all, or 
substantially all, of the contractor’s own 
expenses that are incurred by the contractor in 
performing the service or services;
7. The marketplace contractor is responsible 
for providing the necessary tools, materials, 
and equipment to perform the service(s); and
8. The marketplace platform does not con-
trol the details or methods for the services 
performed by the marketplace contractor by 
requiring the contractor to follow specified 
instructions on how to perform the services;
9. The marketplace platform does not require 
the marketplace contractor to attend manda-
tory meetings or mandatory training.186
The rule change came after a big behind-the-
scenes push from lobbyists. In fact, Tusk Ventures, 
hired by the housecleaning platform Handy, wrote 
the draft that ultimately became the crux of the bill, 
as it has done in other jurisdictions. Furthermore, 
this rule, which may have widespread effects on 
workers, was not passed in the legislature but by “an 
opaque bureaucracy. There were no hearings where 
outsiders were questioned, no meaningful floor 
debates—just a few perfunctory statements at public 
meetings and a 30-day comment period before the 
agency could issue a final proposal.”187

About This Report
In recent decades, a variety of forces—rapid technological change, globaliza-
tion, and demographic shifts, among others—have contributed to changes in the 
nature of work in the United States and most other advanced economies. The 
transformation is marked by the perception that an increasing share of the work-
force is in nonstandard employment relationships as self-employed workers, inde-
pendent contractors, freelancers, contract workers, and temporary help employ-
ees. Yet, it is not clear whether the laws, regulations, and case law established in 
the industrial era—to protect workers and provide access to remedies when laws 
and regulations are violated—are sufficient in this new and continually evolving 
world of work. 
It is important for policymakers at the national and state levels to have objective 
information about how the changing nature of employment in the United States 
is affecting workers’ access to needed protections, to appropriate remedies when 
those protections fail, and to forms of insurance linked to traditional jobs. What 
are the policy considerations with respect to access to these forms of worker pro-
tection and social and private insurance with the changing nature of work? To 
address these issues, we developed a taxonomy for the nature of worker protec-
tions associated with traditional employment, assessed the size and trends in the 
key groups of workers who are excluded from these worker protections, and con-
sidered alternative approaches to ensuring that workers have access to the protec-
tions they need.
This study will be of interest to policymakers in the public and private sectors 
focused on the changing nature of work, on the implications for access to worker 
protections and other forms of benefits typically associated with traditional employ-
ment, and on strategies for ensuring that members of the workforce have access to 
protections that are valued by society, regardless of the nature of their work.
