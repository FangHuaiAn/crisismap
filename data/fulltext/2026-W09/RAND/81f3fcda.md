About This Report
In this report, we describe New York City Public Schools’ (NYCPS’s) implementation of the Next Generation 
Community Schools pilot program. NYCPS implemented this program in 20 elementary and middle schools 
that were designated as community schools in 2021 during the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 school years. The 
pilot program consisted of three academic enhancements to the traditional community schools strategy: 
(1) collaborative leadership development, (2) instructional leadership development, and (3) a co​­curricular 
enhancement. We use original data that we collected from surveys and interviews to explore the key barriers 
and enablers to implementing these enhancements in NYCPS’s specific context, and we offer considerations 
for other practitioners and policymakers considering similar academic enhancements in their community 
schools initiatives.

Summary
Community schools partner with community-based organizations and families to share leadership duties at 
schools and offer a wide array of services that support student learning and well-being. Community schools 
have expanded nationwide in the past few decades because they have proven to be a promising strategy for 
improving student outcomes, particularly in high-poverty communities.
New York City’s Community Schools Initiative (NYC-CSI) is the largest citywide system of community 
schools in the country. The initiative is a program of NYC Public Schools (NYCPS), and it serves almost 
200,000 students per year in more than 400 schools. Prior research on NYC-CSI shows that participation in 
the schools leads to positive impacts on attendance and academic achievement (Johnston et al., 2020; Covelli, 
Engberg, and Opper, 2025).
Historically, the community schools strategy focused on meeting students’ nonacademic needs as a pre-
requisite to academic success. With NYC-CSI well established, the NYC Office of Community Schools (OCS) 
(which is part of NYCPS) launched the Next Generation Community Schools (NGCS) pilot program in 2022 
to add three academic enhancements to the community schools strategy: (1) collaborative leadership devel-
opment, (2) instructional leadership development for school leaders, and (3) a cocurricular enhancement for 
kindergarten math. OCS implemented these three enhancements over a period of two years in 20 elementary 
schools that were designated as new community schools in fall 2021.
In this report on the program’s implementation, we draw on original surveys and interviews we con-
ducted across stakeholder groups (e.g., OCS staff, school district leaders, school leaders, and service 
providers). We examine how the pilot program was designed and rolled out, how schools implemented the 
enhancements, and what factors enabled or hindered implementation. This report is for OCS and should also 
be of interest to state and local leaders of community schools strategies who are interested in enhancing the 
academic components of their community schools strategies.
Key Findings
Our key findings are as follows:
•  A short rollout timeline contributed to logistical and staffing challenges, confusion about expectations 
for leaders at pilot schools, and a lack of coherence across the three enhancements, although this coher-
ence improved over time.
•  Implementing enhancements in newly established community schools rather than existing community 
schools hindered success; many schools began the pilot without the necessary staff and were still devel-
oping their understanding of the community schools strategy.
•  Staff from schools that participated in the pilot had overwhelmingly positive perceptions of the instruc-
tional leadership supports and found coaching to be an effective capacity-building mechanism.
•  During the pilot, school staff developed foundational understandings of collaborative leadership and 
were engaged in the critical collaborative leadership practice of including diverse stakeholder groups in 
school decisionmaking.
•  Interview participants reported that collaborative school leaders’ mindsets and trusting relationships 
among school leaders enabled the implementation of all three enhancements.
•  Many community school directors in the pilot schools lacked classroom teaching experience, which 
limited their capacity to effectively engage in the instructional leadership enhancement.

Recommendations
Our recommendations for OCS or other stakeholders are as follows:
•  Before launching a new pilot program, give adequate lead time to program leadership for coordinating, 
contracting, hiring, and onboarding to ensure clarity and coherence across program components and 
with other system-level priorities.
•  When piloting enhancements, select established community schools with evidence of success in using 
the community schools strategy rather than newly established community schools.
•  Intentionally design complementary program enhancements at the outset of a pilot and provide train-
ing and written guidance about how enhancements should fit together.
•  Select enhancements that align to the community schools strategy or adapt enhancements to the com-
munity schools strategy prior to implementation.
•  Invest in on-site and personalized coaching, which proved to be an effective capacity-building mecha-
nism during the NGCS pilot, to support academically oriented community schools.

CHAPTER 1
Introduction
Community schools have been around since the early 20th century and have grown in popularity since 
2008 as a strategy to mitigate some of the poverty-related challenges that undermine students’ readiness to 
learn. Community schools partner with local community-based organizations (CBOs) to provide additional 
services for students and families that traditional public schools typically do not offer, such as physical and 
mental health care services, support with food or housing insecurity, and other services that are targeted to 
each school community’s specific needs.
The community schools strategy is intentionally flexible so that each school can adapt to its community’s 
unique needs. However, many community schools operate within a commonly adopted strategy with four 
key pillars that focus on meeting students’ and families’ basic needs as a prerequisite to learning academically: 
(1) integrated student supports, (2) expanded learning time (ELT) and opportunities, (3) family and commu-
nity engagement, and (4) collaborative leadership and practice. As the community school movement matures, 
it has shifted toward an expanded framework that reflects an interest in directly supporting instructional 
change. This expanded framework incorporates a fifth and sixth pillar: (5) rigorous, community-connected 
classroom instruction and (6) a culture of belonging, safety, and care (Community Schools Forward, 2023).
Community schools typically have a dedicated staff member to oversee and facilitate the key pillars’ 
implementation; in New York City (NYC), these staff are referred to as community school directors (CSDs), 
and they work alongside principals and other members of school leadership. A growing body of evidence is 
finding that community schools are effective at improving student attendance and academic achievement 
(for a comprehensive review, see Maier et al., 2017), and states and districts across the country are increasing 
their investments in the model and expanding their community schools programming.
Serving almost 200,000 students per year in more than 400 schools across NYC’s five boroughs, the NYC 
Community Schools Initiative (NYC-CSI) is the largest citywide community schools program in the country. 
NYC-CSI started with an initial cohort of 45 schools in the 2014–2015 school year, and the program incre-
mentally grew and has shown positive academic and nonacademic outcomes for enrolled students (Johnston 
et al., 2017; Johnston et al., 2020; Covelli, Engberg, and Opper, 2025).
NYC-CSI originally focused on meeting students and families’ nonacademic needs, which followed the 
community schools strategy. With NYC-CSI well established, the program’s focus has evolved to include 
academic instruction. Specifically, the Office of Community Schools (OCS) developed a pilot program called 
Next Generation Community Schools (NGCS) to bring three academic enhancements to the community 
schools strategy. After selecting 20 elementary and middle schools for the program during the 2022–2023 
school year, NGCS piloted these enhancements during the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 school years. OCS col-
laborated with external partners to provide training and on-site coaching for each enhancement. The three 
enhancements and their corresponding service provider organizations were as follows:
•  Collaborative leadership development. This enhancement focused on helping principals and CSDs 
work collaboratively with each other and other community stakeholders to support student success. 
Change Impact, a social impact consultancy, supported the implementation of this enhancement.

•  Instructional leadership development. This enhancement focused on helping principals, CSDs, and 
other school leaders build their schools’ capacities to deliver high-quality instruction. The Relay Gradu-
ate School of Education (which we refer to as Relay or Relay Graduate School) supported the implemen-
tation of this enhancement.
•  Cocurricular enhancement for kindergarten math.1 This enhancement, called High 5s, focused on 
helping CSDs and other school staff provide high-quality cocurricular academic programming that 
aligned with schools’ instructional foci. The Bank Street College of Education (which we refer to as 
Bank Street) and the Youth Policy Lab at the University of Michigan supported the implementation of 
this enhancement.
Although previous studies have shown that community schools can improve both academic and non-
academic outcomes, as we discuss in Chapter 2, there is still limited evidence on how these schools can 
intentionally strengthen instruction while preserving the whole‑child approach that defines the strategy. 
The NGCS pilot tested how academic and leadership enhancements can integrate with existing community 
school designs. In this report, we describe the implementation of this pilot program using data from original 
surveys and interviews that we conducted of NGCS staff and other stakeholders. We address the following 
implementation research questions in this report:
•  How was the NGCS pilot designed and rolled out?
•  How did NGCS teams implement the three enhancements?

Community Schools Background
Although educators and policymakers have attempted various school initiatives and improvement efforts 
the past several decades, the community schools strategy distinguishes itself by emphasizing a whole-child 
approach using community resources as integrated student supports, also referred to as wraparound services, 
to facilitate student learning (Heers et al., 2016; Holme et al., 2022; Maier et al., 2017; Maier and Niebuhr, 
2021; Oakes, Maier, and Daniel, 2017). Using this approach helps fulfill students’ and their families’ unmet 
needs, and experts theorize that meeting these needs is a prerequisite to academic success. The aim of using 
this approach is to mitigate challenges that prevent children from being ready to learn. Typically, a dedi-
cated community school staff member, often known as a CSD, leads this work. A CSD’s role is to implement 
the strategy and establish and facilitate partnerships with CBOs that can provide the services that students 
and families need, such as after-school programming or mental and physical health care services. This staff 
member is a part of the school leadership team (SLT), collaboratively working side-by-side with the principal 
and other school leaders.
Proponents regard the community schools strategy as a way to revitalize the public school system, espe-
cially in neighborhoods considered traditionally underserved and in which most residents have low incomes 
(Holme et al., 2022). Although community schools’ roots trace back to the early 20th century, they have pro-
liferated more since 2008, likely in response to the unmet needs and outcomes of students in high-poverty 
areas who have less access to high-quality schools and face out-of-school barriers to learning (Maier et al., 
2017). In 2008, the federal government began funding the Full-Service Community School Program across 
all U.S. states (U.S. Department of Education, 2025). The expansion accelerated in response to the dispa-
rate impacts of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic; as of 2023, there were roughly 8,000 to 
10,000 community schools in the nation (California Teachers Association, undated).
The Four Pillars of the Community Schools Strategy
The community schools strategy uses a context-oriented approach, and thus the implementation differs by 
geography and, naturally, school context. However, most community schools operate within a common strat-
egy that is composed of four key pillars: (1) integrated student supports, (2) ELT and opportunities, (3) family

and community engagement, and (4) collaborative leadership and practice. Each pillar addresses an impor-

comprehensive review of community schools research led to scholars identifying and defining these pillars 
(Maier et al., 2017), and community schools strategies across the country have henceforth adapted these pil-

Integrated Student Supports
Staff at community schools coordinate services that support students’ mental and physical health and well-
being. These wraparound services can lower out-of-school barriers to learning by connecting students to 
such resources as social service providers and health care providers. The composition of these services varies 
because they are tailored to each community’s specific needs, but common examples include on-site physical 
and mental health care services, food pantries, clothing supply closets, and housing assistance. For example, 
a local hospital might partner with community schools to offer an on-site mobile clinic for routine health 
care once a month, or a local church might provide Thanksgiving meals to all families in a school. The dedi-
cated community school administrator (i.e., a community school coordinator or a CSD) typically coordinates 
these services by building relationships with external partners depending on the school community’s needs.
Family and Community Engagement
In community schools, students are not the only ones who receive wraparound services. These schools offer 
additional support and learning opportunities for adults, such as English-as-a-Second-Language classes, 
citizenship test preparation, technology courses, and mental and physical health care clinics. Families and 
community members participate in shared decisionmaking, which is intended to foster collective trust and 
improve the utility of these family and community services. For example, community schools might have 
parent representatives on leadership teams or have dedicated space on campus for a “parent center.” In many 
cases, these additional on-site services are accessible to all community members, not just families of enrolled 
students. This pillar is an acknowledgment that community engagement and well-being are important influ-
ences on student success.
Expanded Learning Time and Opportunities
Community schools sometimes operate with an extended school day and, sometimes, an extended school 
year. In addition, many community schools offer ELT during after-school, before-school, and weekend pro-
gramming. Community schools also prioritize other learning opportunities, such as field trips and summer 
events. The purpose of ELT and related opportunities is to provide additional academic practice and enrich-
ment from real-world experiences outside traditional school hours. Extended school hours also relieve 
working families’ child care burden because many families in community schools work nontraditional or 
extended hours.

Collaborative Leadership and Practice
Community schools aim to include the voices of all stakeholder groups in their decisionmaking processes, 
including school staff, students, families, community members, and external partners. Doing so enables staff 
to successfully implement other pillars by fostering an environment that is conducive to collective trust and 
shared responsibility. Strong collaborative leadership can help with building the capacity of teachers, admin-
istrators, families, and external partners to provide an environment that improves student learning and well-
being. Examples of this collaboration in practice includes community SLTs, additional paid collaborative 
planning time for school staff, and leadership opportunities for family members.
Community Schools Research
There is a large body of research on community schools’ effects on student outcomes, and findings are mostly 
positive. In a comprehensive review of 143 studies of community schools, Maier et al. (2017) concluded that 
the evidence that community schools improve both academic and nonacademic student outcomes is suffi-
cient to justify the strategy’s expansion. Another review of 19 experimental and quasi-experimental studies 
of integrated student supports found either positive or null results in all cases (Moore et al., 2017). Because of 
the strategy’s wraparound nature, evaluators have been interested in both academic and nonacademic out-
comes related to the implementation of community schools.
Researchers have explored community schools’ impacts on indicators of academic achievement. For 
example, the Boston City Connects community schools program led to higher grades and improved math 
and English Language Arts (ELA) test scores, and the program narrowed achievement gaps for English lan-
guage learners (ELLs) (Walsh et al., 2014). Researchers conducted three randomized-control trials of the 
Communities In Schools program in Texas, Kansas, and Florida; the researchers found that there were some 
positive but inconsistent impacts on math and reading scores (ICF International, 2010a; ICF International, 
2010b; ICF International, 2010c).2 A study of two Iowa-based community schools showed improved math and 
English grades but no change in test scores (LaFrance Associates LLC, 2005). An evaluation of the Tulsa Area 
Community Schools Initiative found overall null effects on test scores but also found variation in outcomes 
by the level that schools had implemented the community schools strategy (i.e., the degree to which each 
school had established full-service community schools practices) (Adams, 2010; Adams, 2019). A study of the 
California Community Schools Partnership Program demonstrated that there were positive impacts on math 
and ELA and that impacts were larger in schools with larger improvements in attendance (Swain et al., 2025).
Some researchers have studied community schools’ impacts on nonacademic achievement, addressing 
such areas as attendance, school climate, student and family engagement, and behavioral indicators. Some 
of these studies have found that community schools improve family engagement, reduce family stressors, 
and improve access to services specifically for families (Arimura and Corter, 2010; Olson, 2014). Other stud-
ies have shown improved school climate and relationships between adults and students (Durham, Shiller, 
and McDowell, 2024–25; Johnston et al., 2020; LaFrance Associates, LLC, 2005; Olson, 2014). Commu-
nity schools might also improve attendance and reduce chronic absenteeism (Arimura and Corter, 2010; 
Covelli, Engberg, and Opper, 2025; Dobbie and Fryer, 2011; ICF International, 2010b; Johnston et al., 2020; 
Kemple, Herlihy, and Smith, 2005; Olson, 2014; Swain et al., 2025). Evidence of community schools’ impact 
on discipline and behavior is mixed; some studies have shown a reduction in infractions (Dearing et al., 
2016; Dobbie and Fryer, 2011; Johnston et al., 2020; Walsh et al., 2014), and others have found null effects

The Next Generation Community Schools Pilot Sought to Develop an 
Academically Oriented Community Schools Strategy
The NGCS pilot was intended to integrate an academic focus into the broader community schools strategy. 
The idea came from a task force called the Task Force on Next Generation Community Schools that the 
Brookings Institution established in 2021. The task force argued that the scaling of community schools was 
a research-based strategy for addressing long-standing educational inequities and the challenges the pan-
demic wrought. A report by the task force identified seven reasons community schools are well positioned to 
address inequality and transform education, including that they improve teaching and learning and support 
collaborative leadership with the community (Task Force on Next Generation Community Schools, 2021). 
The report also highlighted community schools’ potential role in supporting pandemic learning loss recov-
ery because of the strategy’s focus on creating the preconditions necessary for learning (“Chancellor David 
C. Banks Announces Partnership with the Robin Hood Foundation,” 2022; Task Force on Next Generation 
Community Schools, 2021). After the report’s publication, OCS launched the NGCS pilot.
 With an academic focus in mind, OCS selected the three enhancements: collaborative leadership devel-
opment, instructional leadership development, and cocurricular enhancement. The inclusion of instruc-
tional leadership development stemmed from the pilot designers’ perception that there were not yet strong 
examples of community schools working intentionally toward academic success. In contrast, collaborative 
leadership—that is, collaborative engagement between CBO staff, CSDs, and principals—was already a foun-
dational component of the community schools strategy, as described in Chapter 2. Through the pilot, OCS 
hoped to strengthen collaborative practices and further support community school stakeholders to work col-
laboratively toward supporting student academics.
OCS selected the High 5s kindergarten math program as a cocurricular enhancement because High 5s 
had already been successfully implemented in some NYCPS schools and had led to improved student aca-
demic and attitudinal outcomes (Mattera, Jacob, and Morris, 2018). Moreover, because High 5s could be 
implemented by staff who are not teachers, the enhancement provided an opportunity for CBO staff to con-
tribute to academic enrichment at their schools.

Chapter Summary
In this chapter, we described the inputs and activities that comprised the NGCS pilot. Overall, the pilot sought 
to test how integrating academic enhancements within the community schools strategy could improve stu-
dent learning. To do so, the pilot leveraged partnerships with external service providers, which provided an 
array of PD opportunities to build NGCS staff’s capacity to engage stakeholders collaboratively, use instruc-
tional leadership practices, and provide cocurricular academic programming. Although all enhancements 
shared the common thread of building NGCS staff’s capacity, the prescriptiveness regarding each enhance-
ment’s implementation varied. Two enhancements—instructional leadership development and the High 5s 
club—had already been implemented successfully in other contexts. OCS hoped to use the pilot to learn 
more about successful practices underpinning a more academically oriented form of collaborative leadership. 
Importantly, although OCS provided NGCS with extensive supports to implement the enhancements, NGCS 
were new community schools and, therefore, worked to implement the core community schools strategy and 
enhancements simultaneously.

NGCS staff’s capacity to promote this alignment, although the trainings were not prescriptive about the spe-

How Next Generation Community Schools Implemented Collaborative

Next Generation Community Schools Staff Conceptualized Collaborative Leadership as the 
Inclusion of Multiple Voices in Decisionmaking
When asked about how CSDs conceptualized collaborative leadership, they discussed the importance of 
having multiple community stakeholders “at the table” to mutually develop strategies toward shared goals. 
CSDs mentioned a variety of stakeholders, including SLTs, CSDs, district staff, instructional staff, parents, 
and students. In particular, CSDs said that collaborative leadership requires humility and receptiveness from 
principals, whom CSDs said should not only be accessible to other stakeholders but also proactive about 
seeking input. Notably, CSDs’ conceptualizations were well aligned to the collaborative leadership pillar of 
the broader community schools strategy, which similarly emphasizes the inclusion of multiple stakeholder 
groups in schools’ decisionmaking processes.
Responses to our surveys of NGCS staff reflected this desire to include multiple stakeholders. When we 
asked CSDs and principals which key stakeholders they should include in school decisionmaking, nearly all 
respondents selected school leaders, teachers, and families. Most selected students as well. In Year 3 (2024–
2025), more NGCS staff than comparison school staff selected “other local community members” (45 percent 
and 26 percent, respectively; see Panel C in Table B.2 in Appendix B).
However, our survey data complicates our understanding of the extent to which NGCS implemented 
collaborative leadership practices. When asked about collaborative leadership practices, staff in comparison 
schools consistently reported higher rates of agreement with statements regarding their school’s collabora-
tive leadership practices than NGCS staff in both Years 2 and 3 (see Panel D in Table B.2 in Appendix B). 
Although this was initially a surprising finding, we hypothesize that NGCS staff might have reported fewer 
collaborative leadership practices because their training on the practices made them more aware of the 
extent to which such practices could improve. Responses from comparison schools’ staff reflected their 
existing understanding of collaborative leadership, which might have been less developed than that of NGCS 
leaders. This explanation might be why surveyed NGCS staff included more people on their lists of key deci-
sionmakers than comparison school staff did. However, we have no way of verifying whether our hypothesis 
is correct or whether these reported differences reflect true differences in implementation of collaborative 
leadership practices.
Responses from our interviews shed further light on why we might observe these differences between 
NGCS and comparison schools. Although some CSDs said that their notion of collaborative leadership did 
not shift over the course of the pilot, a few discussed how their understanding evolved over time. One CSD 
noted that they and other stakeholders, such as parents, had gained a deeper understanding of how they can 
contribute to collaborative leadership and the different facets of principals’ work. Another CSD said that they 
gained an appreciation for the difficulty of implementing collaborative leadership:
Well, collaborative leadership is not always easy. It takes a lot of give and take. It takes patience. It takes 
tenacity, it takes humbleness. It’s challenging work. When you say collaborative, it’d be easier for one person 
to make all the decisions and not consider the thoughts or the needs of others. But it takes more work for a 
collaborative group to come together and look at a situation or a need and be able to effectively input their 
thoughts and then still come out with a solution that works best for all.
Altogether, these results suggest that, although NGCS were new community schools, NGCS staff had 
developed foundational understandings of collaborative leadership and were engaging in the critical collab-
orative leadership practice of including diverse stakeholder groups in school decisionmaking. At the same 
time, by providing NGCS staff with a deeper understanding of the many ways in which collaborative leader-

staff explained ways that this alignment could manifest. For instance, they said that CSDs could translate 
their observations of instruction during the school day into instructional practices for the after-school pro-
gram leaders. CSDs could also analyze student assessment data to support instructional planning for OST 
programming. With the help of CSDs, OST programming could mirror the content or practices in students’ 
classrooms or support the development of skills that classroom teachers lack time to address. CSDs could also 
facilitate joint PD between school and CBO staff to ground them in school goals and priorities.
School-based NGCS staff provided illustrative examples of how academic alignment played out in their 
contexts. In interviews, they described using such strategies as joint goal-setting and information- and data-
sharing. For instance, one principal wanted to train after-school staff on the literacy strategies that teachers

the school to the different CBO partners, and (4) leveraging ELT or family programming to fill students’ aca-

reading skills). One district representative noted that they worked with assistant principals to ensure that 
data used during the school day and during after-school programming were aligned to support consistent 
measurements of students’ needs and progress across the two settings.
Some interviewees noted that the High 5s program—the third pilot enhancement—was an example of 
academic alignment because it provided students with an enrichment experience that aligned with the skills 
that students needed to learn. One CBO staff member remarked that the family math nights were another 
example of how community events and outreach could center on academics and the topics that students were 
learning in their classrooms.

Community Service Directors Experienced Challenges with Participating in the Instructional 
Leadership Supports
One goal of the pilot was to enhance CSDs’ capacity for and involvement in instructional leadership—a goal 
that was not explicitly articulated in the four pillars of the community schools strategy that NYC-CSI adopted. 
However, the degree to which CSDs received support varied significantly. One Relay interviewee explained 
that CSDs were invited to join Relay coaches during their on-site coaching visits, which included classroom 
walkthroughs, PD planning sessions, and analyses of student data. However, Relay coaches revealed that 
CSD participation in instructional coaching was not consistent across schools. Some Relay coaches said that 
they included the CSD in all coaching visits, others said that they attempted to include the CSD in initial 
visits, and still another said that they had never met the school’s CSD.
Interviewees described notable challenges to developing CSDs’ instructional leadership capacity. Inter-
viewees, including service providers, Relay coaches, OCS staff, and CSDs themselves, noted that one of the 
common challenges was the variation in CSDs’ instructional experience. Interviewees explained that CSDs 
who had prior teaching or instructional experience found it easier to engage in the instructional leadership 
training, whereas CSDs who lacked a strong instructional background tended to struggle. One service pro-
vider explained,
The tricky part of it is that some of [the CSDs] are more instructional people, [but] some of them have zero 
instructional [experience]. And so, it almost sometimes feels like more work for principals to figure out 
how to get the CSDs to engage if they don’t have the skill when principals themselves are trying to develop 
the skill and don’t have a lot of time.
Because of these challenges, Relay coaches described scenarios in which they attempted to include CSDs 
during their principal coaching. But Relay coaches found that, as one coach said, it “wasn’t a good use of 
time.” Even coaches who did include CSDs noted that they tried to be selective about the portions of their 
visits that would be useful to CSDs to ensure that the coaching was meaningful to them.

interviews, CSDs described a wide variety of professional backgrounds, including after-school directors, 
school parents, family outreach coordinators, and teachers, and some CSDs had significant prior experience 
in coaching staff. According to our survey, only 7 percent of NGCS CSDs had prior instructional experience, 
although a majority did have experience in after-school programming (see Panel A in Table B.1 in Appen-

ability to work collaboratively and effectively with youth, educators, and families. However, these resources 
did not specify an instructional background as a suggested criterion.

Most interviewees said that finding sufficient staff to serve as High 5s facilitators was a challenge to

That’s the biggest challenge, is making sure that you have enough personnel in the room on a consistent

Chapter Summary
Overall, we found that NGCS were engaging in the foundational work of the community schools strategy by, 
for instance, building routines to integrate multiple stakeholder groups into school decisionmaking, lever-
aging community resources to lessen or remove nonacademic barriers to student success, and providing 
expanded learning opportunities.
However, despite positive experiences with the OCS-provided training, NGCS experienced challenges 
with implementing the more academically focused aspects of the strategy, such as aligning academic learn-
ing between the school day and after-school programming, developing the instructional leadership capac-
ity of CSDs, and rolling out the pilot’s cocurricular enhancement. These challenges arose in part because 
NGCS staff were still developing an understanding of how these various aspects of the pilot should manifest 
within the context of the community schools strategy. Additionally, NGCS were not always staffed with the 
appropriate personnel to implement enhancements. For instance, CSDs often lacked the requisite experience 
to fully engage and participate in the instructional leadership enhancement, and NGCS struggled to find 
sufficient High 5s facilitators to run their math programs. Altogether, these findings suggest that, because 
these were new community schools, NGCS staff’s efforts might have been more focused on developing the 
foundational practices of the community schools strategy instead of implementing the enhancements. The 
implementation of the pilot enhancements varied across schools and depended on the school staff’s knowl-
edge and capacities.

CHAPTER 6
Crosscutting Barriers and Facilitators to Pilot 
Implementation Across the Enhancements
Our third research question is, “What were the barriers and enablers to consistent implementation that 
spanned across pilot enhancements?” In this chapter, we describe themes from the implementation that 
crossed various aspects of the logic model. Some themes relate to pilot inputs—that is, design decisions that 
led to implementation challenges at the provider and school levels. Examples include the decision to pilot the 
enhancements in newly designated community schools, a lack of coherence among the three enhancements, 
the relatively small scale of the pilot, and the lack of built-in mechanisms to enforce participation in pilot 
enhancements. Other themes relate to pilot activities and outputs, such as providers’ use of coaching as a 
capacity-building mechanism and NGCS teams’ response to coaching. As NGCS teams sought to implement 
the practices that were core to the pilot, they also encountered challenges, such as competing priorities, and 
enablers, such as positive mindsets from principals and CSDs and the development of trusting relationships 
among these leaders.
Barriers and Enablers Related to Pilot Inputs
Next Generation Community Schools Encountered Challenges with Rolling Out the Pilot in 
Part Because They Were New Community Schools
At the start of the pilot, NGCS encountered challenges to launching the pilot enhancements, which inter-
viewees attributed to NGCS’s status as new community schools and their limited supports for enacting the 
pillars of the community schools strategy. For example, it takes time for new community schools to contract 
with CBOs. According to interviewees, this led to a cascading set of delays. Without a set contract with a lead 
CBO, some NGCS were unable to hire CSDs, a role that underpins both the collaborative and instructional

the pilot enhancements. Interviewees said that these contracting issues pushed the initial and ongoing imple-

That whole first year was messy. And so, for me, it felt like . . . we’re six months behind where I think we

chose to not opt into the pilot. As described previously, OCS staff sought to assess schools’ commitment to

principals’ skills but not those of CSDs. Thus, although Relay’s supports were overall very well received, OCS

Relay’s proposed scope of work did not explicitly include building CSDs’ capacity for instructional leadership 
or alignment to a CBO’s work, both of which would have supported collaborative leadership implementation. 
Relay staff also echoed the idea of siloing: Some of the coaches we interviewed noted that they did not have a 
strong awareness of the work being done through the two other NGCS enhancements.

leadership enhancement. One service provider explained that, because High 5s was contained to kindergar-

When I’m working with a principal and I’m telling them what to prioritize, it can’t be just one grade . . . I 
think it’s a great program, but I just don’t understand why we would focus just on something that . . . impacts 
five kids in kindergarten and spend all that time. It makes no sense from a leadership lens.

District Leaders to Engage with the Pilot and Enforce School Participation
Nearly all OCS staff said that the pilot’s relatively small scale was an implementation challenge. OCS staff 
members explained that NGCS comprised only 20 out of more than 400 community schools in the district, 
making it difficult for senior district leaders (e.g., deputy chancellors or NYCPS department leaders) to take 
part in the pilot.

lack of commitment. One OCS staff member remarked that schools could opt in or out of any of the three 
NGCS enhancements with no consequences because neither OCS nor other district leaders had the capacity 
to compel schools to engage consistently.

At the start of the NGCS pilot, OCS experienced significant internal turnover—a contextual factor that inter-

those responsible for designing the pilot, left their roles shortly before the pilot began. As a result, the OCS 
staff tasked with implementing the pilot were not the same staff who designed the pilot. OCS staff members 
explained that this arrangement hampered their ownership of the pilot’s design. For instance, as described 
previously, OCS staff had to restrict pilot eligibility to new community schools, which the pilot’s funder 
directed, even if the OCS staff questioned this design choice.

Next Generation Community Schools Participants Viewed Coaching as an Effective

All three NGCS enhancements included some one-on-one coaching during the pilot. Whereas the instruc-

engage with a Change Impact coach to develop a site-specific plan for academic alignment.
Interviewees across role types emphasized the value of hands-on coaching for developing staff capacity.

coaching. Relay coaching included a mix of frequent virtual check-ins combined with extensive in-person

teacher feedback and facilitating data meetings. However, coaches’ interview responses suggested that their

coaching and focused site-specific support could have enhanced the benefits of the collaborative leader-

Barriers and Enablers Related to Implementation Outcomes
Competing Priorities Remained a Persistent Challenge Throughout the Pilot
CSDs and principals had many demands on their time, which made it difficult for them to engage in the 
pilot’s implementation. Nearly every CSD we interviewed said that they held many different responsibili-
ties at their schools, which made it harder for them to focus their time and attention on internalizing and 
implementing the pilot enhancements. Focusing on this implementation was even more difficult in schools 
or communities that were struggling with foundational operational challenges or needs, such as persistent 
leadership turnover, declining student enrollment, or families lacking basic necessities (such as food). As one 
district representative indicated, students and families need support meeting their basic needs, and the CSD 
is focused on that. 
The challenge of competing priorities was similarly acute for principals. All Relay staff we interviewed, 
as well as some OCS staff and district representative interviewees, described how principals were constantly 
“responding to the fires” in their schools, which often occupied the bulk of their time and attention. Inter-
viewees noted that this challenge was especially prevalent with new principals, principals of struggling 
schools, and principals of community schools. For instance, interviewees stated that principals in strug-
gling schools are usually preoccupied with issues relating to school culture, student behavior, or staffing

challenges, and principals of community schools have even more resources and programs to oversee than a 
principal of a typical school. Interviewees noted that, while Relay’s supports could help principals focus on 
instruction, principals need to resist allowing “all the noninstructional things [to] consume [their] time.” 
One Relay coach provided an example of how addressing a school’s operational challenges can dominate 
principals’ time:
The last time I went, [the principal] was missing a secretary, and so he was kind of having to answer a lot

Competing priorities were also a challenge when implementing the collaborative leadership enhance-
ment. OCS staff said that implementing the collaborative leadership enhancement might be affected more by 
competing priorities than implementing the instructional leadership enhancement because principals likely 
see instructional leadership as more central to their work as school leaders.
Interviewees noted that principals could lighten the demands on their time by delegating responsibilities 
to other leaders in their schools; however, for some principals, this might require a more-fundamental shift 
in their leadership approaches.
District initiatives and messaging can heighten—or alternatively, ameliorate—the conflict that schools 
experience when juggling their multiple priorities. Interviewees noted that NYCPS schools are subject to 
many different state priorities and district mandates (e.g., NYC Reads, a science-of-reading-focused initiative 
to improve the reading curriculum and instruction in NYCPS, launched in 2023). One district representa-

help NGCS staff understand how these different priorities work together. However, supporting this align-
ment would likely require knowledge of the NGCS’s contexts—knowledge that was not always accessible 
to the organizations supporting NGCS. Service providers noted that priorities varied by district, and they 
sometimes had little context for understanding what those priorities were. One Relay staff member said, “I 
just felt like I was just dropped into this district in the school without [the] full context of what’s at stake or 
what’s going.”
As an example of how aligned district priorities facilitated the pilot’s implementation (and could facili-
tate future pilots), one interviewee noted that the schools that most successfully engaged in the instructional 
leadership enhancement were in districts in which the superintendent supported the instructional leader-
ship practices.
Misalignment between a pilot’s, school’s, and district’s priorities can also lead to confusion about what 
school staff should prioritize. One service provider said that, during one of their virtual PD sessions, CSDs 
appeared to be simultaneously attending a different virtual PD session, suggesting that CSDs did not receive 
direction from their principal on which session to attend. A CSD echoed this sentiment, noting that they 
lacked clarity of when trainings would occur or which trainings were mandatory. This CSD said that, in mul-
tiple instances, they were required to be at different trainings at the same time. Another interviewee said that 
superintendents’ lack of involvement in the pilot could make it harder for principals involved in future pilots 
to prioritize instructional leadership.

CHAPTER 8
Conclusions and Recommendations
The NGCS pilot was one of the first attempts, to our knowledge, to introduce academic enhancements into 
a well-established community schools strategy. As community schools programs continue to expand across 
the country, taking the step to focus on academics within the community school strategy is important.
Rolling out the three pilot enhancements during such a short timeline proved challenging for the 20 newly 
established community schools. The short timeline created logistical and staffing challenges, unclear expec-
tations, and limited coordination at the pilot’s start.
Implementation was further hindered by staff turnover, capacity constraints, and mismatches between 
staff experience and the instructional demands of adding academic enhancements to the community schools 
strategy. Coherence among the three pilot enhancements improved over time as service providers began col-
laborating. But stronger planning at the outset could have better linked the enhancements and aligned them 
with existing district priorities and the community school context.
Despite the implementation challenges, NGCS staff were pleased with the instructional leadership and 
collaborative leadership training they received from the pilot. Coaching proved to be an effective capacity-
building mechanism for implementing the pilot enhancements. Additionally, OCS staff learned many useful 
lessons to guide their ongoing work with community schools. Altogether, our findings highlight the impor-
tance of ensuring readiness, coherence, and contextual alignment when introducing new enhancements to 
the four-pillar community schools strategy. In this chapter, we offer more-detailed recommendations for 
other community school leaders hoping to increase their focus on academics and instruction.
Assess and Build Readiness of School and Program Leaders Prior to 
Implementation
NYCPS implemented the NGCS pilot in newly designated community schools. The coordination, contract-
ing, hiring, and onboarding required to (1) include 20 new schools in NYC-CSI and (2) secure three new 
service providers with years-long contracts to support the pilot (through training and other activities) were 
large endeavors, making the full implementation a massive effort. The scale of work just to establish the pilot 
was likely a key contributing factor to the challenges OCS faced in identifying and contracting with lead 
CBOs, staffing the new roles associated with the pilot, coherence among the three enhancements, and clarity 
regarding expectations. With further development of the necessary preconditions for implementing NYC-
CSI, OCS might have avoided some of the pitfalls related to the quick stand-up of the program.
NYCPS implemented NGCS in new community schools to provide a clean slate for launching an enhanced 
version of the community schools strategy. However, this decision led to additional responsibilities and 
changes that prevented the pilot’s implementation from occurring on schedule. For example, when the pilot 
began, some schools still had not identified a lead CBO to partner with and therefore had not yet hired a CSD. 
Other community school program leaders should consider identifying well-established community schools 
that have a strong foundation of implementing the strategy’s four key pillars before adding enhancements.

Assessing readiness for implementation does not just apply to the schools, but it also applies to district 
leaders. In the case of NGCS, OCS staff experienced turnover prior to the pilot’s launch; the turnover was 
such that the pilot’s developers were no longer employed by NYCPS when the pilot began. This, too, contrib-
uted to a lack of coherence and clarity on the purposes of the three enhancements. Additionally, OCS staff 
expressed in interviews that, despite efforts to recruit schools that were interested and committed to partici-
pating in the pilot, no formal systems were in place to ensure that NGCS staff participated fully in the pilot 
program. What is more, OCS was limited in its ability to compel NGCS staff to comply with the level of par-
ticipation that the pilot’s designers had expected.
Finally, leaders of community schools programs should consider the readiness and fit of individual school 
staff members to lead the implementation of new enhancements. In NGCS, CSDs were hired by and techni-
cally employees of lead CBOs. CSDs were not recruited, hired, or compensated by NYCPS. This approach led 
to the hiring of CSDs with little to no background in instructional leadership; despite this, the CSDs were 
expected to co-lead instruction alongside principals. The experiences that principals, CSDs, and instruc-
tional leadership coaches had during the implementation demonstrated that many CSDs did not have the 
necessary instructional leadership skills to effectively engage in coaching with Relay and meaningfully con-
tribute to instructional leadership in their schools. In many other community school initiatives, CSDs are 
promoted as veteran, certificated teachers at the school they work at, and they have long-standing ties to the 
community, which might facilitate the smoother integration of CSDs into both instructional and collabora-
tive leadership roles.
In sum, the findings from our implementation study of the NGCS pilot suggests that important precondi-
tions might include (1) well-established community schools, (2) staff with relevant background experience 
related to the pilot enhancements, (3) strong buy-in of school leaders, (4) stable staffing at both the system 
and school levels, and (5) sufficient time to ensure that all the preconditions are in place prior to implementa-
tion. Leaders of other community schools programs should consider these and other preconditions necessary 
for success in their own contexts.
Design for Coherence Both Within the Program and Across Other District 
Priorities
If multiple program components need to be implemented simultaneously, which was the case with the NGCS 
pilot, planners should ensure that the components complement each other. For NGCS, there was minimal 
coordination during the planning phase with regards to the three enhancements. This led to a lack of clarity 
about the rationale for grouping those three enhancements and how they should fit together, and it led to a 
lack of coherence across the service providers. Over the course of the pilot, the service providers began to col-
laborate, and this improved the pilot’s coherence. Intentional collaboration established during the planning 
phase could increase coherence across a new initiative’s moving parts.
Equally critical is aligning pilot programs with existing school district priorities. For example, tensions 
can arise for school staff when a district’s focus is on improving literacy yet the selected pilot emphasizes 
mathematics. As another example, school and district leaders were not always aligned in what and how much 
they understood about the pilot. Some superintendents were unaware of which schools were NGCS, and the 
districts and OCS did not coordinate when hiring new principals. Scheduling conflicts, such as overlapping 
mandatory PD sessions (from NGCS and the districts), further complicated implementation. Establishing 
strong infrastructure and communication channels between NYCPS leadership, superintendents, and school 
leaders at the outset can help coordinate these efforts, reducing friction and thereby improving the likelihood 
of success.

Conclusions and Recommendations
47
Adapt Academic Initiatives to Fit Within the Community Schools Strategy
When introducing academic initiatives into a community school context, it is important to consider how 
these initiatives align with the school’s existing structure. The Relay training had not been applied in a com-
munity school context in which a CSD was expected to participate in training and instructional planning. 
Taking these differences into consideration in advance of implementation would have allowed Relay to better 
adapt its training strategy to the community school context.
Additionally, academic enhancements that impose a narrow or prescriptive program, such as the cocur-
ricular kindergarten math enhancement, might not be best suited for integration into the community schools 
strategy. Community school enhancements should empower schools to integrate academics in ways aligned 
to community needs, or they at least might need to be sufficiently adapted to the community context prior to 
implementation. The pilot’s top-down design is not aligned with the community-based approach of the com-
munity schools strategy. Future pilots to enhance the academic components of community schools should 
consider a school-level codesign approach to program development.
However, the need for flexibility in community schools might be one reason that site-based coaching was 
one of the pilot’s effective capacity-building mechanisms. The personalized attention that coaches provided 
to individuals during site visits was a flexible way in which coaches met individuals’ unique needs (e.g., prin-
cipals with different levels of instructional leadership experience, CSDs with different levels of collaborative 
leadership experience) within the larger framework of the enhancements. This suggests that community 
schools should invest in on-site and personalized coaching to support academically oriented community 
schools.
Thoughtful sequencing of enhancements may also help integrate them into the community schools strat-
egy. For example, one OCS interviewee reflected that, of the three enhancements, it might have been more 
effective to have started with collaborative leadership training. If collaborative leadership practices were 
well developed and well established, especially with principals and CSDs, there would have been a stronger 
foundation on which to layer instructional leadership training. Then, potentially, the CSD’s instructional 
leadership skills would be stronger, helping them oversee the staffing and implementation of the High 5s 
program.

APPENDIX D
Research Methodology
Survey Methodology
In spring 2024 (in Year 2) and spring 2025 (in Year 3), we administered a survey to all NGCS principals, 
CSDs, and High 5s facilitators (i.e., school-based staff who ran the cocurricular enhancement, a kindergar-
ten math club). Each year, the survey administration window lasted approximately six weeks. The survey 
was designed to take no more than ten minutes to complete and contained questions about four main topics, 
each their own section: (1) respondent background, (2) collaborative leadership practices and related train-
ing from Change Impact, (3) instructional leadership practices and related training from Relay, and (4) the 
kindergarten High 5s math program and related training from Bank Street. Principals and CSDs received 
questions about their experiences with both collaborative and instructional leadership supports, and 
High 5s facilitators received questions only about High 5s. If principals or CSDs indicated that they partici-
pated in High 5s training from Bank Street, they also received questions about High 5s.
To compare collaborative and instructional leadership practices between NGCS and non-NGCS commu-
nity schools, we also administered a modified version of the survey to principals and CSDs in community 
schools that were eligible to apply to the NGCS pilot program but either did not apply or applied and were not 
selected. In the comparison survey, we gave principals and CSDs the same survey items about collaborative 
leadership and instructional leadership practices, but we did not give them items about the enhancements in 
the pilot program, such as trainings and High 5s. We used data from publicly available files (e.g., files from 
NYC Open Data and the NYCPS website) to explore whether student demographics differed between NGCS 
and comparison schools; we found that the demographics were roughly equivalent. Although data from com-
parison schools can serve as useful reference points for a “business as usual” alternative, any differences in 
survey responses between NGCS and comparison schools are descriptive and not causal in nature.
Participation in the survey was voluntary; therefore, response rates of some groups were low, and responses 
might not represent the entire NGCS program or the entire group of comparison schools for either Year 2 or 
Year 3. Table D.1. shows the total sample and response rates for NGCS and comparison school staff by year. 
Because there might have been multiple respondents within a given school, we interpret all survey statistics 
to represent the average respondent experience as opposed to the experience of the average school.
Throughout this report, we presented survey data to show trends from and changes over time in the 
NGCS pilot. The survey data presented in our findings are not comprehensive, and we selectively reported 
them as they were useful. See Appendix B (for 2025 results) and Appendix C (for 2024 results) for complete 
summary statistics across all survey items and years for both NGCS and comparison schools.

Methodology Limitations
Our analysis is subject to several limitations. First, for some role types, we interviewed a very small number 
of individuals despite our outreach efforts. Most individuals who we reached out to for participation did not 
respond to our requests. Similarly, our survey data captured a limited number of respondents, especially in 
our first year of data collection. It is possible participants might have held different perspectives than those 
who declined participation, and we have no way of knowing whether participants had systematically differ-
ent experiences than nonparticipants. Second, all survey and interview data represent self-reported percep-
tions. Both the surveys and interviews captured participants’ perspectives about NGCS trainings, how NGCS 
enhancements were being implemented, and the factors that facilitated or challenged implementation. We 
could not independently validate these perceptions. Third, we did not include teachers—primary agents of 
instructional change—in our sample because they did not directly receive any of the service provider train-
ings as part of the pilot. Therefore, we did not directly measure teacher perceptions or practices; rather, we 
inferred these perceptions from interviewees’ responses. And finally, we did not interview school staff from 
comparison schools; as a result, we have limited insight into why we observed some differences in survey 
responses from NGCS and comparison school staff.
