About This Report
In this report, we present state-level estimates related to veterans’ health, well-being, and economic condi-
tions. These estimates are derived from several existing data sources. These estimates are presented to help 
inform planning, program development, and resource allocations at the state level to improve the welfare of 
the U.S. veteran population. These findings will be of interest to policymakers, government officials, veteran-
aligned nonprofit organizations, and members of the public who are interested in improving the well-being 
of veterans.
RAND Epstein Family Veterans Policy Research Institute
This work was conducted within the RAND Epstein Family Veterans Policy Research Institute, which is 
dedicated to conducting innovative, evidence-based research and analysis to improve the lives of those who 
have served in the U.S. military. Building on decades of interdisciplinary expertise at RAND, the institute 
prioritizes creative, equitable, and inclusive solutions and interventions that meet the needs of diverse vet-
eran populations while engaging and empowering those who support them. For more information, visit ­ 
veterans.rand.org or email veteranspolicy@rand.org.

CHAPTER 1
Background
In this report, we present several measures of veteran service needs for each of the 50 states and the District 
of Columbia. These state-specific profiles are important because veterans’ needs vary substantially across 
states, and these differences should be considered when funding, designing, or implementing services and 
benefits designed to improve the lives of veterans. The report is intended primarily for individuals involved 
in decisions relevant to state and local programs that are meant to benefit veterans.
A substantial number of the programs designed to assist veterans are planned, funded, or implemented 
within states or similar geographic regions. Some of these geographically specific programs are operated by 
state agencies or state-specific nonprofits. At the same time, national programs operated by the U.S. Depart-
ment of Veterans Affairs (VA) or by national veteran-serving nonprofits may distribute their resources at 
the state or regional Veterans Integrated Service Networks (VISN) level. Because of this factor, officials in 
VA, state legislatures, state veterans departments, and veteran-aligned nonprofits routinely need to make 
resource-allocation decisions at the state level to address the full range of veteran needs within that state. 
However, the challenges facing veterans can vary tremendously across states. For example, Morral, Schell, 
and Scherling (2024) show that the risk for firearm suicide varies by almost an order of magnitude across 
states. Similarly, estimates of veterans’ unmet need for caregiving varies by more than a factor of two across 
states (Ramchand et al., 2024), as does veterans’ current cigarette use (McDaniel and Klesges, 2019). However, 
almost all published studies estimating the needs of veterans have looked exclusively at national samples of 
veterans and often only look at veterans who use VA services. Because of the national focus and the non-
representativeness of studying VA-involved veterans, empirical guidance for state-level decisions is often very 
limited, which may impede good resource-allocation and program-development decisions. 
To help inform state-level policy, some organizations have funded their own veterans’ needs assessments. 
When the New York State Health Foundation wanted empirical guidance on making veteran-focused invest-
ments, it funded research to assess the needs of New York veterans (e.g., Farmer et al., 2011; Ringel et al., 
2024). Similarly, several other states have conducted their own needs assessments to help inform their vet-
erans’ program development and investments, including California (California Department of Veterans 
Affairs, 2011), Massachusetts (Dalzell et al., 2025), and Texas (Center for Social Inquiry, Texas State Univer-
sity, 2016). Unfortunately, these state-specific needs assessments are often quite expensive and might not be 
feasible for smaller states. 
Even when state-specific needs assessments have been conducted, they often have limitations that make 
them less useful. They might assess only a small or unrepresentative subpopulation of veterans, for example, 
college students, women, or those who already receive particular veterans’ services. 1 Regardless of who exactly 
is assessed, these data are also expensive to keep updated to document changes in veterans’ needs over time. 
This report is designed to help fill this knowledge gap using several existing, population-representative 
data sources. The use of existing data sources has several advantages because it does not require expensive

We hope that these state-level estimates can help government officials and aligned nonprofits make better

more—and more effectively—in programs promoting veterans’ well-being.

Data and Methods
Data Sources
We drew on four publicly available federal data sources to construct our well-being measures. These data 
sources were selected because they are some of the only data sources that contain sufficiently large samples 
of veterans within each state to allow reliable, veteran-specific estimates on topics related to health and eco-
nomic well-being. 
Veteran Population Projection Model
For each state and the District of Columbia, we summarized the veteran population size and demographic 
characteristics using information from the Veteran Population Projection Model 2023 (VetPop2023). This 
is the model developed by VA to estimate the size and characteristics of the veteran population using a mix 
of administrative records and actuarial data. For more information, see VA National Center for Veterans 
Analysis and Statistics (2025). We present the total size of the veteran population, as well as the percentages 
of those who are female, who are older than 65, and who served in the military after the terrorist attacks of 
September 11, 2001. These values were estimated as of September 30, 2025. For calculating the percentage of 
the adult population (18 or older) who were veterans, we used estimates of the general adult population as of 
July 2024, the most-recent state-level estimates available from the U.S. Census Bureau.
American Community Survey 
Conducted annually by the U.S. Census Bureau, the American Community Survey (ACS) is a nationally rep-
resentative survey of approximately 3.5 million addresses. This survey provides detailed demographic, social, 
economic, and housing data on the country’s population. Information is collected on each person living at 
the address. The ACS is administered on a rolling basis throughout the year using a three-phased approach. 
Each month, a systematic sample of addresses is drawn to receive the survey. The addresses receive an initial 
mailing with information directing them to an internet questionnaire. If there is no online response within 
approximately two weeks, a second mailing is sent; it contains a paper questionnaire. After two months, 
a sample of nonresponding mailable addresses, as well as unmailable addresses, is selected for in-person 
follow-up by Census Bureau fieldworkers using computer-assisted personal interviews. The overall response 
rate for the 2023 ACS was 84.7 percent (U.S. Census Bureau, undated).
We used individual-level information available through the 1-year Integrated Public Use Microdata 
Sample (IPUMS) (Ruggles et al., 2025) to construct our estimates. The 2023 survey was the most recent 
1-year IPUMS available at the time of our analysis, and it includes 190,258 veterans from all 50 states and 
Washington, D.C.

Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System 
The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) survey, conducted annually by the U.S. Centers 
for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and participating states and territories, collects information on 
health risk behaviors, chronic conditions, and the use of preventive health services among noninstitutional-
ized adults. All of the questions that we used to construct our well-being measures were drawn from the core 
BRFSS module that is administered in all participating jurisdictions.1 Since 2011, BRFSS has relied on a dual 
sampling frame of landline and cellular telephones. Data were collected using computer-assisted telephone 
interviews. The combined response rate on states’ surveys ranged from 21.7 percent to 66.8 percent during 
this period.
To improve the precision of our state-level estimates of the veteran population, we aggregated the 2022, 
2023, and 2024 BRFSS surveys. All 50 states and the District of Columbia participated in the 2022 and 2024 
surveys. In 2023, Kentucky and Pennsylvania were unable to collect large-enough sample sizes to be included 
in the CDC’s public use file. Because we are combining estimates across years and the standard weights pro-
vided by CDC were designed to be representative within each year, we slightly rescaled the standard BRFSS 
weights. Specifically, we adjusted the weights so that each year contributed equally to the estimates by divid-
ing each respondent’s original weight by their state-year’s mean weight. In total, the aggregation yielded a 
sample of 154,515 veterans; this aggregation was designed to be representative of the veteran population 
across all 50 states and the District of Columbia. 
National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report
State suicide rates were derived from data published by VA as part of its annual suicide report (VA, Office 
of Suicide Prevention, 2024). The rates were averaged over the three-year period from 2020 through 2022 by 
taking the total number of veteran suicides that had been found by VA researchers within the National Death 
Index during that period, then dividing by the sum of the veteran population across those same years. State 
veteran populations were estimated with the VetPop2023 model. 
Measures of Veteran Needs
Using these federal data sources, we estimated a variety of different measures of veterans’ well-being for each 
state and the District of Columbia. Within this report, we focus on outcome measures that were assessed in 
the same way across all 50 states and District of Columbia and that were sufficiently common that their prev-
alence could be reliably estimated with the available sample sizes. Measures were selected that correspond 
to the domains of needs that are commonly addressed in veterans’ programs or benefits, including mental 
health, physical health, employment, college education, and housing. 
Having Fair or Poor Self-Rated Health
From the BRFSS, we used responses to a self-reported measure of general health. Respondents were asked, 
“Would you say that in general your health is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” This measure or 
similarly worded variants are common metrics of health (e.g., Palladino et al., 2016; Ware and Sherbourne, 
1992) and have been repeatedly found to be strong and independent predictors of mortality across several 
populations (e.g., Burström and Fredlund, 2001; Ganna and Ingelsson, 2015). We calculated the percentage

Average Number of Days of Bad Mental Health in the Past Month
From the BRFSS, we used responses to a self-reported measure of mental health. Respondents were asked, 
“Now thinking about your mental health, which includes stress, depression, and problems with emotions, for 
how many days during the past 30 days was your mental health not good?” Responses were numeric from 1 
through 30. This item has been part of CDC assessments of health-related quality of life since 1993 and has 
been widely used in the scientific literature (e.g., Moriarty, Zack, and Kobau, 2003; Zahran et al., 2005). Using 
responses to this item, we calculated the average number of bad (“not good”) mental health days reported by 
veterans. This measure was averaged over a three-year period from 2022 through 2024.
Daily Smoking
From the BRFSS, we used a derived measure of smoking behavior constructed by the CDC. Daily smokers 
were defined as respondents who answer “Yes” to the question, “Have you smoked at least 100 cigarettes in 
your entire life?” and who answer “Every day” to the question, “Do you now smoke cigarettes every day, some 
days, or not at all?” We calculated the percentage of veterans in the state who smoke daily. This measure was 
averaged over a three-year period from 2022 through 2024.
Binge Drinking
From the BRFSS, we assessed the percentage of respondents who reported binge drinking in the prior 30 
days. Binge drinking was defined by the CDC based on two items. The first item was, “During the past 30 
days, how many days per week or per month did you have at least one drink of any alcoholic beverage? (A 
40-ounce beer would count as 3 drinks, or a cocktail drink with 2 shots would count as 2 drinks.)” Respon-
dents who answered that they had had a drink in the past month were asked the follow-up, “Considering all 
types of alcoholic beverages, how many times during the past 30 days did you have 5 or more drinks for men 
or 4 or more drinks for women on an occasion?” This is the standard method to assess binge drinking across 
a range of studies and has been found to be associated with risk for motor vehicle accidents, birth defects, 
cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality (e.g., Carpenter and Dobkin, 2009; Mathurin and Deltenre, 
2009; Wood, 2007). Respondents who answered that they had done so at least once in the past month were 
classified by the CDC as binge drinkers. This measure was averaged over a three-year period from 2022 
through 2024.
Being Overweight or Obese
BRFSS collected the self-reported height and weight of respondents and derived their body mass index (BMI). 
Individuals with BMI greater than or equal to 25 were classified by the CDC as overweight or obese. This 
criterion corresponds to the standard measure used across a range of studies and has been found to be asso-
ciated with an variety of health problems, resulting in substantially disability and reduced life expectancies 
(e.g., Abdelaal, le Roux, and Docherty, 2017). We calculate the percentage of veterans in each state who were 
overweight or obese. This measure was averaged over a three-year period from 2022 through 2024.

Having a Service-Connected Disability Rating
As defined by VA, “a service-connected disability is an injury or illness that was incurred or aggravated 
during active military service” (U.S. Department of Defense, 2020). For each person described as a veteran, 
the ACS asked, “Does this person have a VA service-connected disability rating?” which had “Yes” and “No” 
response options. We calculated the percentage of veterans in each state with a reported disability rating. 
This measure was taken in 2023.
Suicide Rate
The three-year average suicide rates for veterans in each state were expressed as suicides per 100,000 person-
years (i.e., the number of suicides over the three-year period divided by the number of veterans residing in 
the state in each year summed over the same three years). This measure was an averaged over a three-year 
period from 2020 through 2022. The District of Columbia’s suicide rate is not reported because these data 
were not provided in the underlying VA data. This is due to privacy concerns about presenting suicide counts 
of less than ten within any subpopulation. We can, however, infer from this suppression that the suicide rate 
in Washington, D.C., was less than the national average. 
Having Family Income Under 200 Percent of the Federal Poverty Level
From the ACS, we calculate the percentage of veterans whose families’ incomes were under 200 percent of the 
federal poverty level (FPL). Total family income represented the total self-reported pretax income of related 
individuals living together in a veteran’s household. IPUMS derived the income relative to the FPL using data 
on family size, number of children, and age of the head of the household (over or under 65). This measure was 
taken in 2023.
Unemployment Rate
From the ACS, we calculated the percentage of veterans in each state’s labor force who were employed. Being 
employed was defined as having done any work for pay in the past week as of the time of the survey. Individu-
als who were neither employed nor looking for work (i.e., individuals out of the labor force) were excluded 
from the calculation. Thus, the large proportion of veterans who are retired were not included in these cal-
culations. This is the definition of unemployment that is used for the U.S. official unemployment rate, called 
the U-3 measure by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. This measure was taken in 2023.
Having Housing Costs of More Than 30 Percent of Income
From the ACS, we calculated housing cost burden as the percentage of pretax household income spent on 
housing. Individuals whose households were spending more than 30 percent of their income on housing were 
considered housing cost burdened. For occupants of rental units, housing costs included contract rent and 
the additional costs of water, gas, electricity, and fuel. For owner-occupied units, housing costs included the 
cost of mortgages or similar debts; real estate taxes; fire, hazard, and flood insurance on the property; utili-
ties, such as gas, water, and sewer; and fuels (IPUMS USA, undated). This measure was taken in 2023.
No Bachelor’s Degree
From the ACS, we calculated the percentage of veterans who had not attained a bachelor’s or graduate degree. 
This measure was taken in 2023.

Statistical Analyses
Statistical analyses were conducted in the R statistical programming language. All analyses on ACS and 
BRFSS were conducted using the survey (version 4.4-8) and srvyr (version 1.3) packages (Freedman et al., 
2024; Lumley, Gao, and Schneider, 2024) and accounted for the sample design and survey weights when esti-
mating means and CIs. Variances and CIs were calculated using Taylor series linearization for the means of 
continuous variables and logistic regression for proportions of dichotomous variables. Three years of BRFSS 
data were combined in our estimates. To do this, the survey weights for each year were rescaled so that 
respondents in each year within that state were given equal weight on average. 
Each data page presents the estimates for each of these measures and information about how that state (or 
jurisdiction, in the case of Washington, D.C.) compares with the national average for its veteran population. 
The leftmost column of each data page names the measure. The second column includes a range plot, where 
the point at the center represents the estimated average for the state’s veteran population, and lines represent 
the 95-percent CI for the estimate. A vertical reference line in the center of these plots indicates the national 
average for veterans, and that average value is reported below the line. Estimates to the left of the national 
average line indicate that the state’s veteran population has better well-being (lower need) on that measure 
compared with the national average, while estimates to the right of the national average line indicate that the 
state’s veteran population has worse well-being (higher need) compared with the national average. The third 
column reports the estimated state mean or percentage for that measure. The fourth column reports the 
state’s rank compared with other states with the highest well-being (lowest need) being first and the lowest 
well-being (highest need) being 51st. (For suicide rate, the ranks are out of 50 rather than 51 because of the 
lack of data from Washington, D.C.) 
In addition to the state and jurisdiction data pages, we include tables for each of the 11 measures of needs 
by state. This alternative method for presenting the same data contained in the state data pages may be better 
for those wishing to make comparisons across states.
Limitations
The data and analyses used to create each state’s data sheet have several limitations that should be kept in 
mind when interpreting them. First, the domains of veterans’ needs that we have included are limited to 
those for which there are data available from the small number of data sources that can support state-level 
analyses. Most surveys, even most large federal surveys—such as the National Survey on Drug Use and 
Health or the National Health Interview Survey—do not have nearly enough veteran respondents within

many state programs. This is particularly true for data regarding mental health diagnoses and symptom 
severity and health service utilization, domains in which our state data sheets have little information. Sec-

are some measures for some states that are less precise than would be desirable because of small sample sizes. 
We have attempted to mitigate this by combining results over the most recent three-year period for measures 
from the BRFSS. However, this aggregation trades better statistical precision because of larger respondent 
samples for reduced temporal precision. This temporal averaging may mask recent changes over time within 
states. Relatedly, even averaging over three-year periods, the samples are generally too small to support inves-

needs by gender or age, even though veteran needs likely vary substantially across those dimensions. Finally, 
our primary data sources (BRFSS and ACS) are surveys that were designed and weighted to be representative 
of each state’s total population. These weights help to mitigate biases that might otherwise occur because of 
systematic survey nonresponse. However, these survey weights may not eliminate all nonresponse bias. Even 
when these weights ensure that the weighted total sample is representative of the total population, they may 
not fully ensure that the veteran subsample of respondents is representative of the veteran population in each 
state. It is possible that a lack of full representativeness of the veteran population may introduce biases into 
our estimate and cause our CIs to underestimate the uncertainty in those estimates. However, the magnitude 
and direction of any bias are not known.

A
substantial number of the programs designed to assist veterans are planned, funded, 
or implemented within states or similar geographic regions. At the same time, national 
programs operated by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) or by national 
veteran-serving nonprofits may distribute their resources at the state or regional 
Veterans Integrated Service Networks level. 
Challenges facing veterans can vary tremendously across states, but almost all published studies 
estimating the needs of veterans have looked exclusively at national samples of veterans and often 
only look at veterans who use VA services.
This report is designed to help fill this knowledge gap using several existing, population-representative 
data sources. The authors of this report present several measures of veteran service needs for each of 
the 50 states and the District of Columbia. These state-specific profiles show how veterans’ needs vary 
substantially across states and how these differences should be considered when funding, designing, 
or implementing services and benefits designed to improve the lives of veterans.
