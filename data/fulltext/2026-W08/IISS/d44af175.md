# Research PaperFrom Fragmentation to Integration: Prospects for Security Sector Reform in Post-Assad Syria17th October 202517th October 2025

---

In late November 2024, a coalition of Syrian armed opposition groups, led by Hay’at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS), embarked on a military offensive against Syrian government forces. Initially targeting Aleppo, the rebel fighters met only limited resistance from the Bashar al-Assad regime and were able to continue advancing at a rapid pace towards the Syrian capital, Damascus. By early December 2024, the rebel coalition had taken control of most of Syria, including the strategically important cities of Hama and Homs. By 8 December 2024, the rebel forces had overthrown the decades-old authoritarian regime of Assad. In January 2025, Ahmed al-Sharaa, the leader of HTS, held the ‘Syrian Revolution Victory Conference’ in Damascus, during which he declared himself president of the interim authorities. He officially announced plans to disband all institutions of the Assad regime, including the Syrian Arab Armed Forces, and to integrate all armed opposition factions into a new armed force that would be under the command of a reformed Ministry of Defense, now firmly under HTS control. To understand this ongoing integration process, it is instructive to examine the various opposition forces in greater detail.
This report first traces the initial months after the fall of the Assad regime, including the post-revolutionary honeymoon and the approach taken by the interim authorities regarding force integration. It then focuses on the escalation of violence on the Syrian coast and the sectarian fighting in the south, demonstrating some of the shortcomings of the transitional authorities’ current approach to security sector reform. From there, it outlines the role of regional and international actors that hold vastly different viewpoints towards the new government in Damascus, ranging from supportive to hostile. The report then maps out the main opposition groups that existed in Syria prior to the fall of Assad, outlining their backgrounds, ideologies, organisational structures and financing. This includes HTS, the Syrian Democratic Forces, the Syrian National Army and the National Liberation Front. It also maps out other non-state armed groups, including the Islamic State and the remnants of the Assad regime. Finally, the report concludes with some considerations for Syria’s nascent security sector.
