This study examines how Japan, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand are adopting cloud technologies for national-security and defence (NS&D) purposes. It situates these developments within a global context in which governments must manage and exploit rapidly expanding volumes of data and information to gain strategic advantage over competitors and adversaries. Cloud computing now underpins that effort, offering cost-effective, resilient and scalable infrastructure for storing and analysing data critical to military, intelligence and governance functions. Yet, given the NS&D purpose, each state must balance scale, innovation and value for money with sufficient sovereign assurance and control, finding its own path to using cloud technology.
The region’s strategic environment is shaped by intensifying strategic competition between the United States and China, increasingly complex threats, and a blurred line between war and peace. Cyber operations, disinformation campaigns and other malign activities by state and non-state actors create challenges for both security and decision-making. At the same time, digital connectivity is generating vast quantities of data across government, industry and critical infrastructure. To turn this data into insight and advantage, states need to store, process and analyse it securely and at scale. Traditional departmental IT systems can no longer meet this requirement, prompting a turn to commercial cloud solutions – often provided by the world’s largest technology firms. The most advanced of these providers, known as ‘hyperscalers’, deliver the scale, resilience and analytic capability required to manage the vast volumes of data integral to NS&D. These allow governments to draw on decades of private-sector investment while retaining oversight through tailored technical and governance arrangements.
A small number of US firms dominate the hyperscale cloud market. This means that, depending on the level of sovereign control deemed necessary, states might have a choice to make: they can opt for controlled use of public clouds; commission foreign hyperscalers and/or domestic suppliers to build bespoke cloud instantiations separate from the public cloud (closed clouds); or adopt a hybrid use of public and closed clouds.
Japan faces an increasingly complex threat landscape involving China, Russia and North Korea. Its NS&D modernisation strategy emphasises cross-domain integration and alliance inter-operability. The government is working with global and domestic commercial partners to build hybrid cloud environments that combine commercial innovation with sufficient sovereign control.
The Philippines, facing persistent maritime and internal security threats, is at an early stage of adoption. Its ´Digital Transformation Strategy´ and its Armed Forces modernisation programme aim to strengthen inter-service coordination, consolidate IT systems and build capacity for secure cloud management.
Singapore’s approach reflects its long-standing pursuit of technological superiority as a strategic equaliser. Through the Defence Science and Technology Agency (DSTA), it is developing a secure hybrid cloud solution for defence purposes that supports the integration of land, sea, air and digital/cyber operations. Cloud adoption is closely tied to its ´Smart Nation´ and ´Total Defence´ initiatives, linking military readiness with national resilience.
Finally, Thailand is pursuing gradual digital modernisation within a hybrid framework. Its development of a policing solution that uses Amazon Web Services (AWS) reflects a pragmatic approach to leveraging the public cloud where appropriate while building internal governance capacity and protections for sensitive data.
The study concludes that cloud technologies have become increasingly central to NS&D. The four case studies show that maintaining sovereign control and using non-national cloud providers are not necessarily conflicting goals. States are developing technically sovereign solutions that provide control over data, access and security while benefiting from the resilience and scalability of commercial infrastructure. The experience across the region demonstrates that sovereignty in the cloud era is achieved not through isolation but through the ability to govern, secure and leverage digital infrastructure effectively to maintain a decision advantage in an increasingly contested information environment.
