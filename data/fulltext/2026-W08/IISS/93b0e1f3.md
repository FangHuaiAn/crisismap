# Research PaperThe EU’s Burgeoning Defence Role and its Impact on Third-country Market Access11th December 202511th December 2025

---

NATO remains Europe’s primary security provider, and its regional plans and capability targets have set strenuous objectives for its European members. However, its role in the continent’s defence market is limited. The European Union, by contrast, has become an increasingly significant actor, particularly since it set up instruments offering financial support to EU industry and member states for capability development and procurement programmes. Having had no dedicated defence budget before 2014, the EU allocated EUR591 million in the 2014–20 Multi-Annual Financial Framework (MFF) rising to EUR11.37 billion for 2021–27. In 2025 the European Commission proposed a EUR131bn budget for defence and space for the 2028–34 MFF.
Having initially focused on supporting defence research and development, the Commission recently expanded its purview to encompass procurement and industrial capacity with a series of instruments, the most significant of which is the EUR150bn Security Action for Europe (SAFE), adopted by EU member states in May 2025. The growing budget, broader mandate and proliferation of instruments will give the EU greater influence over the evolution of the defence market in Europe.
At the same time, because all EU defence instruments impose stringent conditions on participation by non-EU states, EU states’ defence markets are expected to become less accessible to third countries.
However, the EU’s leverage on the defence market is limited by the sharp rise in member states’ defence spending. European defence outlays in 2025 – including EU and NATO states – are 55% higher than in 2022, following double-digit annual growth in 2024 and 2025, compared with near-stagnation in 2009–21. With Europe’s NATO members spending 2.1% of GDP on defence in 2025 and aiming for 3.5% by 2035, national budgets will increasingly outweigh even the growing EU funds, giving governments more scope to act outside EU frameworks.
Third countries will still be able to operate in the parts of the EU defence market beyond the control of the European Commission, but more closely aligned partners may also hope to gain preferential access to the expanding, Commission-regulated segment. Any such arrangements are likely to be transactional, however, with the Commission weighing a country’s potential contribution to the EU’s defence-industrial objectives against the political or industrial obstacles it presents.
