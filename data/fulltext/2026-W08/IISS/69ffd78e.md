The Security Action for Europe (SAFE) through the Reinforcement of the European Defence Industry Instrument, or ‘SAFE instrument’, is a mechanism that provides loans to European Union member states for investment in defence capabilities and to strengthen their defence industries. It was launched in March 2025 as part of the ReArm Europe Plan/Readiness 2030 and adopted by member states in May 2025. With EUR150 billion in available funds over the five-year period of 2025–30, it is set to have a significant impact on the European defence market, with an annual disbursement likely to equal at least 25% of the current annual total of EU members’ defence procurement, which is expected to just exceed EUR100bn in 2025 according to the European Defence Agency.
The opportunity to access long-maturity EU loans at low interest rates, with potential pre-financing of up to 15% of the total value of each loan, offers valuable support for loan applicants, illustrated by the fact that the total of EU member states’ preliminary requests for SAFE loans, in July 2025, exceeded the EUR150bn budget.
SAFE is intended to support the European defence industry through common-procurement projects. A loan may be made available if at least two member states are together acquiring defence equipment that falls under either of two capability categories mentioned in the SAFE regulation. The requirement for projects receiving SAFE support to be completed by 2030 raises questions about the feasibility of implementing the timeline. A further criterion is that the equipment has to be mostly produced in the EU, with components produced outside the EU constituting no more than 35% of the total cost of the end product. For products under category two of the regulation, design authority also has to reside in or be transferred to the EU. These rules can nevertheless be interpreted as indicating a degree of receptiveness from the EU towards third-country participation in defence projects, at least in comparison with previous EU regulations regarding Europe’s defence-industrial sector.
However, IISS research indicates that these restrictions may limit the participation of third countries to a greater extent than is currently understood. In this report, detailed analysis of the SAFE instrument assesses the feasibility of third-country inclusion given the 35% rule and the design-authority stipulation, as well as the potential unintended effects of other SAFE requirements. The report finds, for example, that the full transfer of design authority over some types of equipment is likely to be a major impediment from the perspective of non-EU partners. If so, SAFE will limit EU countries’ access to capabilities that contain components produced by close allies and partners, undermine long-standing development partnerships, reduce interoperability, and slow the introduction of innovative solutions into EU-funded projects.
In a first for an EU defence instrument, SAFE allows countries that are in the process of acceding to the EU, candidates for accession or potential candidates, and countries that have a Security and Defence Partnership with the EU, to apply to participate on enhanced terms. Canada, South Korea, Turkiye and the United Kingdom applied for enhanced conditions. However, unattractive terms and a limited time frame for negotiations have proven to be insurmountable obstacles for the UK, while an agreement with Canada was only finally announced on 1 December 2025. How much value Canadian industry will realistically gain from such a late agreement remains to be seen, given that member states were required to submit their procurement plans to the Commission by 30 November, giving them little chance to take account of the Canadian deal. As for South Korea and Turkiye, they were not invited to conduct negotiations, which suggests the European Council did not give equal consideration to all potential partners. Furthermore, the negotiation processes with Canada and the UK showed how politically complex it can be to reach enhanced agreements on defence-industrial cooperation under the EU framework. From the perspective of the third countries that entered formal negotiations, the complexity of finding a compromise was further exacerbated by the requirement to pay significant fees to secure enhanced participation terms.
