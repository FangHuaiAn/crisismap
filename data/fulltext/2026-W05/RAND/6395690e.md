Summary 
A substantial body of research has established that there is a clear causal relationship 
between school spending levels and student outcomes. It follows that how West Virginia 
distributes its funding to schools and students matters as well. 
To promote improved learning in West Virginia’s PK-12 schools, the West Virginia House 
of Delegates sponsored RAND to conduct an independent review of the design and adequacy of 
West Virginia’s state school aid funding formula. Informed by prior research, benchmarks from 
other states, and analyses of spending data in West Virginia and nationwide, we offer 
recommendations for how the formula and funding-related policies could be enhanced. 
Key Findings and Recommendations 
• Based on an assessment of the adequacy of current spending levels relative to student 
needs, we recommend that West Virginia continue to increase overall funding levels for 
public education, while simultaneously working to improve system effectiveness. 
• Compared to other states, West Virginia under-allocates resources to districts that serve 
students who cost more to educate. We recommend distributing more funding to districts 
with larger proportions of low-income, special education, and English learner students. 
Implementing weights similar to the median weights used by other states would boost 
funding in 28 districts and cost the state around $37M to hold other districts harmless.  
• In the context of anticipated reductions in force, we recommend phasing out funding 
allocations that reward retention of higher-cost staff. Simultaneously, districts should be 
empowered to make personnel decisions based more on performance than seniority. 
• To remove unhelpful incentives, we recommend basing transportation funding on key 
cost drivers—such as ridership, mileage, transit time, and fleet condition—instead of 
individual districts’ prior-year transportation spending. 
• Directing Hope Scholarship funds toward families who would enroll in private schools 
regardless of the scholarship is inefficient. To reduce this, we recommend canceling the 
planned program eligibility expansion and considering income-based eligibility criteria.

Chapter 1. Introduction 
The scope of our work is informed by conversations with and questions from members of the 
House of Delegates and other state educational leaders who expressed interest in updating the 
formula to help improve student outcomes. Based on their input and on our holistic review of the 
state’s funding strategy, the report focuses on four questions: 
1. What is the adequacy of West Virginia’s per-pupil funding levels in PK-12 education, 
relative to the needs of its students and in comparison to spending levels in other states? 
2. To what extent is the distribution of funds to districts commensurate with the 
educational challenges and costs that districts face? 
3. What are the implications of the design of the current funding formula for districts’ 
incentives around effective use of resources? 
4. What are the implications of the Hope scholarship program and a planned expansion in 
program eligibility for the efficiency of state education spending in West Virginia? 
 
In Chapters 2 through 5 of this report, we summarize our analyses and recommendations 
related to each of these questions. A complete summary of all report recommendations is 
provided in Chapter 6.  
Throughout, we reference more detailed information provided in three supplemental 
appendixes. Appendix A includes additional information from our review of research literature 
and of other state funding formulas. In Appendix B, we provide details about the methods and 
supplemental findings from our analysis of current spending distributions in West Virginia. 
Finally, in Appendix C, we include scenario analyses that illustrate the expected impacts across 
West Virginia’s school districts of the types of distributional changes to the funding formula that 
we recommend. These appendixes can be found in the technical annex available at 
www.rand.org/t/RRA4161-1. 
Our recommendations are informed by desk research and quantitative analyses, including 
review of relevant research literature in several topic areas, a review of state funding formulas 
nationwide, and statistical analyses of school spending distributions nationwide and in West 
Virginia in particular. Our work was facilitated by detailed records provided by the West 
Virginia Department of Education (WVDE) about state school aid funding formula allocations 
and calculations, and conversations with staff at WVDE familiar with the funding formula. We 
also received data and helpful context from state treasury officials associated with the Hope 
Scholarship program.  
One limitation of our work is our limited direct engagement with stakeholders most familiar 
with the historical context around school funding in West Virginia. This poses some challenge 
for our interpretation of the original intent of funding statutes that determine current allocations.

A Brief Orientation to West Virginia’s State School Aid Formula 
West Virginia determines the amount of state aid allocated to districts to fund public schools through a multi-step 
process known as the Public School Support program (also referred to as the state school aid formula) (WVDE, 
2024). The formula estimates a “basic foundation allowance” for each district. This is a baseline funding amount 
that is the sum of estimated costs of various inputs to schooling including personnel salaries, employee benefits, 
transportation costs, general operating costs, and other specific allowances described in statute as intended to 
support areas ranging from instructional program improvement, to technology funding, to educator induction, and 
more.  Most input costs are pegged directly or indirectly to the number of students enrolled. 
While funding amounts are justified based on the estimated costs of each input, almost all funds are provided as a 
single block grant and districts are not required to allocate resources linked to the estimated costs of each input. 
The formula also includes an adjustment in funding for districts that are smaller than 1,400 students to account for 
worse economies of scale, and adjusts funding levels for all districts based on enrollment density (i.e. number of 
students per square mile).  
 
Districts’ foundation allowances are compared to an estimation of the “local share” that each county is assumed to 
be able to contribute based on its local property tax base. The difference between the foundation allowance and 
the local share for each district determines the actual amount of state aid allocated by the formula. 
 
In practice, counties can and do provide more local funding than their local share calculation assumes, and the 
formula does not penalize counties from funding schools at higher levels. 
 
Subsequent to the calculation of allocated state aid, additional allowances are provided to districts, sometimes 
automatically and sometimes as a grant for which districts must apply. These include allowances linked to 
enrollment increases, for the education of children with “high acuity” special needs, for alternative education 
programs, for Limited English Proficiency programs, and more. 
 
In a separate appropriation, the state allocates funding to the Hope scholarship program board, which oversees 
the provision of scholarships to eligible families with children not enrolled in public schools. Scholarships are 
typically spent on private schooling, educational services, or supplies.

Chapter 2. Overall Adequacy of West Virginia’s School Funding 
Levels 
Overview: West Virginia’s student population has significantly higher poverty rates, a larger 
proportion of students in special education, and lower academic performance than the national 
averages, while total per-pupil spending has been close to the national average. In light of 
research evidence about the likely benefits of increased funding, we recommend that the state 
continue to increase its level of funding for PK-12 education. Simultaneously, improvement 
trends of some other states similar to West Virginia indicate that West Virginia has the potential 
to improve the overall effectiveness of its educational system. 
Current spending levels relative to student needs 
To gauge the adequacy of school spending levels in West Virginia, we consider its 
investments in education relative to the rest of the nation, as well as key characteristics of its 
students that relate to the educational disadvantages they face. While each state’s context and 
challenges are unique, these indicators provide a reference point for the degree of educational 
need in the state.  
Spending levels on education in West Virginia are close to the national average, when 
accounting for regional variation in wages. As shown in Figure 1, in 2022-23, the most recent 
year in which national comparative data are available, real wage-adjusted per pupil spending on 
education in West Virginia (counting state, local, and federal resources combined) was three 
percent higher than the national average. As shown in Figure 2, Federal resources have been 
elevated in recent years due to COVID-19 relief, accounting for 19% of total spending in West 
Virginia in 2023 relative to 13% nationwide, but these supplemental resources will soon lapse. 
During this same period, state spending declined in real terms. Moreover, the state’s higher-than-
average reliance on federal resources presents an added funding risk given recent uncertainty 
around federal distributions of resources to support schools (Stadler and Abbott, 2025).  
In 2023-24, real state and local educational spending per pupil increased by nearly 14 percent 
over 2022-23 levels in West Virginia, driven in part by growth in educator pay and funds to 
support additional service personnel (West Virginia Legislature, 2023). This increase helped to 
reverse declines in the real (inflation-adjusted) value of per-pupil spending that had occurred 
over the prior few years in the context of inflationary pressures. However, we lack a reference 
point for how this compares to national spending during this period.

educate large proportions of students in key groups that have been shown in previous research to 
be associated with higher costs and academic disadvantage (Jimenez-Castellanos and Topper, 
2012; Duncombe and Yinger, 2004; Imazeki, 2008). As shown in Table 1, West Virginia ranks 
5th nationally across U.S. states in terms of the proportion of its students classified as eligible for 
special education services under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), and 
ranks 47th nationally in the proportion of students who have a parent with a college degree 
(NCES, 2024; Ruggles et al, 2025). West Virginia ranks 8th out of 50 states in terms of the 
proportion of families with children ages 5-17 living in poverty (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024).1

costs of delivering education in many counties in the state. However, the state does have the

Anticipated benefits of additional investment in education 
Although there has long been debate about whether money matters for schools, there is now 
consensus based on rigorous research that it does. A large body of high-quality research 
examining the effects of school funding levels show a clear relationship between sustained (i.e., 
for four years or more) changes in funding levels and student outcomes over both the short- and 
long-term (Jackson and Mackevicius, 2024; Baker and Knight, 2025). On average across 
rigorous causal studies from diverse contexts and policy reforms, a sustained $1,000 increase in 
per-pupil spending yields sustained increases in test scores by 0.03 standard deviations (i.e. 
raising the median student from the 50th percentile nationally to the 51st), high-school graduation 
by around 2 percentage points, and college-going by 2.8 percentage points (Jackson and 
Mackevicius, 2024). Moreover, this meta-analysis indicates that sustained spending increases 
can be expected to improve test scores in over 92 percent of studied cases and educational 
attainment outcomes in over 97 percent of cases, with improved probabilities of impact when 
funds are allocated to higher-poverty settings.2 The documented effects of funding increases 
appear to flow through a wide range of potential uses of funding, including wages (which can 
indirectly affect teacher quality and retention), staffing ratios, capital investments, and a variety 
of other instructional supports. However, investments in capital (e.g., school infrastructure) tend 
to take longer to yield positive impacts. Effects of funding increases are smaller when resources 
are provided to more economically advantaged student populations.  
Overall, this prior research shows that sustained increases in education spending are a 
reliable and beneficial investment across a range of contexts. On the flip side, decreases in 
funding levels (regardless of the cause) can be reliably expected to harm student achievement 
and attainment.  
Recommendations around adequacy of overall spending levels 
In light of the factors we have reviewed, including the research evidence on spending 
impacts, the high degree of disadvantage facing West Virginia’s student population, expected 
declines in federal COVID-19 era supports, and the current low level of academic performance 
state-wide, we recommend that the state continue to increase its investments via the state school 
aid formula to more adequately address student needs. Increasing total education spending could 
also be used to facilitate the types of distributional changes in formula allocations which we 
recommend in Chapter 2. For example, it could allow the state to hold all districts “harmless” in 
the face of those changes.  
We recognize that the state’s current overall funding levels represent an above average 
degree of “effort” relative to its tax base. For example, West Virginia’s state and local spending

Mississippi, Louisiana and Tennessee have all seen relative improvements in academic 
outcomes over the past decade, spurred by a range of evidence-based state-wide reform efforts 
(Hovanetz, 2025). Each of these states has also been active in updating school funding formulas 
to make progress over time around fairness in funding distributions (Education Commission of 
the States, 2024, Tennessee Department of Education, 2025, BESE Admin, 2025). 
Once ranking near the bottom nationally in reading outcomes, Mississippi’s large-scale 
literacy instructional reforms linked to the science of reading have helped to improve teaching at 
scale and improve outcomes (Spencer, 2024). These reforms were complemented by an 
enhanced system of school accountability and a realignment of state exams to match national 
standards.  
In Tennessee, improvements appear to have stemmed from similar large-scale development 
of teachers’ instructional practices, higher academic standards, and aligned curriculum and 
assessments. Enhanced accountability systems for both schools and teachers were coupled with 
structured local autonomy to allow districts to adapt policies within a coherent state framework 
(Matheny, 2023). 
In Louisiana, reforms to adopt higher academic standards, to enhance curriculum, 
assessment, and standards-alignment, and to strengthen teacher preparation and development, 
appear to have increased the quality and alignment of teachers’ instruction (Kaufman et al., 
2018). A range of accountability reforms implemented by the state might have also contributed 
to gains, with overall (though not uniformly) positive effects related to closing and replacing 
struggling schools (Churchill, 2024). However a rigorous study of other aspects of the state’s 
school-level accountability reforms found no significant effects, underscoring the uncertainty 
inherent in any system-wide reform effort (Dee and Ross, 2019).   
Collectively, these examples provide a sense of the scale of evidence-based reforms that may 
be required to enhance teaching and learning statewide—not just updated school funding 
formulas.3   
An examination of the full range of potential school system improvement strategies that may 
be most beneficial for West Virginia is out of scope for this report. Even so, we highlight these 
three states to point to the potential value of combining increased spending with evidence-based 
reforms to improve students’ academic performance overall.  
In the remainder of this report, we return to our focus on updating the state school aid 
funding formula to help improve the efficiency and fairness of the state’s educational 
investments.

Chapter 3. Distribution of Funds to Districts 
Overview: Compared to other states, West Virginia under-allocates funding to districts that 
serve greater proportions of students who have greater needs and who require additional 
resources to educate. We recommend modifying West Virginia’s state school aid funding formula 
to redistribute more resources to districts that serve more students who are from low-income 
families, who are special education students, or who are English learners. The state should 
implement funding weights for these groups that are within the range of additional costs 
identified in prior research, and within the norms set by other states’ formula weights. 
The case for redistribution according to costs of educating to proficiency 
Extensive research has demonstrated how student demographic characteristics, in particular 
family income levels, special education status, and English learner status, are associated with 
increased costs to schools of providing an adequate education (Baker, Weber, and Srikanth, 
2021; Duncombe, William, and Yinger, 2005; Chambers, et al, 2002). High concentrations of 
low-poverty students have also been shown to amplify barriers to student success, due to factors 
including larger gaps in resources, challenges in recruiting effective teachers, and negative peer 
effects (Sirin, 2005; Chetty, Hendren, and Katz, 2016; Reardon and Owens, 2014; and Clotfelter, 
Ladd, and Vigdor, 2006). In terms of specific inputs with cost implications, students from lower-
income families often require additional educational support such as small group instruction, 
extended learning time, or targeted interventions. Special education students might require these 
as well as specialized personnel, more customized behavioral or academic interventions, 
specialized transportation, facility modifications, or additional therapies. English learners may 
require specially trained staff, push-in or pull-out services, and supplemental curriculum and 
instructional materials, among other supports.  
Research-based estimates of the additional costs required to educate students with greater 
needs come from a range of methodologies, including professional judgement panels that assess 
costs of necessary inputs, descriptive analysis of observed spending levels for different student 
populations, and empirical cost-function analyses that estimate the costs of raising students’ 
achievement to an adequate level based on current district performance. We reviewed a range of 
such studies that include specific recommended amounts of additional funding adequate for 
serving these student populations, and detail findings from this literature in Appendix Table A1.   
All cost analyses we reviewed identified incremental costs of adequately educating low 
income, English learner and special education students that exceed the supplemental resources 
currently provided by the West Virginia state school aid formula for these student groups. That 
said, research-based recommendations for the amount of incremental funding required for each

type of student vary as a function of the methods used to estimate funding needs, state contexts 
in which the analyses were done, and choice of acceptable outcome goals. Our review of this 
literature found that recommended weights for students (i.e., the amount of additional funds 
needed to adequately fund their education) from low-income families ranged between 0.3 and 3

The varied estimates from this literature unfortunately do not offer clear guidance as to the 
specific formula weights that would be most appropriate for addressing education cost disparities 
in West Virginia. Deriving precise recommendations around state-specific weights could involve 
either (a) additional analysis based on professional assessments of the costs of key supplemental 
inputs needed to support students in each category, or (b) a statistical cost-function analyses of 
current spending patterns, outcomes, and outcome goals specific to the state.  
In what follows, we first summarize the way in which West Virginia currently distributes its 
resources to students in each of the three student groups. Then, to provide a reference point for 
the typical use of formula weights for these groups, we analyze the prevalence and extent of 
weight adjustments used in state formulas nationwide.  
West Virginia’s current funding distribution patterns 
Currently, West Virginia’s state school aid funding formula includes little consideration of 
the cost implications for districts serving higher-cost student categories:  
• The formula does not provide any additional resources related to low-income student 
status.  
• Additional resources for special education students are limited to a supplemental grant 
program that districts must apply for that is aimed at students with the most “high acuity” 
needs.4  
• Additional grant funding to serve the state’s approximately 2,000 English learner students 
is very limited, amounting to just $96,000 statewide annually in recent years.

Recommendations and scenario analyses 
Based on our findings on current funding distributions in West Virginia and prior research on 
district costs of serving higher proportions of these student groups, we recommend adjusting the 
state funding formula’s calculation of each district’s “total basic program allowance” to account 
for the proportion of students certified as low-income and those classified as English learners in 
a given school year. We also recommend adjusting for the proportion of students with an IEP, 
either in their entirety or at least for those with moderate disabilities and associated cost 
implications. We recommend increased per-pupil funding weights that are in the range of those 
recommended by research about the cost implications of serving these students subgroups, and at

14 
least equivalent to norms set by other states’ funding formulas.5 The state should also collect data 
on districts’ distribution of funds to schools to help ensure that funding is distributed 
commensurate with the student populations at each school. 
As the heterogeneity in formula weights for these groups across states suggests, arriving at 
specific weights is a nuanced task which demands consideration of the implications for 
communities impacted by distributional changes, and additional data on the cost implications of 
various categories of special needs students served in West Virginia’s schools. We do not yet 
have access to complete data sufficient to analyze educational costs and outcome functions 
specific to West Virginia’s special education students, and have not yet engaged with 
stakeholders to recommend a specific set of weights. Instead, we provide here and in Appendix 
C a few scenarios intended to help policymakers assess the general implications of an adjustment 
to current school aid formula distributions in line with our recommendation. If the legislature 
opts to proceed with a change in formula distributions of this type, additional analyses may be 
warranted to identify weights tailored to West Virginia’s context.

Considerations around special education classification 
Research has shown that changes to special education funding incentives can influence 
classification rates, particularly for students on the margins of special education classification, 
for mild disability classifications, and in the context of large financial incentives that do not 
differentiate between classification types (Dhuey and Lipscomb, 2011; Kwak, 2010; Cullen and 
Berry, 2003; Battisti, Friesen, and Hickey, 2012). At the same time, changes in funding formula 
incentives do not consistently influence classification rates, and the extent to which any changes 
represent misclassification versus differences in effort around accurate classification of students 
is not always clear (Powers and Uhing, 2023; Parrish et al., 2003; Dhuey and Lipscomb, 2011). 
For example, if IEPs introduce costs to schools associated with specialized instruction and 
sufficient compensatory funding is not currently provided, schools could have a disincentive to 
accurately identify student needs. Given that correctly targeted special education services are 
generally shown to improve student outcomes, selecting an appropriate level of funding tied to 
accurate student classification is important (Hanushek, Rivkin, and Kane, 2002; Schwartz, 
Hopkins, and Steifel, 2021).  
Partly in response to concerns about misclassification, some states (7) do not differentiate 
amounts of special education funding linked to district-specific special education enrollment 
(e.g., LAO, 2023). Many states (21), however, leverage severity-based weights, which can help

17 
to reduce marginal incentives around classification into mild special education categories (e.g., 
Connecticut School Finance Project, 2024, Owens, 2024). Funding tier groupings could also take 
into consideration which special education categories are more and less prone to potential 
misclassification.  
Tightening accountability around special education classifications might also be beneficial, 
including clarifying eligibility criteria and standardizing processes for classification to include 
tiered “Response to Intervention” screening, improving training of educators around 
classification processes, and monitoring classification rates and auditing in high-incidence 
categories (U.S. Department of Education, 2011; Horowitz, Raw, and Whittaker, 2017; U.S. 
Government Accountability Office, 2019; Westmoreland, 2025). These monitoring and 
accountability approaches could leverage data already being collected for the purpose of 
determining if there exist any “disproportionate representation” among racial or ethnic groups in 
special education based on “inappropriate identification” (Echevarria, Powers, and Elliott, 2004; 
U.S. Department of Education, 2007; U.S. Department of Education, 2008). States are required 
to describe their approaches to determining whether disproportionate representation stems from 
inappropriate identification in their annual IDEA state performance plans, and these plans could 
provide a reference point for additional monitoring steps to consider (U.S. Department of 
Education, undated).8

State funding formulas nationwide weight funding using a wide range of student and 
community characteristics. While we have analyzed and made recommendations related to three 
metrics that have been most frequently shown in research to be related to costs for schools

Distribution of resources based on district size and sparsity 
Small school districts and districts serving sparsely populated communities can face 
substantially higher educational costs per student due to worse economies of scale, difficulties 
recruiting and retaining staff, and higher costs of basic services. For example, estimates from a 
recent cost-function analysis in Vermont suggest that rural sparsity of <36 people per square mile 
is associated with a 23 percent increase in costs, while average school enrollments of less than 
100 students are associated with a 26 percent increase in costs (Kolbe et al., 2021). As such, 
many state formulas adjust per pupil funding weights for very small and sparse districts.  
In 2008-09 West Virginia amended its funding formula to provide additional resources to 
districts with fewer than 1,400 students, with the sparsest districts in this category (Pocahontas 
and Pendleton) receiving funding as if their enrollment was around 1,400 students, regardless of 
actual district size (West Virginia Legislature, 2008). As shown in Table 7, as of the 2010-11

Chapter 4. School Aid Formula Design and Associated Incentives 
Overview: We are agnostic as to whether to make a wholesale shift to the design of the state 
school aid formula. However, we recommend two targeted updates to the formula to better align 
district incentives around resource use. First, we recommend phasing out the link between 
funding allocations and staff salary bands, which creates incentives to overlook staff costs in 
personnel decisions. Strategic personnel management is particularly important for West Virginia 
given anticipated workforce reductions and the opportunity to maximize the benefits of recent 
teacher pay increases. Second, we recommend removing references to individual districts’ prior-
year spending in transportation funding allocations. Instead, allocations should be based on key 
cost drivers—such as ridership, mileage, travel time, and fleet condition—that preserve 
incentives for districts to reduce costs where feasible. 
General funding formula design considerations 
A growing majority of states’ school funding formulas (35) are structured around the concept 
of providing a foundation level of funding per student, with overall funding levels not explicitly 
tied to the costs of inputs (ECS, 2024). In contrast, West Virginia’s funding model includes 
many discrete funding allocation steps, each associated with actual or estimated costs of specific 
inputs related to the delivery of educational services. However, although West Virginia’s state 
school aid formula refers to many inputs, the majority of the funding allocation steps are linked, 
directly or indirectly, to student enrollment levels. Moreover, almost all funds contribute towards 
a single block grant of funding, allowing districts to use funds flexibly within broad guidelines 
such as minimum staff-to-student ratios. As such, for the most part West Virginia’s approach is 
similar to a student-based foundation funding models in its ultimate effect.  
An advantage of retaining references to specific inputs is that it could help to monitor 
whether funding levels are adequate to provide key inputs. Potential downsides of the input-
based formula relate to the degree of complexity involved in calculating total funding levels, as 
well as the difficulty of keeping multiple different cost justifications up to date. For example, 
allocations for substitute teachers, for 21st century technology, or for instructional improvement 
activities are specific appropriations for which the amount provided does not clearly correspond 
to an identified benchmark cost or usage rate.  
Going further than the current formula and mandating levels of district expenditure in 
specific areas could enforce investments by districts, but would impose an oversight burden for 
both the state and districts and require more frequent formula updates. Given these tradeoffs, 
proscriptive earmarks are typically best suited to areas that are a high priority for the legislature

21 
and where there is evidence that districts would not otherwise make adequate investments on 
their own initiative.  
We are agnostic as to the value of changing the entire formula to eliminate references to 
inputs and instead use a student-based foundation. Following in the footsteps of other states in 
this way might reduce some complexity. It could also be helpful as a rebranding or reorientation 
towards a statewide focus on using school resources to improve student outcomes “through any 
means necessary” rather than via pre-determined inputs. However, shifting the conceptual basis 
for the formula would not lead to a major change to the level or distribution of resources 
provided to districts, since the bulk of current distributions are already tied to student enrollment 
numbers.  
While we are agnostic as to whether the entire school state aid formula should be overhauled, 
we identify in our review two key areas where the costs of inputs are estimated in a way that 
provides unhelpful incentives. In the remainder of this chapter, we dive deeper into these specific 
issues and provide recommendations for how the legislature could address them.    
Allocations linked to staff characteristics and associated salary costs 
West Virginia’s current funding formula allocates funding to districts based in part on the 
salary costs of current staff, with consideration of factors including educators’ years of 
experience and educational attainment. While this approach has the advantage of compensating 
districts for the type of staff they currently employ, it also reduces incentives for districts to 
consider cost efficiency tradeoffs when making personnel management decisions.   
An alternative approach would be to provide funds for staff based on a per pupil funding 
amount for districts without pegging funding amounts to the salary bands of current staff. 
Districts could still receive additional funds for factors outside of their control such as local labor 
market conditions that may make it more costly to recruit and retain qualified staff. They could 
also receive supplemental funds for any strategic initiatives the state is explicitly seeking to 
incentivize or subsidize. For example, a state incentive might be appropriate for hiring and 
retaining teachers with certifications in math or special education if districts would otherwise be 
reluctant to pay the higher salary costs associated with hiring these “high need” teacher groups.  
Personnel management incentives for districts are especially important for West Virginia’s 
school system in light of two related factors.  First, declining enrollments will likely lead to 
reductions in force statewide in the near-term. The National Center for Education Statistics has 
forecasted that West Virginia’s total enrollment in public schools will continue to decline by 
approximately 13% between Fall 2022 and Fall 2031 (NCES, 2022). Second, West Virginia’s 
recent investments in improving teacher wages are more likely to help improve teacher 
effectiveness if schools practice strategic personnel management. Higher wages can expand the 
pool of job applicants and teachers’ willingness to stay in teaching, but school personnel 
management decisions will determine if that translates into retaining the most effective teachers.

Research on best practices in managing teacher reductions in force highlights the benefits to 
students of considering factors such as teacher skillsets and performance in those decisions, 
rather than traditional “last in first out” policies that prioritize retaining the most senior tenured 
staff above all other considerations (Kraft, 2015; Goldhaber et al., 2016; Boyd et al., 2011). 
Strategic staff management can also help to ensure that a balanced mix of effective teachers are 
retained rather than incentivizing the retention of only the most expensive veteran staff in a 
manner that would ultimately drive average salary costs higher statewide.9  
The current funding formula approach of compensating districts based on the salary bands of 
current staff makes some sense in the context of West Virginia’s limits on the amount of 
autonomy districts have in personnel management, with policies that heavily prioritize seniority 
in any reductions of force (West Virginia Legislature, §18A-4-7a, undated; Thomsen, 2014). If 
districts ultimately lack meaningful control over these staffing decisions, then continuing to 
compensate them based on legacy personnel costs might be fair. However, if districts were 
afforded more control over personal management decisions, then updating the funding 
allocations to be neutral with respect to staff characteristics would allow districts to realize cost-
efficiency gains should they opt to retain a more balanced mix of more and less veteran staff. 
Overall, we recommend that legislators should allow for more strategic personnel 
management, balancing considerations of educator seniority alongside other key factors when 
making reductions in force. Connected with this, we recommend updating the funding formula to 
avoid pegging funds allocated to individual districts based on the characteristics of the personnel 
that currently work at their schools, excepting any staff characteristics the state is explicitly 
seeking to subsidize or promote.  
Considerations around updating formula allowances for school staff 
The primary goal of our recommendation around modifying staff-related allocations is not to 
change the total amount of funds each district receives, but to change the incentives around the 
use of those funds. Unless the state is explicitly seeking to subsidize district hiring and retention 
of more veteran staff or staff with more advanced degrees statewide, eliminating links to current 
staff characteristics would improve personnel management incentives for all districts. However, 
there are multiple ways this could be accomplished. 
One option for eliminating current incentives around staff-related funding would be to 
eliminate the link to changes in staff characteristics moving forward and simply peg districts’ 
relative per-pupil funding levels for staffing to the most recent prior distributions. Future changes 
in statewide funding levels related to staffing could still be implemented as needed (e.g. 
adjustments for inflation or minimum salary increases). This would be sufficient to ensure that,

Currently, the state school aid formula incorporates numerous factors when allocating

smaller amount (i.e. ~6% of total staffing resources) of district-specific “salary supplement”

We estimate that if “basic” per-student formula funding allocations for professional

allowance). We discuss this scenario in more detail in Appendix C and show potential impacts 
on individual districts in Table C.4.

the original intent) may be warranted to assess whether an update to supplemental allocations is 
needed to better reflect current disparities in district needs. It may also be appropriate to adjust 
the size of the supplements if equalization of “basic” staff-related allocations were to alter 
districts’ need for the supplements.

Allocations linked to prior spending on transportation 
A second area where we believe the current funding formula may be providing unhelpful 
incentives to districts is with respect to transportation costs. Currently, allowances for 
transportation explicitly reference prior-year spending on transportation as a key benchmark 
when determining allocations to districts. While the formula includes some guardrails against 
excessive spending, this approach reduces incentives for districts to reduce their transportation 
costs over time, where feasible.   
It is important to ensure that districts receive adequate funding to address their transportation 
needs, but we believe this can be accomplished (as many states do) by pegging funding to 
underlying cost drivers, rather than spending levels in the prior school year (ECS, 2021; 
Zamarripa, Dammu, and O’Keefe, 2024). Factors such as ridership, mileage covered by routes, 
time spent in transit (in case of variation in terrain and navigability), additional costs for special 
needs rider accommodation, and upkeep costs associated with the age and condition of vehicles, 
could all be considered in an updated formula.  
We lack sufficient data of these types to recommend a specific alternative transportation 
formula at this time, but the aforementioned factors should allow for estimation of costs facing 
districts that are in line with their true levels of need.  Such a formula could be designed to align 
closely with recent prior spending levels, as these provide valuable information about the costs 
facing districts. But by removing explicit references to prior-year transportation spending levels, 
the new formula would incentivize districts to use the resources they are allocated to address 
their underlying transportation needs in the most cost-effective way possible.

Chapter 5. Implications of Hope Scholarship Program Design and 
Eligibility Expansion 
Overview: Currently, most Hope Scholarship participants must have been enrolled in the public 
school system. A planned eligibility expansion in 2026–27 would remove this requirement. 
Evidence from other universal voucher programs suggests that such an expansion would 
inefficiently subsidize private school tuition for many families who would have chosen private 
schools regardless. We therefore recommend against the expansion, and recommend considering 
adding income eligibility criteria to focus resources towards families less able to afford private 
schooling. We also recommend limiting the rollover of unused scholarship funds, as indefinite 
rollover delays the use of substantial state resources for educational improvement. Finally, we 
recommend that the state continue to improve data collection around the characteristics and 
experiences of families who use scholarships. 
Hope Scholarship program current design and efficiency considerations 
Through a supplemental appropriation, the state allocates resources to the Hope Scholarship 
program, which provides funding for educational expenses to participating students’ families. 
Scholarship amounts are equivalent to the statewide average state aid allotted per pupil (after 
accounting for local share estimates). As of school year 2023-24 this amounted to approximately 
$4,900 per student annually. Parents can also rollover unused funds from one year to the next, 
with more than $3M (~11%) of funds rolled over in this way in school year 2023-24.11 The 
program is intended to provide all taxpayers the opportunity to use state contributions toward 
their child’s education if they opt not to participate in the public school system, and to promote 
competition with public schools to encourage sector-wide school improvement. 
Currently, the program is only open to families with a rising Kindergarten-age child or a 
child who transferred out of regular attendance at a public elementary or secondary school (Hope 
Scholarship WV, 2025). As of the 2024-25 school year, the program served an estimated 10,542 
students (18% of them rising Kindergarteners), relative to approximately 241,000 students 
enrolled in public schools of all kinds state-wide.   
From an allocative efficiency standpoint (i.e., the use of state educational aid for the purpose 
of providing more or better educational services), scholarships to support students who would 
have otherwise attended public schools is a break-even spending proposition for the state, and 
could free up local resources that could be used to support students still in public schools. While 
there are other impacts such as contributing to declining overall enrollment in the public system 
 
11 According to records provided by the State Treasurer’s Office.

27 
and loss of associated federal resources, the overall arrangement is reasonably cost-efficient if it 
allows families to access alternative educational services they prefer. 
The educational efficiency of state-provided scholarships is lower when considering students 
who would have attended private schools regardless of the presence or absence of the 
scholarship. For these families, scholarships are expected to largely substitute for spending they 
would have undertaken regardless (Fernandez and Rogerson, 2003). In other words, state 
educational dollars in this case are providing a financial boost to families already using private 
schools, but not a meaningful change in the education their children receive. In cases where 
scholarships largely substitute for families’ existing spending, there is also reduced potential for 
the program to spur increased competition in the overall education sector. 
Families who were always going to home-school their children and who use scholarships 
would likely see a mix of substitution effects and increased total education expenditures. There is 
limited data on typical home-school educational expenditures, but the most recent estimates 
suggest parents spend around $1,300-$1,600 dollars annually (Duvall 2025) on educational 
services and supplies. Thus, for this group, only a portion of the scholarship is expected to 
substitute for spending that would have occurred regardless.  
Overall, the efficiency of the Hope program spending for promoting educational expenditures 
depends upon the mix of families who use the scholarships. In the short term, the program’s 
current eligibility restrictions related to prior public school enrollment are preventing enrollment 
by most families already attending private schools. However, even with these rules in place, over 
time an increasing proportion of families that would have enrolled in a private school regardless 
of the scholarships will become eligible to enroll. Reducing their participation rate over the long-
term would boost the cost efficiency of the program.  
Given the strong relationship between family income levels and private school participation, 
we recommend considering income eligibility thresholds for participation in the program. This 
could reduce participation rates by wealthier families who are more likely to use private schools 
absent the scholarship (Murnane et al., 2018).12 Many voucher programs nationwide include 
income-based eligibility thresholds in this way to help improve program targeting and efficiency 
(Erwin 2024). That said, we appreciate that there is a tradeoff between program goals related to 
universality of access and the cost efficiency of the program as an investment in the education 
sector. Regardless of whether new eligibility criteria are adopted, improved tracking of the 
income levels and educational experiences of families who use scholarships would be helpful to 
allow legislators to better understand who is benefitting from the program. 
Unlimited rollover of funds by families is another area where the efficiency of the existing 
program could potentially be improved. While the current flexibility is generous to participants, 
setting aside substantial amounts of state education funding year over year that is unused –

28 
potentially for several years in a row if families opt to accumulate funds – represents a missed 
opportunity to invest these funds towards West Virginia students’ education in a more timely 
way. As such, we recommend that unused funds should return to the state, or for caps to be 
placed on total rollover amounts allowed per participant.  
Efficiency implications of Hope eligibility expansion 
In school year 2026-27, the Hope program is expected to eliminate eligibility requirements 
requiring prior public school enrollment and attendance, becoming a universally available 
program. This will have important near-term implications for its cost efficiency. A sizeable pool 
of around 36,000 school-aged youth including a mix of current private school attendees (~38%) 
and home-schooled students (~62%) will be immediately eligible to participate with no 
significant barrier to entry (NCES, 2023; Johns Hopkins University School of Education, 
2024).13 Hope participants from this newly eligible population could represent a sizeable new 
burden on state educational finances, e.g. ~$105M annually if half of the newly eligible opt to 
take a scholarship. This would be a population that is much more likely to use the scholarship as 
a partial or full substitute for educational spending they would have undertaken regardless of the 
Hope program.  
In addition, universal access to scholarships may spur increases in private school tuition 
costs, as private-school attending families state-wide become eligible to receive scholarships at 
the same time. Prior studies of universal voucher programs have found that this approach can 
lead to private schools charging higher tuition, reducing access to private options and the 
financial benefits to families that use scholarships to attend private schools (Fontana and 
Jennings, 2024; Hungerman and Rinz, 2016). Price impacts may be less likely if program 
expansion is gradual, leaving more time for private school supply to match growing demand.   
Overall, the sudden expansion of the Hope program to become universally available without 
eligibility barriers may produce a substantial new cost burden to the state. The additional 
resources spent by expanding eligibility are likely to be less efficient—in terms of increasing the 
net educational services students receive—than the current version of the Hope program or the 
use of state resources directly towards the delivery of public educational services. Additionally, 
if private school tuition increases, many current scholarship recipients could also be negatively 
impacted. In light of these considerations, we recommend against the current planned expansion 
of eligibility for the Hope Scholarship program in school year 2026-27.

Chapter 6. Summary of Findings and Recommendations 
• West Virginia’s spending levels on education are close to national averages, but its 
students face well above average socio-economic and academic challenges. Research 
indicates a sustained increase in spending would very likely improve outcomes.  
- We recommend that the state increase funding through the school aid formula while 
simultaneously working to improve system effectiveness. 
• Compared to other states, West Virginia under-allocates resources to districts that serve 
students who cost more to educate. 
- We recommend distributing additional funding to districts with larger proportions of 
low-income, special education, and English learner students. Associated weights 
should be at least as large as the median weights used by other states. 
- The state should collect data on district’s distribution of funds to individual schools to 
help monitor alignment between resource levels and local needs. 
• West Virginia appropriately allocates additional resources to very small and sparse 
districts.  However, over time this allocation has grown unusually large for some districts. 
- We recommend updating the formula to cap the maximum proportional funding 
increase that districts can receive under this provision. 
• The current formula provides additional resources to districts that have staff in more 
expensive salary bands. This provides an unhelpful incentive to disregard staff costs 
when making personnel management decisions. 
- We recommend that the state update the formula to decouple per-pupil funding 
allocations from current staff characteristics. 
- We recommend this change be paired with reforms to allow reductions in force to be 
based primarily on staff performance rather than on educator tenure. 
• The current formula allocates transportation funding to districts tied to prior-year 
spending levels, reducing incentives for districts to cut costs. 
- We recommend transportation funding be tied instead to underlying cost drivers, such 
as ridership, mileage, time in transit, and condition of the fleet.   
• It is inefficient to direct Hope Scholarship funds toward families who would choose 
private schools even without the scholarship, or to permit indefinite rollover of funds. 
- We recommend against expanding Hope program eligibility, as this will open 
participation to a substantial number of students already enrolled in private schools 
and could lead to tuition cost increases by private schools statewide. 
- We recommend considering income-based eligibility to help steer scholarships 
towards students who are otherwise less likely to attend private schools. 
- We recommend the program limit rollover of unused scholarship funds to allow state 
resources to be used in a more timely way to support educational goals.
